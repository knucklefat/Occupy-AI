# Crypto in Retirement Accounts: ETFs, 401(k)s, and Self-Directed IRAs

**In one sentence:** You can now hold crypto in an IRA or 401(k) three ways (spot ETFs at any brokerage, a plan-sponsored option like Fidelity's Digital Assets Account, or a self-directed IRA holding actual coins), and the Roth version turns the biggest tax problem in crypto (constant taxable events) into no tax at all, provided you respect fees, custody, and the prohibited-transaction rules.

*Current as of September 11, 2026.*

## Why retirement accounts and crypto fit

Inside an IRA or 401(k) there are no taxable events. Swap ETH for SOL, harvest nothing, rebalance monthly, collect staking yield: none of it hits Form 8949. Traditional accounts defer tax until withdrawal (then taxed as ordinary income); **Roth** accounts, funded with after-tax dollars, make qualified withdrawals completely tax-free after age 59½ and five years. For an asset you believe could compound at high rates, Roth treatment is the most valuable tax structure that exists.

The trade-offs: contribution limits ($7,500 IRA plus $1,100 catch-up if 50+, and $24,500 401(k) elective deferral plus $8,000 catch-up if 50+, for 2026; indexed annually), a 10% penalty plus tax on early withdrawals, no ability to use losses against other income, and, for the self-directed route, fees and custody risk.

## The 2025 policy change

- **May 28, 2025:** the Department of Labor rescinded its 2022 Compliance Assistance Release that had told 401(k) fiduciaries to exercise "extreme care" before offering crypto. The DOL returned to a neutral stance: it neither endorses nor discourages crypto; fiduciaries apply ordinary ERISA prudence.
- **August 7, 2025:** Executive Order 14330 directed the DOL (within 180 days) to clarify fiduciary standards and propose rules, possibly including safe harbors, for "alternative assets" in 401(k)s, explicitly including "actively managed investment vehicles that are investing in digital assets," and told the SEC to ease retail access. A proposed DOL rule reportedly followed in early 2026; its final status was not confirmed in the sources reviewed for this page.
- **Effect:** plan sponsors that had shelved crypto options in 2022 are re-evaluating; brokerage windows in large plans increasingly allow spot crypto ETFs; litigation risk still makes most sponsors cautious about direct coin options.

## Route 1: Spot crypto ETFs in any IRA or 401(k) brokerage window

The simplest path. Since January 2024 (bitcoin), July 2024 (ether), and the SEC's September 2025 generic listing standards (Solana, XRP, Litecoin, Dogecoin, multi-asset baskets), spot ETFs trade like any stock. Hold IBIT, FBTC, ETHA, BSOL, or similar in a Roth IRA at Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard (Vanguard began allowing crypto ETF trades in late 2025 after years of refusing) and you get:

- Expense ratios of roughly **0.20–0.50%** per year, no trading fee beyond the spread.
- Regulated custody (Coinbase Custody, Fidelity Digital Assets, BitGo) with no keys to lose.
- Staking yield in some ETH and SOL products, paid inside the fund.
- Ordinary IRA beneficiary and estate handling.

Downsides: you own a fund share, not coins; no DeFi, no self-custody; small tracking drag; the ETF universe is limited to large caps.

## Route 2: Employer 401(k) crypto options

**Fidelity's Digital Assets Account** (launched 2022) lets participating plans offer bitcoin (and later ether and litecoin) as a core menu option, with employers able to cap allocations (Fidelity's default cap is 20% of contributions; many sponsors set lower). Fidelity charges an account fee in the range of roughly 0.75–0.90% per year plus a trading spread; confirm current figures in your plan documents. Adoption was slow after the 2022 DOL warning; the 2025 rescission and executive order have revived sponsor interest, and recordkeepers such as ForUsAll and Alight offer similar windows. Most participants' realistic path remains a self-directed brokerage window plus ETFs.

## Route 3: Self-directed IRAs holding real coins

A **self-directed IRA (SDIRA)** uses a custodian that permits alternative assets. Crypto-specialist platforms wrap the custodian, the exchange, and the storage:

| Provider | Model | Typical cost (verify current) |
|---|---|---|
| **iTrustCapital** | Trade ~100 coins plus metals inside the IRA; institutional cold storage via qualified custodian | 1% per trade, no monthly/annual fee, ~$1,000 minimum |
| **Alto CryptoIRA** | Trades executed through Coinbase; 200+ coins | ~1% per trade, no account fee |
| **Unchained IRA** | Bitcoin only; **collaborative multisig** where you hold two of three keys and Unchained holds one | Setup fee plus annual fee (several hundred dollars); low trading fees |
| **BitIRA, Bitcoin IRA, Swan IRA** | Various; some charge setup fees of $50–$300 and annual fees of 1–2% of assets | Higher-fee tier; read the schedule |

**The "checkbook IRA" or IRA LLC** variant has the IRA own an LLC that you manage, letting you hold keys and use DeFi. It is legal in principle but the IRS won *McNulty v. Commissioner* (2021), where an IRA owner who took personal possession of IRA-owned gold coins was treated as having taken a distribution. Holding the private keys to IRA crypto in your own wallet looks very similar. Use a qualified custodian or a multisig where you cannot unilaterally move funds.

**Roth conversion angle.** You can convert a traditional IRA to a Roth and pay ordinary income tax on the converted amount now. Doing this after a crypto drawdown means paying tax on a lower value and letting the recovery compound tax-free. Conversions cannot be undone, so size them to stay within your current bracket; many people convert in chunks over several years.

**Worked example.** Age 40, 24% bracket. You convert a $60,000 traditional IRA holding a bitcoin ETF at the bottom of a 40% drawdown, paying **$14,400** of tax from outside cash. If bitcoin recovers and compounds 10% a year for 25 years, the account is worth about $650,000, all withdrawable tax-free after 59½. Had you left it traditional and it grew the same, withdrawals would be taxed as ordinary income: at 24% that is roughly **$156,000** of tax, versus the $14,400 paid up front. (If it instead crashed to zero, you paid $14,400 for nothing, which is the risk of converting a volatile asset.)

## Fees and custody risk, quantified

**Example.** $50,000 in a self-directed IRA rebalanced quarterly with 1% per trade on the full balance each time = 8 trades × $500 = **$4,000 per year (8%)**. The same $50,000 in an ETF at 0.25% = **$125 per year**. Self-directed only makes sense if you rarely trade or need assets the ETFs do not cover.

Custody: with an SDIRA you rely on the platform's custodian and insurance; read who holds the keys, whether assets are segregated, and what happens in the platform's bankruptcy. The 2022 failures (Celsius, FTX) included IRA money on some platforms. Prefer qualified custodians (chartered trust companies) and multisig arrangements where feasible.

## Prohibited-transaction pitfalls

The IRA rules under Section 4975 are unforgiving: if a prohibited transaction occurs, **the entire IRA stops being an IRA as of January 1 of that year** and is deemed distributed (taxed, plus a 10% penalty if under 59½). Crypto-specific traps:

- **Personal use or control:** holding IRA coins in a wallet you control; staking IRA coins through your personal validator; using IRA assets as collateral for a personal loan.
- **Self-dealing:** selling your own coins to your IRA, or buying coins from your IRA; providing "services" (running a node for pay) to it; paying yourself from IRA staking income.
- **Family:** any of the above with a spouse, parent, child, or their spouses.
- **Collectibles:** an IRA cannot hold collectibles under Section 408(m); Notice 2023-27 says art-like NFTs may be collectibles, so buying such an NFT in an IRA is a deemed distribution of its cost.
- **UBTI:** if an IRA borrows (leveraged DeFi, margin) or runs an active business (commercial mining), income may be taxable inside the IRA as unrelated business taxable income and require Form 990-T.
- **Required minimum distributions** from traditional accounts starting at 73 (75 for those born 1960 or later) must be satisfied even if the assets are illiquid; Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs.

## How to actually do it

1. Decide the account type first: Roth if you expect high growth and can pay tax now; traditional if you need the deduction today.
2. If a spot ETF covers what you want (BTC, ETH, SOL, XRP, baskets), open or use a Roth IRA at a mainstream brokerage and buy it. This is the right answer for most people.
3. If your 401(k) has a brokerage window, check whether crypto ETFs are allowed; if not, ask HR whether the plan is reviewing options after the 2025 DOL changes.
4. Only go self-directed for assets or custody models the ETFs cannot provide; compare the full fee schedule, confirm a qualified custodian, and never take personal possession of keys.
5. Consider Roth conversions in drawdown years, in bracket-sized chunks.
6. Name beneficiaries and document how the account is accessed; an IRA custodian handles this far better than a seed phrase in a drawer.

## Key takeaways

- Inside an IRA or 401(k) there are no taxable events; a Roth makes the eventual withdrawal tax-free too.
- The DOL rescinded its 2022 anti-crypto 401(k) guidance in May 2025 and an August 2025 executive order pushed further; plan adoption is rising but still cautious.
- Spot crypto ETFs in a Roth IRA at a normal brokerage are the cheapest and safest route for most investors (0.2–0.5% per year).
- Self-directed IRAs (iTrustCapital, Alto, Unchained) allow real coins but cost about 1% per trade or fixed annual fees, and custody risk is on you to evaluate.
- Never hold IRA coins in a wallet you personally control; the *McNulty* case makes that a likely deemed distribution.
- Prohibited transactions and collectible NFTs can blow up the whole account, not just the bad trade.
- Roth conversions during drawdowns are the highest-leverage tax move available for volatile assets.
