# Nic Carter and the Bitcoin Analysts

**In one sentence:** Nic Carter, Pierre Rochard, and the Coin Metrics school built the analytical toolkit — on-chain data, proof of reserves, the "bearer asset" framing, and an evidence-based reply to the energy critique — that lets you evaluate Bitcoin with numbers instead of slogans, even though the analysts themselves disagree sharply about everything that isn't Bitcoin.

## Who they are

**Nic Carter** was Fidelity's first cryptoasset analyst (2016–2017), wrote a master's thesis on Bitcoin governance, co-founded the data firm **Coin Metrics** in 2017, and co-founded **Castle Island Ventures** in 2018, a venture fund investing in crypto financial infrastructure. He is a prolific essayist (CoinDesk, Harvard Business Review, Pirate Wires, his own site) and a frequent public combatant with both skeptics and Bitcoin maximalists.

**Pierre Rochard** co-founded the Satoshi Nakamoto Institute in 2013, which curates the cypherpunk canon; worked at Kraken; was VP of research at the Bitcoin miner Riot Platforms; and in April 2025 launched The Bitcoin Bond Company, which structures Bitcoin-backed securities for institutions with a stated goal of acquiring $1 trillion of Bitcoin for clients over 21 years. He is an unapologetic maximalist.

**The Coin Metrics school** refers to the analysts — Carter, Antoine Le Calvez, Lucas Nuzzi, Kevin Lu and others, plus adjacent researchers like Hasu, Murad Mahmudov, David Puell, and Willy Woo — who built the on-chain metrics now used everywhere: realized capitalization (valuing each coin at the price it last moved), MVRV (market value divided by realized value), NVT (network value to transaction volume), and free-float supply. Coin Metrics' *State of the Network* newsletter remains a standard reference.

## Core frameworks

**1. Bitcoin as a bearer asset.** A bearer asset is one where possession *is* ownership — like cash or a physical gold bar, unlike a bank deposit, which is a claim on an institution. Carter's argument (developed with Hasu in "How to Scale Bitcoin Without Changing a Thing," 2019) is that Bitcoin's real innovation is final settlement of a bearer asset over the internet, and that most users will still hold claims on intermediaries — "Bitcoin banks" — which makes *auditing those intermediaries* the critical problem.

**2. Proof of reserves.** The follow-on: exchanges and custodians should cryptographically prove they hold the coins they owe customers, with an auditor verifying liabilities. Carter has pushed this since 2018–2019 and maintains a tracker of who has and hasn't done it. FTX's collapse in November 2022 turned this from a niche demand into a mainstream expectation.

**3. Visions of Bitcoin.** Carter and Hasu's 2018 essay identified seven competing narratives — e-cash proof of concept, cheap payments network, censorship-resistant e-gold, private dark-web currency, reserve currency for crypto, programmable shared database, uncorrelated financial asset — and showed how dominance shifted over time. The lesson: "what is Bitcoin for?" has never had a single answer, and price cycles often coincide with narrative changes.

**4. The energy debate.** In 2021 essays for CoinDesk and Harvard Business Review, Carter argued that the right questions are *what* energy Bitcoin uses (often stranded, curtailed, or off-peak), *what it displaces*, and how it compares with other industries — rather than raw kilowatt-hour totals extrapolated forward. He conceded the consumption is real and large but argued it is a cost of a specific security property, and that miners act as a flexible buyer of last resort for energy.

**5. Rochard's "speculative attack."** In a 2014 essay, Rochard argued that as Bitcoin adoption grew, people would borrow weak fiat currencies to buy it, accelerating adoption reflexively. Michael Saylor's corporate strategy from 2020 onward is essentially this essay executed at scale — and the Bitcoin Bond Company aims to institutionalize it.

## Scorecard

**What they got right**
- **Proof of reserves (2018–2022):** FTX, Celsius, and others failed exactly because liabilities exceeded assets. Post-FTX, major exchanges published reserve attestations, though full liability audits remain rare.
- **Narrative rotation (2018):** the "uncorrelated asset" and "digital gold" narratives took over from "payments," as predicted.
- **Operation Choke Point 2.0 (February 2023):** Carter alleged regulators were pressuring banks to cut off crypto firms. Dismissed at the time as conspiracy, it was substantially vindicated when FDIC "pause letters" to banks were released in December 2024 and January 2025.
- **Realized cap and MVRV** became standard cycle tools; MVRV extremes flagged the 2021 and 2025 tops and the 2022 bottom reasonably well.
- **Energy mix:** the shift of mining toward flexible loads and grid-balancing (notably in Texas after 2021) developed as Carter described.

**What they got wrong**
- **Carter and the maximalist wars.** His June 2022 essay "Setting the Record Straight" — "I am not a Bitcoin maximalist" — followed a Castle Island investment in a non-Bitcoin wallet company; he lost credibility with the hardest-line Bitcoiners and with skeptics who noted his fund profits from the broader ecosystem he analyzes.
- **Stablecoin optimism.** Carter has been a vocal stablecoin advocate; critics note that dollar stablecoins undermine the "censorship-resistant money" thesis and that Tether's reserves remained unaudited for years even as he defended the category.
- **Energy claims about renewables** were sometimes stated more confidently than the data supported; independent estimates of the sustainable share of mining energy vary widely.
- **Rochard's maximalism** dismissed Ethereum and stablecoins as scams or irrelevant; stablecoins now settle trillions of dollars annually, and his bond company depends on exactly the kind of Wall Street intermediation maximalists once derided.
- **Carter's December 2024 argument** that a U.S. strategic Bitcoin reserve could call U.S. solvency into question drew heavy fire from Bitcoiners; the reserve was established by executive order in March 2025 without the feared consequences (so far).

## Criticisms

- **Conflict of interest.** Carter analyzes an industry his fund invests in; Rochard sells Bitcoin-backed bonds. Both disclose, but readers should discount accordingly.
- **Motivated reasoning on energy.** Skeptics argue that "stranded energy" and "flexible load" arguments are post-hoc rationalizations and that the counterfactual (that energy would otherwise be wasted) is rarely proven.
- **On-chain metrics are noisier than they look.** Exchange internal transfers, wrapped Bitcoin, and ETF custody all distort realized cap and address counts.
- **Tribalism.** The maximalist community's hostility toward Carter's pluralism illustrates how ideology polices analysis in this sector.

## Rules you can steal

1. **Ask "is this a bearer asset or a claim?"** Coins on an exchange are a claim; coins in your wallet are the asset.
2. **Demand proof of reserves *and* liabilities.** Assets without liabilities is half an audit.
3. **Identify the dominant narrative before you buy.** Narratives rotate; the price you pay is a price for a story.
4. **Use on-chain valuation tools as ranges, not triggers.** MVRV above 3–4 has historically been dangerous; below 1 has been opportunity — but each cycle stretches the bands.
5. **Argue about energy with counterfactuals.** The question is what the marginal kilowatt-hour would otherwise do, not the headline total.
6. **Treat maximalism as a red flag for analysis quality.** Any analyst who cannot say what would change their mind is selling, not analyzing.
7. **Disclosure is not absolution.** Knowing an analyst's book tells you where to look harder, not that you can stop looking.

## Key takeaways

- Carter's lasting contributions are proof of reserves, the bearer-asset framing, and "Visions of Bitcoin" — all still useful in 2026.
- Coin Metrics-style on-chain data (realized cap, MVRV, NVT) turned Bitcoin analysis from vibes into measurable cycles.
- The energy debate is best argued on energy mix and counterfactuals, where Carter's side has partial but not total support.
- Operation Choke Point 2.0 was a correct, unpopular call; the maximalist wars and stablecoin cheerleading are the weak spots.
- Rochard's "speculative attack" thesis predicted the leveraged-treasury era a decade early and is now his business model.
- All these analysts have books to talk; use their tools, not their conclusions.
