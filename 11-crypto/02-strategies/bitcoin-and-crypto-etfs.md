# Bitcoin and Crypto ETFs

**In one sentence:** Spot crypto ETFs let you own bitcoin, ether and (since late 2025) several other coins inside an ordinary brokerage or retirement account for 0.15–0.35% a year, trading counterparty and self-custody risk for convenience, tax reporting and access to IRAs — a good deal for most investors, and a bad deal only for people who specifically want the properties of holding their own keys.

## What they are and why they matter

A spot crypto ETF (technically most are grantor trusts, not '40 Act funds, but they trade like ETFs) holds the actual coins with a custodian — Coinbase Custody for most issuers, Fidelity Digital Assets for Fidelity's funds, Gemini for VanEck — and issues shares that track the price minus a fee. Unlike the futures-based funds that came earlier (BITO, launched 2021), there is no roll cost and no persistent tracking gap.

The U.S. SEC approved spot bitcoin ETFs on January 10, 2024, and spot ether ETFs in July 2024. In September 2025 it adopted "generic listing standards" that let exchanges list ETFs on any sufficiently large, liquid crypto asset without a bespoke approval — which is why Solana, XRP and other single-asset funds appeared within weeks. Vanguard, the last major holdout, opened its brokerage platform to these funds on December 3, 2025.

The practical consequence: the "how do I even buy this" barrier that kept most retirement money out of crypto is gone, and the mechanism for holding a 1–5% allocation in a normal portfolio is now the same one you use for gold or emerging-market stocks.

| ETHW / ETHV / EZET | Bitwise / VanEck / Franklin | 0.19–0.20% | <$0.2B each | Varies |

Ether is a proof-of-stake asset that pays roughly 3% a year to stakers (see the staking file). The first generation of ETH ETFs could not stake, so holders forfeited that yield; staking-enabled versions began appearing in late 2025 and 2026 after the SEC clarified that protocol staking is not a securities offering. If you want ETH exposure through an ETF, check whether the fund stakes and how much of the reward it passes through after fees — a 0.25% fee against a 3% yield is a fair deal; a 2.5% fee is not.

## Other spot crypto ETFs

**Solana:** Bitwise (BSOL, 0.20%), VanEck (VSOL, 0.30%), Grayscale (GSOL, 0.35%), Canary Marinade (SOLC, 0.50%) and REX-Osprey (SSK, 0.75%) — all stake their holdings, which is a bigger deal for SOL (6–7% nominal yield) than for ETH. Bitwise's launch in October 2025 was the strongest crypto ETF debut of that year.

**XRP:** Six spot funds launched between September and December 2025 — Bitwise (XRP, 0.34%), Franklin (XRPZ, 0.19%), Canary (XRPC, 0.50%), 21Shares (TOXR, 0.30%), Grayscale (GXRP, 0.35%), REX-Osprey (XRPR, 0.75%) — with roughly $1.5B combined AUM as of mid-2026.

**Litecoin, Dogecoin, Hedera, Avalanche and others** have filed or listed under the generic standards; treat these as niche and check current status and AUM before buying, since thin funds have wide spreads and can close.

**Index products:** The Bitwise 10 Crypto Index Fund (BITW) began trading on NYSE Arca on December 9, 2025 after years as an over-the-counter trust; it holds the ten largest coins by market cap (about 76% bitcoin, 15% ether, 4% XRP, 3% Solana as of September 2026), rebalances monthly, charges 0.75%, and has about $690M in assets. Grayscale's Digital Large Cap fund (GDLC) is the other multi-asset option. For most people, a cheap bitcoin ETF plus, optionally, an ether ETF gets you 90% of the index at a third of the fee.

## ETF versus holding your own coins

| | Spot ETF | Self-custody |
|---|---|---|
| Annual cost | 0.14–0.35% | ~0 after hardware wallet (~$80–150) |
| Custody risk | Custodian (Coinbase etc.) and issuer; SIPC does not cover the coins | You: lost seed phrase = lost coins, no recourse |
| Accessible in IRA / 401(k) | Yes | Only via specialized self-directed IRA custodians |
| Trading hours | U.S. market hours only; crypto moves 24/7 | 24/7 |
| Tax reporting | 1099-B from your broker | Your own records (1099-DA from exchanges from 2025 tax year) |
| Can you use it on-chain (DeFi, payments, staking yourself) | No | Yes |
| Censorship/seizure resistance | None — it is a security in a brokerage | High if done properly |
| Options, margin, easy rebalancing | Yes | Limited |
| Risk of "paper bitcoin" | ETFs hold real coins, audited; but you hold a claim, not keys | You hold keys |

For an allocation whose purpose is portfolio diversification, the ETF wins on almost every practical dimension. The case for self-custody is philosophical (the point of bitcoin is not needing a custodian), functional (you want to use the coins), or about extreme tail risk (a future in which brokerage claims are frozen or confiscated). Many long-term holders do both: ETF in retirement accounts, self-custody for a core position.

Bitcoin-in-a-brokerage has one underappreciated advantage: the 0.25% fee is cheaper than the 1–2% spreads and withdrawal fees that many retail exchange users pay over time without noticing.

## Where ETFs fit in IRAs and 401(k)s

- **IRAs (traditional or Roth):** any brokerage IRA at Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard and others can now hold spot crypto ETFs. This is the single best home for a crypto allocation: rebalancing has no tax consequences, and in a Roth the gains are never taxed. A Roth is the natural place for the highest-variance asset you own.
- **401(k)s:** depends entirely on your plan's menu. The Department of Labor withdrew its 2022 guidance discouraging crypto in plans in May 2025, and an August 2025 executive order pushed regulators toward allowing alternatives (including crypto) in defined-contribution plans; some plans and brokerage windows now offer crypto ETFs, most still do not. If yours has a self-directed brokerage window, it probably works.
- **Taxable accounts:** long-term capital gains treatment after one year, just like the coins themselves (bitcoin is property, not a collectible, so the 28% collectibles rate does not apply). ETF share sales create tax lots the broker tracks for you.

## How to actually do it

1. **Decide the allocation first** (see the small-allocation file), then decide which account — IRA/Roth if you have the room.
2. **Pick the fund on fee, size and spread.** For bitcoin: IBIT if you value liquidity and options; BTC (Grayscale Mini), MSBT, EZBC, BITB or HODL if you want the lowest fee. Avoid GBTC and ETHE unless you have a large embedded gain you don't want to realize.
3. **For ether**, prefer a staking-enabled fund with a low fee if one is available at your broker; otherwise ETHA/FETH.
4. **Use limit orders**, especially outside the busiest hours — crypto ETFs can gap at the open because the underlying traded all night.
5. **Skip altcoin ETFs unless you have a specific thesis**; the bitcoin-plus-ether combination captures most of the asset class, and small funds carry closure and spread risk.
6. **Set your rebalancing rule** and let the brokerage do the reporting.
7. **Check the fund's 10-K once a year** for custodian changes, fee changes or liquidation notices.

## Key takeaways

- U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs have existed since January 2024, ether since July 2024, and Solana, XRP and others since late 2025 under generic listing rules; the access problem is solved.
- Fees cluster at 0.15–0.25% for bitcoin; GBTC (1.50%) and ETHE (2.50%) are legacy products worth switching out of unless taxes prevent it.
- IBIT dominates (~$47B as of September 2026, down from a ~$100B peak with the bitcoin price); size matters for spreads and options liquidity.
- Ether and Solana ETFs increasingly stake; check that the yield is passed through, or you are giving up 3–7% a year.
- An ETF in a Roth IRA is arguably the best possible wrapper for a high-variance asset: tax-free gains and free rebalancing.
- Self-custody is cheaper and gives you real ownership, at the cost of irreversible personal responsibility — the two approaches suit different goals and many holders use both.
- Multi-coin index funds (BITW at 0.75%) are convenient but mostly bitcoin at three times the fee.
