# The Bear Case and the Bull Case

**In one sentence:** The strongest bull case and the strongest bear case for crypto are both partly right, they are mostly about different things, and an investor who can hold both at once will size the position better than anyone who has picked a side.

## Why both cases matter

Most crypto commentary is written by people who own it or people who despise it. Both groups have been embarrassingly wrong: bears called the top at $1,000 and again at $20,000; bulls predicted "hyperbitcoinization" and got a 77% drawdown, then predicted ETFs would end volatility and got a 50% drawdown. The useful exercise is to steelman each side, note the evidence, and let the uncertainty determine position size rather than conviction.

Jargon: a **monetary asset** is something held as a store of value rather than for cash flow (gold is the classic example). **Greater-fool** means an asset whose only return comes from selling to a later buyer at a higher price. **Layer 1** is a base blockchain such as Bitcoin or Ethereum.

## The arguments, side by side

| Question | Strongest bull argument | Strongest bear argument | Where the evidence points (Sep 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Is Bitcoin a monetary asset?** | Fixed supply, no issuer, censorship-resistant, verifiable; a digital gold that has survived four cycles, multiple bans and every "death"; now held by ETFs, corporations and some governments. | Gold has millennia of monetary use and industrial demand; bitcoin has fifteen years and none. It trades as a high-beta tech stock, not a hedge: it fell with equities in March 2020, August 2024 and the 2026 selloff. A store of value that loses 50–90% every few years is a speculation with good branding. | Both. It has behaved as a risk asset in every liquidity shock, *and* it has survived and recovered from each one so far. Sample size: four cycles. |
| **Greater-fool or network good?** | Adoption metrics (addresses, ETF holdings, corporate treasuries, stablecoin volume) grew across cycles; Lindy effect. | Retail adoption follows price, not utility (BIS), and returns are structurally transfers from late buyers to early holders. Most tokens are pure greater-fool: the 2018, 2021 and 2025 cohorts of altcoins mostly went to zero. | The bear case is decisively right about most tokens. For BTC and to a lesser degree ETH, it is unresolved. |
| **Are blockchains useful infrastructure?** | Stablecoins settle hundreds of billions monthly; DeFi protocols with real fee revenue (lending, perpetual exchanges) survived the 2026 shakeout when token-funded projects died; tokenized Treasuries and settlement pilots exist. | Fifteen years in, the killer apps are speculation, stablecoins and sanctions evasion (Chainalysis: $154B illicit volume in 2025, 84% in stablecoins, dominated by a sanctioned Russian token). Everything else is slower and costlier than a database. Over 100 projects folded in 2026 because they never had revenue. | Stablecoins are the clear product-market fit. Broader "web3" claims remain mostly unproven. |
| **Energy** | Bitcoin mining uses roughly as much electricity as a mid-sized country but increasingly from stranded, curtailed or flare gas sources; it is a flexible load that grids can shed. Ethereum cut its energy use by ~99.9% by moving to proof-of-stake in 2022. | Any energy use for a settlement network that processes a few transactions per second is hard to justify; miners follow cheap power, which is often coal. The "flexible load" argument works only where regulators allow it. | ETH's move removed the objection for that chain; for BTC it is a genuine cost that bulls must price in politically. |
| **Regulation** | The US moved from enforcement to frameworks in 2024–25 (spot ETFs, stablecoin legislation, market-structure rules); institutional access is now permanent. | Regulation cuts both ways: it legitimizes but also captures. Sanctions, tax reporting and stablecoin rules reduce the censorship-resistance that was the point. Policy can reverse: China banned mining in 2021; ETF flows reversed in 2026. | Regulatory risk fell for BTC/ETH in major jurisdictions but remains total for most tokens and for privacy-focused projects. |
| **Quantum computing** | A cryptographically relevant quantum computer is years to decades away; the community can migrate to post-quantum signatures; the same threat applies to all online banking. | Roughly a quarter of BTC sits in old address types that expose public keys, including Satoshi-era coins that cannot be migrated; migration requires a contentious upgrade (the 2026 BIP-110 debate showed how divisive protocol changes are). The risk is low-probability, high-impact and uninsurable. | A real tail risk on a horizon nobody can date; a reason for sizing, not for panic. |
| **Concentration** | Concentration is falling as ETFs and corporations distribute supply; the same is true of early-stage equity in any company. | Ownership is far more concentrated than in equities; whales sold into retail in every crash (BIS); the largest single corporate holder's selling in 2026 moved the market. A "decentralized" asset with centralized ownership. | The bear case is factually right; whether it matters depends on horizon. |
| **Stablecoins as the real product** | Dollar stablecoins are the first crypto product used by people who do not care about crypto: emerging-market savers, cross-border payments, 24/7 settlement. They also create demand for US Treasuries. | Then the winning product is the dollar, not the blockchain; issuers are regulated banks in all but name, and their reserves carry bank risk (USDC at $0.87 in March 2023). Most stablecoin volume is trading and, in 2025, illicit. | Largely agreed by both sides: stablecoins work. The disagreement is whether that validates the rest. |

## What the bears got right

- Almost every token outside the top handful has been a greater-fool asset, with three full cohorts (2018, 2022, 2026) going to near zero.
- Yield platforms, algorithmic stablecoins and exchanges failed exactly as critics predicted, taking customer money with them.
- Retail was the exit liquidity in every crash, and the majority of retail buyers lost money.
- Crime is a large, real use case: $154B in illicit volume in 2025 by Chainalysis's count, $11.4B in US fraud losses by the FBI's.
- "Institutional adoption ends volatility" was false; ETFs amplified the 2025 top and 2026 decline.

## What the bulls got right

- Bitcoin did not die. It has recovered from four 77–93% drawdowns and remained the best-performing major asset over any ten-year window to date.
- Regulators in the largest economies chose to integrate rather than ban, which was the bears' central prediction for a decade.
- Stablecoins found genuine users and now hold a meaningful slice of the Treasury market.
- A small number of protocols generate real fees from real users and survived a brutal shakeout.
- Ethereum solved its own energy problem, which critics said was impossible.

## How a non-ideological investor holds both

1. **Separate the assets.** The bull case is defensible for BTC, arguably ETH, and dollar stablecoins as a tool. It is not defensible for the long tail, where the bear case has been right three cycles in a row.
2. **Size for the bear case, hope for the bull case.** If the bear case is right, the position goes to zero or near it. Size it so that outcome is annoying, not life-altering: for most people, a low single-digit percentage of net worth, and never with borrowed money.
3. **Treat it as a venture bet with a liquid price.** A 5–10% chance of a monetary-asset outcome and a large chance of long stagnation or worse can still have positive expected value at a small size. That is a reason to own some, not a reason to own a lot.
4. **Rebalance mechanically.** Trim when it grows beyond the target, add when it shrinks below. This is the only way to be a seller at tops and buyer at bottoms without predicting either.
5. **Update on evidence, not price.** The things that would strengthen the bull case (non-speculative usage, falling concentration, successful protocol upgrades, quantum migration) and the bear case (a cycle without recovery, a successful attack, a major-jurisdiction ban) are observable. Price is not evidence of either.
6. **Read the other side on purpose.** If you own it, read Molly White and the BIS. If you dismiss it, read the surviving protocols' revenue data and the stablecoin flows.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- Write both cases in your own words in your investment policy statement, with the evidence you would need to change your mind. See the [investment policy statement template](../../10-checklists-and-templates/investment-policy-statement-template.md).
- Cap the allocation at a level where a 100% loss does not change your plans; cap the altcoin sub-allocation at a level where you would not bother to check the price.
- Rebalance on a calendar, not on conviction.
- Keep a list of "what would prove me wrong" for whichever side you lean toward, and review it every cycle.

## Key takeaways

- Both sides have been repeatedly wrong on timing and repeatedly right on structure.
- The bear case is settled for most tokens: three cohorts of altcoins have gone to near zero.
- The bull case is unresolved but not refuted for BTC, arguably ETH, and clearly supported for stablecoins as a product.
- Energy is a solved problem for ETH and a live political cost for BTC.
- Quantum risk and ownership concentration are real tail risks that argue for sizing, not avoidance.
- Regulation has been integrative in major markets, which the bears did not expect; ETFs increased rather than decreased volatility, which the bulls did not expect.
- Hold both cases by sizing for the bear outcome and rebalancing mechanically toward the bull one.
- Update on usage, concentration and protocol evidence; never on price.
