# Dividend and Income Investing

**In one sentence:** Build a portfolio that pays you cash on a schedule — through dividend-growing stocks, bonds, and sometimes option premium — while understanding that a dividend is not free money and that total return, not yield, is what actually determines how much you can spend.

## Two very different mindsets

**The income mindset** treats a portfolio like an orchard: never sell the trees, live on the fruit. Its appeal is psychological — you never have to decide when to sell, and a falling share price feels less threatening when the checks keep arriving. Its danger is that chasing yield leads to concentrating in a few slow-growing or troubled sectors and to reaching for payouts that are about to be cut.

**The total-return mindset** says that a dollar of dividend and a dollar of capital gain are the same dollar. When a company pays a $1 dividend, its share price drops by roughly $1 on the ex-dividend date; nothing was created, the money simply moved from the company's account to yours (and, in a taxable account, to the IRS's). Selling $1 of shares from a non-dividend stock produces the same cash with more control over timing and taxes. Vanguard's research on retirement spending consistently finds that a total-return approach with a fixed withdrawal rule is safer than an income-only approach, because it lets you hold a better-diversified portfolio.

Both mindsets can work. The key is to know which one you are using and not to pretend that a high-yield portfolio is "safer" merely because it pays cash.

## Dividend growth investing

The most defensible form of dividend investing focuses not on current yield but on the *rate at which the dividend rises*. A company that has raised its dividend every year for 25+ years has, by construction, survived several recessions, kept earning more than it pays out, and has a board that treats the dividend as sacred. That selection effect is where the strategy's merit lies.

Key numbers:

- **Dividend yield** = annual dividend ÷ price. The S&P 500 yields roughly 1.2%–1.5% in the mid-2020s; a dividend-growth portfolio typically yields 2%–3.5%.
- **Dividend growth rate**: the 5- and 10-year compound growth of the payout. 6%–10% a year is a strong record.
- **Payout ratio** = dividends ÷ earnings (or better, dividends ÷ free cash flow). Below 60% for most businesses leaves room for growth and for bad years; utilities and REITs run structurally higher. Above 100% means the dividend is being funded by debt or asset sales and will eventually be cut.
- **Yield on cost**: your current annual dividend ÷ what you originally paid. A stock bought at a 2.5% yield with 8% dividend growth yields 5.4% on cost after ten years and 11.7% after twenty. This is the engine of the strategy.

**Dividend Aristocrats** are S&P 500 members with 25+ consecutive years of dividend increases; there are 69 as of 2026 (examples: Johnson & Johnson, Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble, Walmart, Chevron, Emerson). **Dividend Kings** have 50+ years (roughly 55–60 companies depending on who is counting — Sure Dividend lists 59 as of September 2026 — including Procter & Gamble, Genuine Parts, Dover, Parker-Hannifin, Emerson, and American States Water at 72 years). The ProShares S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats ETF (NOBL, 0.35% expense ratio) tracks the former.

## Yield vs. growth: the trade-off

| Approach | Typical yield | Typical dividend growth | Typical holdings | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High yield | 5%–9% | 0%–3% | Telecoms, tobacco, MLPs, mortgage REITs, BDCs | Cuts, no growth, sector concentration |
| Dividend growth | 1.5%–3% | 7%–12% | Consumer staples, industrials, healthcare, tech that pays | Lower income today |
| Balanced ("quality yield") | 3%–4% | 5%–7% | Utilities, banks, pharma, REITs | Moderate |

A useful heuristic (the "Chowder rule," from a well-known Seeking Alpha contributor) is that yield plus 5-year dividend growth should exceed roughly 12% for a normal stock, or 8% for a utility. It forces you to think about both halves.

## Dividend traps

A dividend trap is a stock whose yield looks generous because the price has collapsed in anticipation of a cut. Warning signs:

- Yield far above peers (a 9% yield when the sector pays 4%).
- Payout ratio above 90% of earnings or above 100% of free cash flow.
- Rising debt used to fund the payout; declining revenue.
- Dividend held flat for several years after a long growth streak — a freeze often precedes a cut.
- Cyclical industry at a cycle peak (energy, shipping, chemicals) — the trailing yield reflects peak earnings.

History is littered with examples: General Electric, Kraft Heinz, AT&T, Intel, and Walgreens (a former Aristocrat) all slashed dividends after years of high yields. A 40% price drop wipes out ten years of a 4% yield.

## Covered-call ETFs: income that is really a trade

Funds such as JEPI (JPMorgan Equity Premium Income), JEPQ, XYLD, and QYLD sell call options against a stock portfolio and distribute the premium, producing headline yields of 7%–12%. Understand what you are buying: you keep the downside of stocks but give away the upside above the strike price. In flat or gently rising markets this looks wonderful; in strong bull markets the fund badly lags (QYLD has trailed the Nasdaq-100 by enormous margins since inception), and in crashes it falls almost as much as the index. Much of the "income" is a return of your own capital's upside, and distributions are often taxed as ordinary income. The Cboe BXM buy-write index over 1988–2006 matched the S&P 500's return with two-thirds the volatility — respectable, but that was a period with two crashes. These funds are tools for a specific view (range-bound markets), not a free lunch. See [options-strategies.md](options-strategies.md).

## Bonds and bond ladders

Bonds are the cleanest income instrument: a contractual coupon and return of principal at maturity. A **bond ladder** holds individual bonds (Treasuries, CDs, high-grade corporates, or municipals in a taxable account) with staggered maturities — say, one maturing each year for the next ten. Each year, the maturing bond is spent or reinvested at the long end. Advantages: known cash flows, no interest-rate loss if held to maturity, no fund fees. The mechanics are simple through any broker's bond desk or with TreasuryDirect; "defined-maturity" ETFs (iShares iBonds, Invesco BulletShares) build a ladder for you at 0.07%–0.10% a year.

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) ladders are the closest thing to a guaranteed real income stream; a 30-year TIPS ladder currently locks in roughly 2% real yield plus inflation (rate as of 2025–2026; check current levels).

## Taxes, briefly

Qualified dividends from U.S. and most foreign stocks held 60+ days are taxed at long-term capital-gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20%). REIT dividends, most covered-call distributions, bond interest, and BDC/MLP income are largely ordinary income. This argues for holding bonds, REITs, and covered-call funds in IRAs and 401(k)s, and dividend-growth stocks in taxable accounts if they must go somewhere.

## How to actually do it

1. **Decide the goal.** Are you building for income you will need in 20 years (choose dividend growth) or income you need now (choose a blend of dividend stocks, bonds, and possibly annuities)?
2. **Set the asset split first.** Income needs do not change the fact that a 100% stock portfolio can fall 50%. A retiree drawing income typically holds 40%–60% in bonds/cash.
3. **Core holding**: one or two dividend ETFs (SCHD plus VIG or DGRO is a common pairing; add VYMI for international).
4. **Optional individual stocks**: screen for 10+ years of dividend growth, payout ratio under 60% of FCF, net debt/EBITDA under 3, and yield + 5-yr growth above 12%. Hold 20–30 names across at least 8 sectors. Cap any sector at 20%.
5. **Build a bond ladder** for the income you need in the next 5–10 years — Treasuries or CDs, one rung per year. This is your "sleep at night" money.
6. **Reinvest dividends automatically** during accumulation (DRIP); switch reinvestment off when you begin drawing.
7. **Monitor annually**: payout ratio trend, dividend growth rate, and any freeze. Sell a stock when the dividend is cut or the payout ratio passes 90% with declining earnings — do not wait for the announcement.
8. **Check total return** against a plain index fund every year. If your income portfolio trails VTI by several points per year over five years, the yield is costing you.

## Key takeaways

- A dividend is a transfer from the company's cash to yours, not a gift; total return is what pays for retirement.
- Dividend *growth* (long streaks, low payout ratios) is a good quality screen; high current yield by itself is a warning sign.
- Yield on cost compounds: a 2.5% yield growing 8% a year becomes 5.4% in a decade.
- Watch payout ratio (< 60% of free cash flow), debt, and dividend freezes to avoid traps.
- SCHD, VIG, DGRO, and VYM deliver the strategy at 0.04%–0.08% a year; NOBL and Aristocrat lists are useful reference points.
- Covered-call ETFs trade away upside for current cash — fine in flat markets, expensive in bull markets.
- A Treasury or CD ladder is the simplest dependable income stream; put ordinary-income payers in tax-advantaged accounts.
