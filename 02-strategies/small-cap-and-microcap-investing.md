# Small-Cap and Microcap Investing

**In one sentence:** Small companies are followed by fewer analysts, owned by fewer institutions, and priced with less precision than large ones, which creates both a modest long-run return premium (mostly in cheap, profitable small caps) and the only part of the public market where a diligent individual can still know more than the professionals — at the cost of illiquidity, volatility, and a much higher rate of outright failure.

## Definitions

| Category | Market capitalization (approx.) | Index examples | Number of U.S. stocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large cap | > $10 billion | S&P 500, Russell 1000 | ~700 |
| Mid cap | $2–10 billion | S&P MidCap 400, Russell Midcap | ~800 |
| Small cap | $300 million – $2 billion | Russell 2000, S&P SmallCap 600 | ~2,000 |
| Microcap | $50–300 million | Russell Microcap | ~1,500 |
| Nanocap | < $50 million | none meaningful | thousands, mostly OTC |

The thresholds drift with the market. Roughly speaking, the Russell 2000 is what "small cap" means to institutions; the S&P 600 is a curated subset that requires positive earnings, which is why it has beaten the Russell 2000 by about 1–2 points a year since 1994.

## The size premium: what the evidence says now

Rolf Banz documented in 1981 that small stocks had beaten large stocks since 1926 by more than their extra risk explained. Fama and French made size one of their three factors in 1992. Over 1927–2025, the smallest U.S. stocks have returned roughly 1.5–2 points a year more than the largest, but almost all of that came in a few bursts (the 1930s–40s, 1975–1983, 2000–2010), and since publication the raw premium has been weak: the Russell 2000 has trailed the S&P 500 for most of 2011–2025, badly so in the mega-cap-led years after 2020.

Two findings rescue the idea:

1. **Quality.** Asness, Frazzini, Israel, Moskowitz, and Pedersen ("Size Matters, If You Control Your Junk," 2018) showed that the size premium is dragged down by a large population of tiny, unprofitable, heavily indebted "junk" companies. Remove them, and small caps beat large caps consistently across time and countries. The Russell 2000 is roughly 40% unprofitable companies in a typical year; the S&P 600 excludes most of them, which explains much of its edge.
2. **Value.** Small-cap *value* — cheap small companies — has a much stronger and more persistent record than small caps as a whole. Dimensional Fund Advisors' data shows U.S. small value returning roughly 13%–14% annualized since 1927 versus about 10% for the total market, though with deeper drawdowns and a long lag in 2010–2020.

The practical conclusion: do not buy "small caps." Buy small, profitable, cheap companies — through a fund built that way (AVUV, DFSV, VIOV, or the S&P 600 via IJR/VIOO) or by hand.

## Why the inefficiency exists

- **Coverage**: a large-cap stock has 30–50 sell-side analysts; a $200 million company may have one or none. Earnings surprises are bigger and prices adjust more slowly.
- **Institutional constraints**: a $10 billion fund cannot buy a meaningful position in a $150 million company without owning half of it. The largest and most sophisticated investors are structurally absent.
- **Index dynamics**: annual Russell reconstitution in June forces mechanical buying and selling; stocks entering the Russell 2000 from the microcap universe get a bid, and those leaving get dumped.
- **Information access**: management of small companies will take a call from a serious individual investor. Try that at Apple.

This is also why Peter Lynch, Warren Buffett (who has said he could earn 50% a year on $1 million because of the small-cap universe), and Joel Greenblatt all point retail investors here.

## Peter Lynch's ten-bagger approach

Lynch ran Fidelity Magellan from 1977 to 1990 at about 29% a year and wrote *One Up on Wall Street* (1989) to argue that ordinary people have an edge over professionals in small companies. His method:

- **Invest in what you know**, meaning use your job, hobbies, and shopping as an idea source — the store that is always packed, the product your industry suddenly cannot live without. Then *do the work*; the observation is the lead, not the thesis.
- **Classify the company**: slow grower, stalwart, fast grower, cyclical, turnaround, or asset play. Each has its own valuation logic and exit rule. Ten-baggers (stocks that rise tenfold) come almost entirely from fast growers found early and cyclicals bought at the bottom.
- **Check the story against the numbers**: the PEG ratio (P/E ÷ growth rate, ideally below 1), a strong balance sheet (cash exceeding debt), rising same-store sales for retailers, inventory not growing faster than sales, and insider buying.
- **Prefer the boring**: dull names, dull industries, no analyst coverage, something nobody would put on a magazine cover. Lynch's examples included funeral homes, bottle-cap makers, and a rock-quarry company.
- **Give winners room**: a stock up 50% is not automatically a sell; if the story is intact and it is still a fast grower at a reasonable PEG, the next 500% is where the return is. Selling the flowers and watering the weeds is the mistake he warned against.
- **Diversify across many ideas** because most will not be ten-baggers; Magellan held hundreds of positions. For an individual, 15–30 small caps is a reasonable range.

## Where to find information

| Source | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **SEC EDGAR** (sec.gov) | 10-K, 10-Q, proxy (DEF 14A), 8-K, Form 4 insider trades, 13D/13G large holders. Free and definitive. |
| **Company investor relations** | Presentations, transcripts, and — for microcaps — an actual phone call with the CFO. |
| **OTC Markets** (otcmarkets.com) | Tier (OTCQX, OTCQB, Pink) and disclosure status for over-the-counter companies. "Pink No Information" is a red flag. |
| **Screeners** | Finviz, TradingView, Stock Rover, GuruFocus, and broker screeners; filter by market cap, profitability, insider ownership. |
| **Whale Wisdom / Dataroma** | 13F filings of specialist small-cap funds. |
| **Value Investors Club, MicroCapClub, Seeking Alpha** | Write-ups by other investors; read for ideas, verify everything. |
| **Industry trade publications** | Often the only coverage a small company gets. |
| **Local knowledge** | Customers, suppliers, employees — Fisher's scuttlebutt is most powerful where no analyst has done it. |

## The risks, which are severe

- **Liquidity.** A stock trading $200,000 a day cannot absorb a $50,000 order without moving. Bid-ask spreads of 2%–5% are common in microcaps; getting out in a panic can cost 20%. Position size must be set by daily volume, not by conviction.
- **Failure rate.** A meaningful share of microcaps go to zero through dilution, delisting, or bankruptcy. The index return hides the fact that it is a few big winners paying for many losers.
- **Fraud and promotion.** Pump-and-dump schemes live almost exclusively in nanocaps and OTC stocks. Paid promotion, reverse mergers, serial share issuance, auditors you have never heard of, and management with a history of failed shells are disqualifying.
- **Volatility.** Small caps fell roughly 60% in 2008 and 40% in early 2020; individual names routinely halve on one earnings report.
- **Dilution.** Small companies raise money by issuing shares; check whether share count has grown faster than the business.
- **Taxes and costs.** High turnover in illiquid names is expensive on both counts.

## How to actually do it

1. **Get the beta cheaply first.** Hold 10%–25% of your equity allocation in a quality-screened small-cap or small-value fund: AVUV or DFSV (small value with profitability screens, ~0.25%–0.31%), VIOV or IJS (S&P 600 value, ~0.10%–0.18%), IJR or VIOO (S&P 600 blend, ~0.06%–0.10%). Avoid plain Russell 2000 funds (IWM, VTWO) — the junk drags them. Add AVDV for international small value.
2. **Only then consider individual names**, and cap them at 5%–10% of the total portfolio across 10–20 positions, no single one above 2% at purchase.
3. **Screen**: market cap $50M–$2B; positive free cash flow in at least 3 of the last 5 years; net debt/EBITDA under 2; insider ownership above 10%; share count growth under 3% a year; EV/EBIT under 12 or PEG under 1.2. Exclude OTC Pink "No Information" and anything with a recent reverse merger.
4. **Read everything filed in the last three years** — a small company's 10-K is 80 pages, not 300. Read the proxy for compensation and related-party dealings. Read Form 4s for insider buying.
5. **Call the company.** Ask the CFO what the market misunderstands and what keeps them up at night. Note whether the answers match the filings.
6. **Classify the company Lynch-style** and write the thesis and the exit rule: for a fast grower, sell when growth falls below the PEG threshold; for a cyclical, sell when margins peak; for an asset play, sell when the asset is recognized.
7. **Size by liquidity**: a position should be no more than a few days of average volume, so you can exit inside a week.
8. **Buy over weeks using limit orders**; never market orders in illiquid names.
9. **Let winners run and cut broken theses**; review each quarterly on the thesis, not the price.
10. **Track your record against the small-value fund** you hold in step 1. If after five years the hand-picked sleeve has not beaten it, that is the answer.

## Key takeaways

- The raw size premium is small and has mostly been absent since 2011; the premium for *cheap, profitable* small companies is much stronger — buy that, not "small caps."
- The S&P 600 (profitability required) beats the Russell 2000 by 1–2 points a year for a reason; AVUV, DFSV, VIOV, and IJR are the sensible fund choices.
- Small caps are the one arena where an individual's research can beat institutions, because the institutions cannot participate.
- Lynch's method: source ideas from daily life, verify with numbers (PEG < 1, cash > debt), classify the company, and let winners run.
- Liquidity, dilution, fraud, and outright failure are the price of admission; size positions by daily volume and cap the hand-picked sleeve at 5%–10%.
- Read the filings and call management; that access is the edge.
- Benchmark your picks against a small-value fund and be honest about the result.
