# Contrarian and Special-Situations Investing

**In one sentence:** Look where other investors are structurally unable or emotionally unwilling to look — spin-offs, post-bankruptcy equities, merger deals, forced selling, and hated sectors — because the mispricing there comes from the mechanics of the market rather than from anyone being smarter than you.

## The contrarian premise

Markets overshoot. Prices are set by the marginal buyer and seller, and at extremes those people are acting on emotion (panic, euphoria) or on constraints (a fund that *must* sell, an index that *must* drop a stock). Contrarian investing bets on **mean reversion**: the tendency of extreme prices, valuations, and sentiment to move back toward normal. Evidence includes De Bondt and Thaler's 1985 finding that the worst-performing stocks of the prior three to five years went on to beat the best performers over the next three to five years, and the century-long record of cheap markets (low CAPE) delivering higher subsequent decade returns than expensive ones.

Being contrarian is not simply doing the opposite of the crowd — the crowd is right most of the time, which is why trends exist (see [momentum-and-trend-following.md](momentum-and-trend-following.md)). It is identifying situations where the crowd's action is *forced* or *emotional* rather than informed, and where you have a specific reason to expect reversion. Howard Marks's phrase for the goal is "second-level thinking": not "this is a good company" but "this is a good company that everyone hates for reasons that will pass."

Mean reversion is slow and unreliable at the single-stock level; a beaten-down stock can stay beaten for years or go to zero. The special situations below are attractive because they add a *catalyst* and a *structural* reason for the mispricing.

## Joel Greenblatt's playbook

Greenblatt's *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* (1997) is the canonical retail guide to special situations, drawn from his Gotham Capital record (reported ~50% annualized over ten years, 1985–1994, before returning outside capital). The book's thesis: corporate events create temporary mispricings because the investors who receive the new securities did not choose them and often cannot hold them.

### Spin-offs

A parent company distributes shares of a subsidiary to its own shareholders. The new stock arrives unbidden in the accounts of index funds (which may have to sell if it is not in their index), large institutions (too small for them), and dividend funds (it pays none). Nobody covers it, nobody wants it, and it is dumped in the first weeks. Meanwhile the spun-off management, often newly incentivized with stock, is finally free to run the business.

The evidence is unusually strong for an anomaly. Cusatis, Miles, and Woolridge (1993) found spin-offs beat their peers by about 30 percentage points over three years, 1965–1988. McConnell and colleagues at Purdue re-tested for 2001–2013 and found spun-off subsidiaries returned 31.7% over their first 22 months versus 14.6% for a matched benchmark — 17 points of excess return, or about 0.7% per month. Parents outperformed too, but modestly (about 3.7 points over 15 months). Greenblatt's refinements:

- Look for spin-offs where **insiders are buying** or receive large option grants.
- Look for spin-offs that institutions **structurally must sell** (too small, wrong sector, no dividend).
- Look for a **hidden asset** — a good business buried inside a bad one, or a loaded-up parent shedding debt onto the spin.
- Wait a few weeks to a few months for the forced selling to finish before buying.

Two cautions: the spin-off ETF (CSD, later renamed) has had a poor record because it buys mechanically at the wrong time and holds everything; and some spin-offs are dumps of the parent's worst assets and debt (the "bad SpinCo"). Read the Form 10 filing.

### Merger arbitrage

When Company A agrees to buy Company B for $50 a share, B typically trades at $47–$49 until the deal closes. The gap ("spread") compensates for the risk the deal fails, the time until closing, and the cost of capital. Buying B and, in a stock deal, shorting A, captures the spread if the deal closes.

Mitchell and Pulvino's study of 4,750 deals from 1963 to 1998 found merger arbitrage earned meaningful excess returns — the authors estimated roughly 4 percentage points a year above risk-free rates after costs, below the double-digit figures other studies had claimed — but with an important pattern: returns are uncorrelated with the market in normal times and turn *sharply* correlated in crashes, when deals break en masse. The payoff looks like selling a put option on the market. Retail access: individual deals through a broker, or the Merger Fund (MERFX) and IQ Merger Arbitrage ETF (MNA). Expect bond-like returns with an occasional bad year; 2022 was decent for the strategy and 2008 was ugly.

### Post-bankruptcy equities

When a company emerges from Chapter 11, its old creditors receive the new stock. Bond funds and banks that hold it did not want equity, cannot hold it under their mandates, and sell. The new company has typically shed its debt and its worst contracts. Greenblatt argued this is one of the least-followed corners of the market. Evidence is mixed — studies find post-reorganization equities outperform on average but with wide dispersion and some large failures — so the approach is to examine each case: is the new balance sheet genuinely clean, does management own stock, and was the underlying business sound (a good business with too much debt) or broken (a bad business that will need Chapter 22)?

### Other special situations from the book

- **Rights offerings and recapitalizations**: complex transactions that create orphaned securities (warrants, preferred stock) few investors bother to value.
- **Stub stocks**: the thin equity sliver left after a leveraged recapitalization; enormous upside, enormous risk.
- **Partial spin-offs and carve-outs**: a parent IPOs a piece of a subsidiary, and the remaining parent stub can trade below the value of its retained stake.
- **Index deletions and forced selling**: a stock dropped from an index, or a fund liquidation, creates selling that has nothing to do with value.

## The Magic Formula

In *The Little Book That Beats the Market* (2005), Greenblatt distilled Buffett into two numbers: buy good businesses (high **return on capital** — EBIT divided by net working capital plus net fixed assets) at cheap prices (high **earnings yield** — EBIT divided by enterprise value). Rank every stock above a minimum size on each measure, add the two ranks, buy the top 20–30, hold one year, repeat.

His backtest claimed about 30% a year from 1988 to 2004 versus 12% for the market. Independent replications have found smaller but real outperformance: a 2016–2025 European out-of-sample test by Quant Investing showed about 13.6% annualized (with dividends) versus 8.3% for the STOXX 600, beating the market in only five of ten years, with a painful 2022–2024 stretch. Critics (Wesley Gray, Tobias Carlisle) argue the return-on-capital half adds little and that earnings yield alone (Carlisle's "Acquirer's Multiple") does most of the work. The Magic Formula's real contribution is behavioral: it is a fully mechanical value-plus-quality rule that a person can run in an afternoon a year. Its failure mode is also behavioral — Greenblatt reported that clients who were allowed to override the formula's picks underperformed the pure formula.

## Contrarian sector and market timing

The broader contrarian approach — buying sectors, countries, or asset classes after they have been crushed — has some support (the CAPE evidence, Rob Arnott's work on "buying the unloved"), but the timing is unreliable enough that most practitioners implement it through rebalancing rather than bets: a fixed-weight portfolio automatically buys what fell and sells what rose. Adding a deliberate tilt toward the cheapest regions or sectors (e.g., overweighting international value when U.S. growth is at record relative valuations) is a defensible mild version. Making concentrated bets on a bottom is a good way to be right eventually and broke first.

## Risks common to all of this

- **Cheap for a reason**: the contrarian's constant trap. Structural decline is not mean-reverting.
- **Time**: catalysts slip; deals take 18 months; spin-off value can take two years to be recognized. Capital tied up waiting has an opportunity cost.
- **Complexity**: special situations require reading Form 10s, proxy statements, plans of reorganization, and merger agreements. If you will not read them, you are guessing.
- **Concentration**: each situation is idiosyncratic; a portfolio of 5–10 of them is not diversified in the usual sense, though it is often uncorrelated with the market.
- **Crowding**: spin-offs are far more followed than in 1997; the easy excess returns have narrowed, though the Purdue data suggests they had not vanished by 2013.

## How to actually do it

1. **Allocate a sleeve** — 10%–20% of the equity portfolio — to special situations. Keep the core indexed.
2. **Build a pipeline.** Free sources: SEC EDGAR full-text search for "Form 10" (spin-offs) and "plan of reorganization" (bankruptcies); Stock Spinoff Investing and similar newsletters; merger announcements via broker news; the 52-week-low list for contrarian candidates.
3. **For spin-offs**: read the Form 10. Note the debt allocated to the spin, the management incentive plan, and which parent shareholders will be forced sellers. Wait 1–3 months after the distribution. Buy if the business is decent and the stock is below a conservative multiple of normalized free cash flow.
4. **For the Magic Formula**: use the free screener at magicformulainvesting.com, choose a minimum market cap ($100M–$1B), buy the top 20–30 in equal weights, hold one year, replace. Do not override.
5. **For merger arbitrage**: use a fund (MNA, MERFX) unless you will read the merger agreement's termination conditions and regulatory risk. Individual deals are for people who enjoy antitrust law.
6. **For post-bankruptcy**: wait for the new shares to trade for 3–6 months, then evaluate as a normal value investment with the plan of reorganization as your 10-K.
7. **Write the catalyst and the deadline** for every position: what has to happen, by when, and what you will do if it does not.
8. **Track outcomes** over at least 15–20 situations before concluding you have an edge. Dispersion is high; one 3x winner and three zeros is a normal-looking record.

## Key takeaways

- Contrarian investing profits from mean reversion, but only reliably where selling is forced or emotional and there is a catalyst.
- Spin-offs have the best-documented excess returns of any special situation: roughly 17–30 percentage points above benchmark over 2–3 years across two academic studies spanning 1965–2013.
- Merger arbitrage earns a few points over cash with put-option-like crash risk; access it through funds unless you read deal documents.
- Post-bankruptcy equities are orphaned by creditors who did not want them; outcomes are widely dispersed.
- Greenblatt's Magic Formula (high earnings yield + high return on capital, rebalanced annually) has beaten the market out-of-sample by a smaller margin than advertised, and only if followed mechanically.
- Every special situation is cheap for a reason; the work is establishing that the reason is structural, not fundamental.
- Keep this as a 10%–20% sleeve and judge results over 15+ situations.
