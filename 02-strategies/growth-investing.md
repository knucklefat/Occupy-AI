# Growth Investing

**In one sentence:** Buy companies whose revenues and earnings are expanding much faster than the economy, accept a high price today on the bet that tomorrow's much larger earnings will justify it, and manage the one risk that ruins the strategy — paying a multiple that collapses when growth slows.

## The lineage: Price, Fisher, and their heirs

Thomas Rowe Price Jr. founded his firm in 1937 on the then-radical idea that the best returns come from owning companies in the *growth phase of their life cycle* — where earnings are rising faster than the economy — and holding them through the cycle rather than trading around it. Price wanted businesses with superior research, expanding markets, patents or cost advantages, rising profit margins, and management that owned stock. He held IBM, 3M, and Merck for decades.

Philip Fisher's *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (1958) supplied the qualitative method. His "scuttlebutt" approach meant talking to customers, suppliers, competitors, and former employees to learn what the financial statements could not show. His fifteen points asked, in essence: does this company have products with years of sales growth ahead, a determination to keep developing new ones, strong margins with a plan to improve them, excellent labor and executive relations, depth of management, honest accounting, and integrity? Fisher held Motorola from 1955 until his death in 2004. Buffett has said he is "85% Graham and 15% Fisher," and the Fisher share has grown over time.

Later practitioners include Peter Lynch (Fidelity Magellan, 29% annualized 1977–1990, who blended growth with common-sense valuation), and the modern growth-fund managers at T. Rowe Price, Baillie Gifford, and ARK — the last a cautionary tale in what happens when growth is bought without any valuation discipline.

## GARP: growth at a reasonable price

Pure growth investing pays any price for the best growers. Pure value refuses to pay up for anything. GARP, associated with Lynch, sits between: look for companies growing earnings 15%–25% a year and buy them only when the price is proportionate to that growth.

The tool is the **PEG ratio**: the P/E ratio divided by the expected annual earnings growth rate (in percent). A company at 30× earnings growing 30% a year has a PEG of 1.0. Lynch's rule of thumb: a PEG around 1 is fairly priced, under 1 is attractive, above 2 is expensive.

| P/E | Growth rate | PEG | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 20% | 0.75 | Cheap for its growth |
| 30 | 30% | 1.0 | Fair |
| 45 | 15% | 3.0 | Priced for perfection |
| 80 | 40% | 2.0 | Expensive, and 40% growth rarely lasts |

PEG's weaknesses: it uses forecast growth (analysts are systematically optimistic), it is meaningless when earnings are near zero, and it treats a 10% grower at 10× and a 50% grower at 50× as equally attractive when the first is far more likely to deliver.

## The Rule of 40 and other growth-company metrics

Many of the most interesting growers (software, biotech, marketplaces) have no meaningful earnings yet, so growth investors developed metrics that work before profitability:

- **Rule of 40** (popularized by venture investor Brad Feld in 2015): revenue growth rate + profit margin (usually free-cash-flow or EBITDA margin) should exceed 40. A company growing 60% with −20% margins passes; one growing 10% with 30% margins passes; one growing 25% at 5% margins fails. It captures the trade-off between growth and burn in a single number.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR)**: revenue from last year's customers this year, including upgrades and churn. Above 120% means the existing base grows by itself.
- **Gross margin**: software above 70% signals a scalable model; hardware or services under 40% means growth costs money.
- **Customer acquisition cost payback**: months to recover the cost of winning a customer. Under 18 months is healthy.
- **Operating leverage**: are operating expenses growing slower than revenue? If not, "growth" is being purchased, not earned.

## Screening for growth

A reasonable starting screen:

| Criterion | Setting | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth, 3-yr CAGR | > 15% | Real growth, not one good year |
| EPS growth, 3-yr CAGR | > 15% (or Rule of 40 > 40 if unprofitable) | Growth reaching the bottom line |
| Gross margin | > 40%, stable or rising | Pricing power |
| Return on equity | > 15% | Growth financed efficiently |
| PEG | < 2 (< 1.5 preferred) | Not paying for perfection |
| Net debt / EBITDA | < 2 | Not growing on borrowed money |
| Share count, 3-yr change | < +5% | Growth not diluted away |

Then ask Fisher's questions: how big is the addressable market, what stops competitors, and is management investing for the next decade or the next quarter?

## The central risk: multiple compression

A growth stock's return has two parts: earnings growth and the change in what investors will pay per dollar of earnings (the multiple). When both rise, returns are spectacular. When earnings keep growing but the multiple falls, returns can be zero or negative for years — this is **multiple compression**, and it is how most growth-investing money is lost.

Arithmetic example: a company earns $1/share, grows earnings 25% a year for five years to $3.05, and trades at 60× today. If the market still pays 60× in five years, the stock rises from $60 to $183 (25%/yr). If the multiple falls to a still-generous 25×, the stock goes to $76 — about 5%/yr, despite flawless execution. If the multiple falls to 15× (roughly the market average), the stock *falls* to $46. Microsoft earned more every year from 2000 to 2013 and its stock went nowhere, because its P/E went from ~60 to ~12. Cisco, at 2000's peak, needed 20+ years to regain its high.

The 2021–2022 episode repeated the lesson: the ARK Innovation ETF fell roughly 75% from peak while many of its holdings kept growing revenue. Growth did not fail; the price of growth reset when interest rates rose.

Defenses:

- Never pay a multiple that assumes growth persists beyond 5–7 years. Very few companies grow 25%+ for a decade; those that do are famous precisely because they are rare.
- Model the "still good but slower" scenario: what is the stock worth if growth halves and the multiple halves? If that number is unbearable, the position is too big.
- Prefer growth that is already producing free cash flow. Profitless growth is a bet on financing conditions, not just the business.
- Diversify. The growth style is a bet that a few holdings become enormous; the base rate for any single candidate is poor.

## Growth vs. value: the long view

Over the 1926–present record, cheap stocks have beaten expensive ones on average, which is a strike against paying up for growth. But the record is lumpy: growth crushed value in the late 1990s and from about 2007 to 2020, and again in 2023–2025 when a handful of AI-related mega-caps dominated. The practical takeaway is not "growth or value" but "growth at what price." The best long-run growth investors — Price, Fisher, Lynch — were all disciplined about price; they just measured value in future earnings rather than current book value.

## How to actually do it

1. **Decide how much of your portfolio is a growth sleeve.** For most people, 10%–30% of equities alongside a core index holding; the rest of the market already contains plenty of growth.
2. **Screen** using the table above, or simply start with a list of companies whose products you see winning in daily life (Lynch's approach) and then verify with the numbers.
3. **Understand the growth engine.** Is it more customers, more revenue per customer, new products, new geographies, or price increases? Each has a different ceiling and durability.
4. **Size the market.** Estimate the total addressable market honestly; if the company already has 40% share, most of the growth is behind it.
5. **Check the unit economics**: gross margin, NRR, CAC payback, Rule of 40. A company can grow fast and destroy value.
6. **Value it with a forward lens**: estimate earnings in year 5, apply a *normal* multiple (15–25×), discount back at 10%, and compare to today's price. If today's price already exceeds that, you are paying for year 8+.
7. **Buy in tranches** rather than all at once — growth stocks move 30% on an earnings report.
8. **Set a thesis-based exit**: sell when growth decelerates below your threshold for two consecutive quarters, when the multiple exceeds what any plausible growth justifies, or when the story changes. Do not sell merely because the stock doubled.
9. **Review quarterly**: revenue growth rate, gross margin trend, and cash burn are the three lines to watch.

## Key takeaways

- Growth investing works when earnings growth is large, durable, and bought at a proportionate price — Price and Fisher were valuation-conscious, not price-blind.
- PEG ≈ 1 is fair; below 1 is attractive; the forecast growth rate is the weak link.
- For unprofitable growers, use Rule of 40, net revenue retention, gross margin, and CAC payback.
- Multiple compression, not business failure, is the primary way growth investors lose money — model the "slower growth, lower multiple" case before buying.
- Very few companies sustain 25%+ growth beyond five to seven years; price accordingly.
- Diversify the growth sleeve and buy in tranches; single-name outcomes are wildly dispersed.
- Sell on thesis breaks and deceleration, not on gains.
