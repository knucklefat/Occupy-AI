# Index and Passive Investing

**In one sentence:** Own the whole market through low-cost index funds, hold for decades, and let the arithmetic of low fees and broad diversification do the work that most professional stock-pickers demonstrably fail to do.

## Why indexing works: the arithmetic and the evidence

Every dollar invested in the stock market is, collectively, "the market." Before costs, the average active investor earns exactly the market return, because active investors *are* the market. After costs — management fees, trading commissions, bid-ask spreads, taxes triggered by turnover — the average active investor must earn *less* than the market. Nobel laureate William Sharpe laid this out in his 1991 paper "The Arithmetic of Active Management," and it is not a theory; it is accounting.

The empirical record confirms it. S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) Scorecard twice a year, comparing actively managed mutual funds to their benchmark indexes with survivorship-bias correction (dead funds are counted, not quietly dropped). The Year-End 2025 U.S. Scorecard, published May 2026, reports the share of actively managed large-cap U.S. funds that *underperformed* the S&P 500:

| Horizon | % of active large-cap funds that lost to the S&P 500 |
|---|---|
| 1 year (2025) | 78.8% |
| 3 years | 66.8% |
| 5 years | 89.0% |
| 10 years | 85.6% |
| 15 years | 89.9% |
| 20 years | 92.9% |

Across *all* domestic equity funds versus the S&P Composite 1500, 95% underperformed over 20 years. Small-cap active managers — the group most often claimed to have an "inefficient market" edge — lost to the S&P SmallCap 600 at a 90% rate over 20 years. The companion Persistence Scorecard finds that of large-cap funds in the top quartile in 2021, essentially 0% remained top-quartile through 2025, which is what you would expect if past outperformance were mostly luck.

Fair warning: a 2026 academic paper by Cremers, Fulkerson and Riley argues that SPIVA's method (equal-weighting funds, including tiny ones, and benchmark choices) overstates active failure somewhat. Even their more generous accounting still shows most active funds trailing after fees. The direction of the finding has never been seriously in dispute.

## The three pillars of passive investing

1. **Broad diversification.** A total-market fund holds thousands of stocks. You are not betting on any company, sector, or manager; you are betting that capitalism as a whole keeps producing earnings.
2. **Very low cost.** Total-market index funds charge 0.03%–0.05% per year. The average actively managed U.S. equity fund still charges roughly 0.6%–1.0%. On a $500,000 portfolio over 30 years, a 0.7% annual cost gap compounds to hundreds of thousands of dollars.
3. **Behavioral discipline.** The plan is set in advance — allocation, contribution schedule, rebalancing rule — so that market news does not become trading decisions. Morningstar's annual "Mind the Gap" study finds that investors as a group earn roughly one percentage point less per year than the funds they own, purely from poorly timed buying and selling.

## The three-fund portfolio

The Bogleheads community (named for Vanguard founder John Bogle) popularized a portfolio of exactly three index funds:

| Role | Vanguard mutual fund | ETF equivalents (any provider) |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. total stock market | VTSAX | VTI, ITOT, SCHB, SPTM |
| International total stock market | VTIAX | VXUS, IXUS, SPDW+SPEM |
| U.S. total bond market | VBTLX | BND, AGG, SCHZ |

That is it. The stock/bond split is the main decision, and the U.S./international split within stocks is secondary. Typical starting points:

| Investor profile | U.S. stocks | Int'l stocks | Bonds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aggressive, 25 years old, 30+ years to retirement | 60% | 30% | 10% |
| Moderate, mid-career | 45% | 25% | 30% |
| Conservative, near or in retirement | 30% | 15% | 55% |

A common rule of thumb is "age in bonds" (a 40-year-old holds ~40% bonds) or, for those comfortable with more risk, "age minus 20 in bonds." Vanguard's own research suggests international stocks at 20%–40% of the equity sleeve captures most of the diversification benefit.

## Even simpler: one-fund and two-fund versions

- **Target-date fund** (e.g., Vanguard Target Retirement 2055, Fidelity Freedom Index 2055, Schwab Target 2055 Index): a single fund that holds the three-fund mix and shifts toward bonds automatically as the target year approaches. The ideal default for 401(k)s and anyone who wants zero maintenance.
- **Two-fund**: a world stock fund (VT — Vanguard Total World Stock) plus a bond fund (BND). VT holds ~9,000 stocks at global market weight, so the U.S./international question answers itself.
- **Balanced fund**: Vanguard Balanced Index (VBIAX) holds 60% U.S. stocks / 40% U.S. bonds in one ticker.

## Other well-known "lazy portfolios"

| Name | Allocation | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Bogleheads three-fund | Above | Everything, cheaply |
| Coffeehouse (Bill Schultheis) | 40% bonds; 10% each in S&P 500, large value, small blend, small value, REIT, international | Tilts toward value and small |
| Swensen "Unconventional Success" | 30% U.S. stocks, 15% international developed, 5% emerging, 20% REIT, 15% Treasuries, 15% TIPS | Real assets and inflation protection |
| Rick Ferri Core Four | 48% U.S. total, 24% international, 8% REIT, 20% bonds (for an 80/20 investor) | Three-fund plus real estate |
| Larry Swedroe "Larry Portfolio" | ~30% small-cap value (U.S. and international), 70% short/intermediate Treasuries | Concentrated factor bet, mostly safe bonds |
| 60/40 classic | 60% total stock, 40% total bond | The institutional default for decades |

None of these is provably better than the others in advance. What they share is what matters: low cost, broad diversification, and a written rule you can follow when markets are ugly.

## Costs and details that matter

- **Expense ratio** is the annual fee taken out of the fund. Under 0.10% is the target for core holdings; under 0.20% is acceptable for niche exposures.
- **ETF vs. mutual fund**: nearly identical for index strategies. ETFs are slightly more tax-efficient in taxable accounts and trade intraday; index mutual funds allow automatic dollar-amount investing and no bid-ask spread. In a 401(k) or IRA the difference is trivial.
- **Tracking error**: how closely the fund follows its index. For the big providers (Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, iShares, SPDR) it is negligible.
- **"Index fund" is not automatically passive.** Leveraged, inverse, thematic, and narrow-sector ETFs are index funds in legal form but speculative in substance. The passive philosophy means owning the *whole* market, not a slice you picked because it was in the news.

## How to actually do it

1. **Pick the stock/bond split** based on how long until you need the money and how much decline you can watch without selling. If you have never held through a 30%+ crash, assume you are less tolerant than you think.
2. **Choose the container**: 401(k)/403(b) first up to the employer match, then Roth or traditional IRA, then HSA if eligible, then a taxable brokerage account. (See the tax section of this library.)
3. **Select three funds** — or one target-date fund — matching the tickers above at whichever brokerage you use. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer equivalent funds; do not chase a 0.01% difference.
4. **Automate contributions** every payday. This is dollar-cost averaging, and its main value is that it removes you from the decision.
5. **Rebalance once a year** (or when an allocation drifts more than 5 percentage points from target). In taxable accounts, rebalance with new contributions rather than sales to avoid capital gains.
6. **Write an Investment Policy Statement** — one page: your allocation, your contribution rate, your rebalancing rule, and what you will do in a crash (answer: nothing). Reread it before making any change.
7. **Ignore the rest.** No stock tips, no fund of the year, no "this time the market is overvalued" timing. The entire edge of this strategy is that you never act on those.

## Key takeaways

- After costs, the average active investor *must* trail the market; this is arithmetic, not opinion.
- SPIVA data through 2025: roughly 93% of active large-cap U.S. funds lost to the S&P 500 over 20 years; 95% of all domestic equity funds lost to the S&P 1500.
- Past fund outperformance does not persist — top-quartile funds almost never stay there for four years.
- A three-fund portfolio (U.S. total market, international total market, total bond) costs ~0.05% per year and beats most professionals.
- The stock/bond ratio is the decision that matters; everything else is fine-tuning.
- A target-date index fund is a perfectly respectable one-decision answer.
- The strategy's real edge is behavioral: automate, rebalance annually, and never react to headlines.
