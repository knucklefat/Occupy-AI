# The Behavior Gap

**In one sentence:** The funds most people own earn more than the people who own them, because investors buy after prices rise and sell after they fall — and that self-inflicted "behavior gap" compounds into a large share of lifetime wealth.

## What the gap is

A fund's published return (its "total return") assumes you bought on day one, held the whole period, and never added or withdrew a dollar. Almost nobody does that. Real investors add money when they feel confident (usually after a rally) and pull it out when they feel scared (usually after a drop). The return the *average dollar* actually earned — the "investor return," or dollar-weighted return — is therefore usually lower than the fund's headline number.

The difference between the two is the behavior gap. It is not a fee. It is not a tax. It is the price of timing, and it is almost entirely under your control.

## The evidence

### Morningstar "Mind the Gap"

Morningstar has run this study annually for over a decade, comparing investor returns to total returns across every US fund and ETF. The latest edition (published 2026, covering the ten years ended December 31, 2025) found:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Average fund total return | 9.9% per year |
| Average investor return | 8.7% per year |
| Annual gap | 1.2 percentage points |
| Share of returns lost to timing | roughly 12% |

The gap varies enormously by fund type, and the pattern is instructive:

- **Broad US stock funds** had one of the smallest gaps (about 0.5 points). Boring, diversified, widely held funds are hard to mistime.
- **Allocation funds** (balanced and target-date funds that hold stocks and bonds together) also had narrow gaps. Investors in these funds tend to contribute on autopilot through retirement plans and rarely tinker.
- **Sector, alternative, and volatile niche funds** had the widest gaps. The most volatile fifth of funds showed gaps above 2 points a year, versus roughly 0.4 points for the least volatile.
- **Index funds** had a smaller gap (about 1.1 points) than **active funds** (about 1.6 points), largely because the people who buy index funds tend to be the kind who hold them.
- **ETFs** had a wider gap than traditional mutual funds — not because ETFs are worse, but because it is easier to trade them, and people do.

Earlier editions of the same study found the same shape: gaps of roughly 1 to 1.7 points a year, worst in narrow, exciting, volatile funds and best in dull diversified ones.

### Dalbar QAIB

Dalbar's Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior has estimated the gap since 1994 using industry-wide fund flows. Its headline numbers are larger and more volatile than Morningstar's:

- In 2024, Dalbar estimated the average equity fund investor earned 16.5% while the S&P 500 returned 25.0% — an 8.5-point gap, the second largest of the decade.
- In 2025, the gap shrank to under one point (17.2% vs 17.9%), one of the smallest since 1985.
- Over the 20 years ending 2024, Dalbar's hypothetical buy-and-hold investor turned $100,000 into roughly $717,000; the average investor's cash-flow pattern produced about $346,000 — less than half.
- Dalbar found the average equity fund holding period was only about 4.8 years in 2024, roughly half a typical market cycle.

**A caveat you should know.** Dalbar's method has been criticized (notably by retirement researcher Wade Pfau) for treating investors' gradual contributions as though they were a lump sum invested at the start of the period, which mechanically exaggerates the gap. A 2024 academic paper also argued Morningstar's method overstates the cost of timing. Take the exact size of the gap with a grain of salt. The *direction* and the *pattern* — worse in volatile funds, better in automated, diversified ones — are robust across every study and every period.

### Academic confirmation

Brad Barber and Terrance Odean, studying tens of thousands of brokerage accounts, found that the households who traded most earned roughly 11.4% a year against 17.9% for the market over their sample period — and that men, who traded 45% more than women, paid for it with lower net returns. The mechanism is the same as the fund studies: activity driven by emotion and overconfidence destroys returns.

## Why the gap exists

1. **Performance chasing.** Money flows into whatever just went up. Morningstar consistently finds purchases concentrated after strong runs. Buying after a rise means buying at higher prices and lower future returns.
2. **Panic selling.** Outflows spike after sharp declines — precisely when expected future returns are highest. The March 2020 and 2022 episodes both showed this.
3. **Volatility invites action.** A fund that swings 40% gives you many opportunities to do something stupid. A fund that swings 10% gives you few.
4. **Narrow funds are bought as bets, not holdings.** Nobody buys a semiconductor ETF as a permanent core position. They buy it because it is hot, and they leave when it cools.
5. **Ease of trading.** The friction that once protected investors (phone calls, paper forms, commissions) is gone. A tap on a phone is enough to mistime.

## What it costs over decades

Suppose two investors each put $500 a month into the same fund for 30 years. The fund returns 9% a year. One holds through everything; the other, through poorly timed moves, earns 1.2 points less — 7.8%.

- The disciplined investor ends with roughly $915,000.
- The typical investor ends with roughly $715,000.

A gap that sounds trivial — "one point a year" — quietly removed about $200,000, more than a fifth of the final result. Using Dalbar's larger estimates, the loss is closer to half. Either way, this is the single largest controllable drag on most people's returns, larger than fund fees and far larger than the difference between good and mediocre fund selection.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- **Automate contributions.** A standing monthly transfer that buys regardless of headlines is the most reliable gap-closer in the data. Retirement-plan investors in target-date funds show the narrowest gaps precisely because they never decide.
- **Own boring things.** Broad, diversified, low-volatility funds are hard to mistime and easy to hold. The wider the fund's potential swings, the wider your likely gap.
- **Measure your own gap.** Most brokerages show a "personal rate of return" (money-weighted). Compare it to the fund's published return once a year. If you are consistently behind, your behavior — not your fund — is the problem.
- **Add friction on purpose.** Delete the trading app from your phone. Use a written rule that any sale must wait 72 hours after the decision. Require a second signature (a spouse, a friend) on any move larger than 10% of the portfolio.
- **Rebalance on a calendar, not on feelings.** A fixed date (or a fixed threshold, such as any asset drifting 5 points from target) converts "should I do something?" into a mechanical answer.
- **Write your rules before you need them.** See [building-an-investment-policy-statement.md](building-an-investment-policy-statement.md).

## Key takeaways

- Investors reliably earn less than the funds they hold; Morningstar's latest estimate is about 1.2 points a year, roughly 12% of returns.
- The gap is caused by timing — buying after gains and selling after losses — not by fund quality.
- Gaps are widest in volatile, narrow, exciting funds and narrowest in diversified, automated ones.
- Dalbar's larger estimates are methodologically contested, but every study agrees on direction and pattern.
- Over 30 years, a one-point gap removes more than a fifth of the final portfolio; larger gaps remove half.
- Automation, boring holdings, and deliberate friction close the gap more reliably than willpower.
- Track your own money-weighted return annually so you can see whether you are the problem.
