# Cognitive Biases Field Guide

**In one sentence:** Your brain runs on shortcuts that were excellent for surviving on the savannah and terrible for managing a portfolio; this guide catalogs the thirty most expensive ones, how each shows up in investing, and the specific habit that neutralizes it.

## How to use this guide

Each entry has four parts: what the bias is, how it shows up when you invest, a real example, and a countermeasure. Read it once end to end, then come back to it whenever you feel a strong urge to act — the urge is usually one of these in disguise. Biases cluster into families: **how we feel about losses**, **how we judge our own ability**, **how we weigh information**, **how we follow other people**, and **how we reason about chance**. The countermeasures repeat on purpose: writing things down, waiting, automating, and asking "what would I have to see to be wrong?" defeat most of them.

## Part 1: Losses and ownership

### 1. Loss aversion
- **Definition:** Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equal gains feel good (Kahneman and Tversky).
- **In investing:** Refusing to accept a loss, over-holding cash, panic at drawdowns that are historically normal.
- **Example:** Investors who pulled out in March 2020 to "stop the bleeding" missed a 70%+ rebound within a year.
- **Countermeasure:** Decide your allocation in calm times and write it down; check the portfolio less often (see [prospect-theory-and-loss-aversion.md](prospect-theory-and-loss-aversion.md)).

### 2. Disposition effect
- **Definition:** The tendency to sell winners too early and hold losers too long.
- **In investing:** "I'll sell once it gets back to what I paid." Your purchase price is irrelevant to the stock's future.
- **Example:** Odean's study of 10,000 brokerage accounts found investors realized gains far more readily than losses, and the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept.
- **Countermeasure:** Ask "would I buy this today at this price?" If no, sell regardless of your cost basis. Tax-loss harvesting turns selling losers into a reward.

### 3. Sunk cost fallacy
- **Definition:** Letting money already spent (and unrecoverable) drive future decisions.
- **In investing:** Adding to a failing position "to average down" because you have already put so much in; refusing to abandon a strategy you spent years learning.
- **Example:** Averaging down on Enron, Lehman, or any bankrupt company all the way to zero.
- **Countermeasure:** Evaluate every position as if you inherited it this morning with no history.

### 4. Endowment effect
- **Definition:** We value things more simply because we own them.
- **In investing:** Demanding a higher price to sell a stock than you would pay to buy it; inherited holdings you would never choose becoming untouchable.
- **Example:** Employees holding concentrated company stock through a collapse because "it's ours."
- **Countermeasure:** The "blank slate" test: if your portfolio were all cash today, would you buy exactly this? Anything you would not buy, sell.

### 5. Status quo bias
- **Definition:** Preferring the current state simply because it is current.
- **In investing:** Never changing a 401(k) default; leaving cash uninvested for years; never rebalancing.
- **Example:** Retirement-plan participants left in money-market default funds for decades before auto-enrollment reforms.
- **Countermeasure:** Use inertia in your favor — set automatic contributions and automatic rebalancing so the default is the right thing.

### 6. Regret aversion
- **Definition:** Avoiding decisions because you fear feeling regret if they turn out badly.
- **In investing:** Sitting in cash waiting for "the right time"; refusing to sell a loser because selling makes the mistake official.
- **Example:** Investors who sat out the 2009–2020 bull market waiting for a "real" bottom.
- **Countermeasure:** Reframe: regret from inaction is just as real. Pre-commit to a schedule (dollar-cost averaging) so no single decision carries the regret.

### 7. Mental accounting
- **Definition:** Treating money differently depending on where it came from or what jar it sits in (Richard Thaler).
- **In investing:** Gambling with "house money" after a win; keeping a low-interest savings account while carrying high-interest debt; treating a bonus as play money.
- **Example:** Taking wild bets with tax refunds or lottery-style profits that would never be taken with salary.
- **Countermeasure:** Money is fungible. Set one written allocation for the whole net worth and let every dollar obey it.

## Part 2: Overconfidence and ego

### 8. Overconfidence
- **Definition:** Believing your knowledge, forecasts, and control are better than they are.
- **In investing:** Excessive trading, concentrated positions, ignoring diversification.
- **Example:** Barber and Odean found the most active traders earned about a third less than the market; men traded 45% more than women and did worse.
- **Countermeasure:** Keep a decision journal with explicit probabilities and score it. Cap any single position at a fixed share of the portfolio.

### 9. Illusion of control
- **Definition:** Feeling that effort or involvement gives you influence over random outcomes.
- **In investing:** Believing that watching the screen, trading more, or reading more news improves results.
- **Example:** Day-traders who feel skilled in a bull market when a random basket would have done as well.
- **Countermeasure:** Benchmark everything against a simple index fund. If the effort is not beating it after fees and taxes, stop the effort.

### 10. Hindsight bias
- **Definition:** After an outcome, believing you "knew it all along."
- **In investing:** Thinking 2008 or the dot-com crash was obvious in advance, which breeds false confidence about predicting the next one.
- **Example:** The countless commentators who "predicted" 2008 but whose actual portfolios were long stocks going in.
- **Countermeasure:** A decision journal written *before* outcomes is the only cure; your own handwriting cannot be argued with.

### 11. Self-attribution bias
- **Definition:** Crediting successes to skill and blaming failures on bad luck.
- **In investing:** A stock that rises "proves" your thesis; one that falls was "manipulated" or "unlucky."
- **Example:** 2020–2021 retail traders who attributed a once-in-a-generation liquidity wave to their own stock-picking.
- **Countermeasure:** For each outcome, write down what portion was process and what portion was luck. Mauboussin's rule: judge the process, not the result.

### 12. Outcome bias / resulting
- **Definition:** Judging a decision by how it turned out rather than by whether it was sound when made (Annie Duke calls this "resulting").
- **In investing:** Concluding that a leveraged crypto bet was a "good decision" because it paid off, or that diversification was a mistake because one holding lagged.
- **Example:** Buying a lottery ticket that wins is still a bad bet.
- **Countermeasure:** Separate decision quality from outcome quality in your journal. Good decisions with bad outcomes should be repeated.

### 13. Confirmation bias
- **Definition:** Seeking and remembering information that supports what you already believe.
- **In investing:** Reading only bullish analysis of stocks you own; following only pundits who agree with you.
- **Example:** Bull-market forums where any bear is banned, and the reverse.
- **Countermeasure:** Before every purchase, write the strongest bear case yourself. Follow the smartest person who disagrees with you.

## Part 3: How we weigh information

### 14. Anchoring
- **Definition:** Over-relying on the first number you see.
- **In investing:** Judging a stock as "cheap" because it is down 50% from its high; fixating on your purchase price or a round-number index level.
- **Example:** A stock that fell from $100 to $50 is not cheap if it is worth $20. Many dot-com stocks fell 90% *after* falling 50%.
- **Countermeasure:** Value assets on what they will earn, never on where they have been. Never let your cost basis enter a sell decision.

### 15. Recency bias
- **Definition:** Weighting recent events far more heavily than older ones when judging what is normal.
- **In investing:** Extrapolating the last three years of returns forever; assuming a calm market will stay calm or a crash will keep crashing.
- **Example:** Surveys in 2021 found individual investors expected annual returns near 17% because that is what they had recently seen.
- **Countermeasure:** Use base rates: long-run stock returns are roughly 7–10% nominal with a bear market every 5–6 years. Compare every expectation to the century-long record.

### 16. Availability heuristic
- **Definition:** Judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind.
- **In investing:** Overestimating crash risk after seeing crash coverage; overestimating the odds of finding the next Nvidia because Nvidia is in every headline.
- **Example:** Plane crashes feel likelier than car crashes; market crashes feel likelier than the far more common long, dull recoveries.
- **Countermeasure:** Replace vivid stories with counted data. Ask "how often has this actually happened per year?"

### 17. Framing effect
- **Definition:** The same facts producing different choices depending on how they are presented.
- **In investing:** "90% of the time this fund beats its peers" feels different from "it loses one year in ten." A "10% correction" sounds scarier than "prices back to where they were six months ago."
- **Example:** Kahneman and Tversky's disease experiment: identical outcomes framed as lives saved versus lives lost flipped most people's choice.
- **Countermeasure:** Reframe every decision at least two ways, including in dollars, percentages, and years, before acting.

### 18. Narrative fallacy
- **Definition:** Our need for a tidy story causes us to see cause and effect where there is only noise (Nassim Taleb).
- **In investing:** "Stocks fell today on inflation worries" — written after the fact by someone who has no idea why stocks fell.
- **Example:** Every daily market wrap-up ever published.
- **Countermeasure:** Prefer numbers to stories. When you hear a compelling narrative, ask what the base rate is for that kind of story being right.

### 19. Survivorship bias
- **Definition:** Drawing conclusions only from the examples that survived, ignoring the ones that vanished.
- **In investing:** Studying Buffett and Nvidia and concluding concentration works; ignoring the thousands of concentrated investors who went broke. Fund performance databases quietly drop dead funds.
- **Example:** Over a century, most individual stocks lost money over their lifetimes; the index's return came from a small minority of huge winners (Bessembinder's research).
- **Countermeasure:** Always ask "where are the failures?" Prefer strategies whose *whole* distribution you can see.

### 20. Authority bias
- **Definition:** Over-weighting the opinion of someone with credentials or status.
- **In investing:** Following celebrity investors, bank strategists, or CEOs without checking their track records.
- **Example:** Strategists' consensus year-end S&P 500 targets have missed by an average of about 14 points a year since 2000 and have never predicted a down year.
- **Countermeasure:** Ask for the track record, scored. If none exists, treat the forecast as entertainment.

### 21. Information bias
- **Definition:** Believing more information always produces better decisions.
- **In investing:** Reading constantly, checking prices hourly, believing the next data point will provide clarity.
- **Example:** Studies of horse-race handicappers found that more data raised confidence but not accuracy.
- **Countermeasure:** Decide in advance which three to five variables matter for your decision and ignore the rest (see [media-noise-and-information-diet.md](media-noise-and-information-diet.md)).

## Part 4: Following the crowd

### 22. Herding
- **Definition:** Copying what others do, on the assumption they know something.
- **In investing:** Piling into whatever is rising because everyone else is; selling because everyone else is.
- **Example:** Every bubble in [market-bubbles-and-manias.md](market-bubbles-and-manias.md), from tulips to meme stocks.
- **Countermeasure:** Fund flows are a contrarian indicator. When an asset is on every magazine cover, expected returns are lower, not higher.

### 23. FOMO (fear of missing out)
- **Definition:** Anxiety that others are getting rich without you, driving late entry into hot assets.
- **In investing:** Buying crypto in November 2021 or tech in March 2000 because a neighbor did.
- **Example:** Bitcoin peaked near $69,000 in late 2021 as retail inflows peaked, then fell about 75%.
- **Countermeasure:** Accept that you will miss most fads and that missing them is free. The only things you must not miss are the broad market's long-run return and your contribution schedule.

### 24. Social proof / bandwagon
- **Definition:** Treating popularity as evidence of correctness.
- **In investing:** Reddit, X, and TikTok "everybody's in this" dynamics.
- **Example:** GameStop, January 2021: many late buyers paid over $300 for shares worth a fraction of that months later.
- **Countermeasure:** Popularity is priced in. Ask: "if everyone already believes this, who is left to buy?"

### 25. Home bias / familiarity bias
- **Definition:** Overweighting your own country, industry, or employer because it feels known.
- **In investing:** US investors holding 80%+ US stocks; employees holding their employer's stock; tech workers overweighting tech.
- **Example:** Japanese investors concentrated in Japan in 1989 waited 34 years for the Nikkei to regain its high.
- **Countermeasure:** Hold a global, market-weighted portfolio by default. Cap employer stock at a small percentage; your salary is already a bet on that company.

## Part 5: Reasoning about chance

### 26. Gambler's fallacy
- **Definition:** Believing that a run of one outcome makes the opposite "due."
- **In investing:** "The market has been up five years, so it must fall next year." Markets have no memory in that sense; annual returns are close to independent.
- **Example:** Sitting out 2013, 2014, 2017, 2019, 2021, and 2024 because the previous year was strong.
- **Countermeasure:** Learn the actual base rates: the US market has risen in roughly three years of every four, regardless of the previous year.

### 27. Hot-hand fallacy
- **Definition:** The mirror image — believing a streak will continue because of "momentum" in skill that is actually luck.
- **In investing:** Buying the fund with the best three-year record; Morningstar and S&P's persistence studies show top-quartile funds rarely stay there.
- **Example:** ARK Innovation attracted its largest inflows right after its 2020 peak and then lost most of its value.
- **Countermeasure:** Evaluate managers on process and cost, not on recent returns. Judge any track record over at least a full market cycle.

### 28. Clustering illusion / pattern-seeking
- **Definition:** Seeing patterns in random data.
- **In investing:** Chart patterns, "January effects," calendar signals, and most technical indicators that do not survive out-of-sample testing.
- **Example:** The "Super Bowl indicator" was right for decades by coincidence and then was not.
- **Countermeasure:** Any pattern must have a plausible economic reason and must work in data it was not discovered in.

### 29. Base-rate neglect
- **Definition:** Ignoring how often something happens in general in favor of the specifics of the case in front of you.
- **In investing:** "This IPO is different." Most IPOs underperform the market over their first three years. Most concentrated bets fail. Most active funds lag their index over 15 years.
- **Example:** Every startup pitch deck claims it is the exception; the base rate for venture-backed companies returning capital is small.
- **Countermeasure:** Start every forecast from the reference class ("what happens to things like this?") and adjust only modestly (see [decision-making-systems.md](decision-making-systems.md)).

### 30. Action bias
- **Definition:** Feeling that doing something is better than doing nothing, especially under stress.
- **In investing:** Trading during crashes "to take control"; tinkering with allocations after every headline.
- **Example:** Soccer goalkeepers dive on nearly every penalty kick even though staying centered would save more; investors do the equivalent in bear markets.
- **Countermeasure:** Make "do nothing" the explicit default and require a written reason to override it. Fidelity's much-repeated (and possibly apocryphal) finding that its best accounts belonged to people who forgot they had them captures the point.

### 31. Optimism bias and planning fallacy
- **Definition:** Systematically underestimating costs, time, and the chance of bad outcomes.
- **In investing:** Retirement plans built on 12% returns and no bear markets; assuming you will "just work longer" if things go wrong.
- **Example:** Most large projects run over budget and time; most personal financial plans assume no job loss, illness, or crash.
- **Countermeasure:** Plan with a margin of safety: assume returns 2 points below history, a bear market in the first years of retirement, and a 30% chance of an unplanned expense.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

Notice how few distinct countermeasures there actually are:

1. **Write it down before you act** — a decision journal with probabilities kills hindsight, self-attribution, outcome bias, and overconfidence at once.
2. **Wait** — a mandatory delay (24–72 hours) between decision and execution defeats action bias, FOMO, recency, and herding.
3. **Automate** — standing contributions and calendar rebalancing defeat status quo bias, loss aversion, regret aversion, and the disposition effect by removing the decision entirely.
4. **Use base rates** — starting from "what usually happens?" defeats anchoring, availability, narrative fallacy, survivorship, and optimism.
5. **Invert** — "what would make me wrong?" defeats confirmation bias and authority bias.
6. **Look at less** — checking rarely and consuming less news starves nearly every bias of the raw material it needs.

## Key takeaways

- Biases are not personal failings; they are standard human equipment. Systems, not willpower, defend against them.
- The most expensive biases for investors are loss aversion, overconfidence, recency, and herding — the four behind almost every panic sale and bubble purchase.
- Your purchase price is irrelevant to a sell decision; anchoring on it drives the disposition effect.
- Judge decisions by process, not outcome; luck dominates short-run investing results.
- Vivid stories and recent events feel more probable than they are; base rates are the antidote.
- Popularity is already priced in; herding and FOMO buy high by construction.
- Six habits — write, wait, automate, base-rate, invert, look less — cover nearly all thirty biases.
