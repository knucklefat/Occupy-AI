# Decision-Making Systems

**In one sentence:** Good investors do not have better instincts than you; they have built systems — checklists, pre-mortems, journals, base rates, inversion, and automation — that catch bad instincts before money moves and that let them tell skill from luck afterward.

## Why systems beat judgment

Investing is a domain with a weak feedback loop: outcomes arrive slowly, are heavily contaminated by luck, and are easy to misremember. Under those conditions, experience alone does not produce expertise — it produces confidence. Psychologists Robin Hogarth and Daniel Kahneman called these "wicked" learning environments, as opposed to "kind" ones like chess where feedback is fast and clean. In a wicked environment, the only reliable way to improve is to impose structure on the decision itself. The tools below do that. None is complicated; all are routinely skipped.

## 1. Checklists

Surgeon Atul Gawande's *The Checklist Manifesto* documented how a simple pre-surgery checklist cut complications by more than a third in hospitals worldwide — not because surgeons lacked knowledge, but because under pressure even experts skip steps. Pilots have used checklists since the 1930s for the same reason.

Investor Mohnish Pabrai built his own after studying his and other investors' mistakes and found that nearly every loss came from a small number of recurring, foreseeable errors — excessive leverage, misjudged management, a business whose moat was shrinking, being wrong about the down-side. His checklist, reportedly over a hundred items, is run *before* any purchase; if an item is flagged, that does not forbid the investment, but it forces him to explicitly acknowledge the risk. Charlie Munger endorsed the approach for the same reason.

A useful checklist for an individual investor is short and mostly asks what could go wrong:

- Can I explain this investment and how it makes money in two sentences?
- What has to be true for this to work, and what is the evidence?
- What is the base rate for investments of this kind?
- How much can I lose, and can I afford that?
- Why is the seller selling? Who is on the other side and what do they know?
- Does this fit my written policy (allocation caps, allowed vehicles)?
- Am I buying because of a story, a tip, or recent performance?
- Have I written the bear case in my own words?
- Have I waited the required cooling-off period?

The value is not in the items; it is in being forced to stop and answer them.

## 2. Pre-mortems

Gary Klein's technique: before committing, imagine it is a year from now and the decision has failed badly. Now write the story of *why*. This is different from asking "what could go wrong?" — which invites vague, polite answers. Framing the failure as already having happened ("prospective hindsight") makes people about 30% better at identifying specific causes, because it gives them permission to be pessimistic and unlocks the same fluency that hindsight bias normally wastes on the past.

For a portfolio: "It is 2029 and this allocation has been a disaster. What happened?" The answers — a concentrated bet failed, I sold in a panic, I used leverage, my income fell at the same time as the market — become the items to address today.

## 3. Decision journals

Farnam Street's Shane Parrish and poker champion Annie Duke both advocate the same tool: at the moment of decision, write down the situation, what you expect to happen, your probability estimate, the alternatives you rejected and why, and how you feel physically and emotionally. Then, later, record what happened and score it.

The journal defeats three biases at once. **Hindsight bias**: you cannot claim you "knew it" when your own handwriting says you gave it 40%. **Self-attribution**: the record shows whether your wins came from the reasons you predicted. **Outcome bias**: it separates the decision from the result, which brings us to the next tool.

A minimal entry:

> Date / state of mind / decision / why / what I expect (with probability) / what would prove me wrong / when I will review this.

## 4. Resulting and the skill-luck continuum

Duke's term **resulting** means judging a decision by its outcome. It is the natural mistake in any activity with luck in it, and investing has more luck in it than almost anything else people do deliberately.

Michael Mauboussin's *The Success Equation* places activities on a continuum from pure skill (chess) to pure luck (roulette). The test: can you lose on purpose? A chess player can; a roulette player cannot; a stock picker mostly cannot in the short run. Investing sits close to the luck end over short periods and drifts toward skill only over long ones.

Two consequences. First, the **paradox of skill**: as all participants become more skilled, luck matters *more* in deciding outcomes, because the skill differences shrink. This is why professional fund managers, who are uniformly skilled, mostly fail to beat the index — not because they lack skill but because they all have it. Second, in a luck-heavy domain, **process is the only thing you can evaluate**. A good decision with a bad outcome should be repeated; a bad decision with a good outcome should not. Duke's four-box grid — good/bad decision by good/bad outcome — belongs on every journal page.

## 5. Base rates and reference-class forecasting

Kahneman's "outside view": before forecasting from the specifics of your case (the inside view), ask what typically happens to things in this category. What fraction of IPOs beat the market over three years? (Most do not.) What fraction of active funds beat their index over 15 years? (Roughly one in ten, per S&P's SPIVA scorecards.) What fraction of individual stocks beat Treasury bills over their lifetimes? (Under half, per Bessembinder.) How far have 60/40 portfolios fallen in bad bear markets? (Around 30%.)

Start with the base rate, then adjust — modestly — for what is genuinely specific. Most people do the reverse: they start from the story and never consult the base rate at all. This is base-rate neglect, and it explains most overpaying.

## 6. Inversion

Munger, borrowing from mathematician Carl Jacobi: "Invert, always invert." Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how to guarantee failure, then avoid that. How would I reliably destroy my wealth? Leverage, concentration, trading on emotion, high fees, chasing performance, selling in crashes, trusting forecasters. The list is short, well-known, and almost entirely avoidable — and avoiding it does most of the work of investing well.

Inversion also applies to information: instead of seeking evidence that you are right, seek what would prove you wrong and go look for it.

## 7. Expected-value thinking

Every decision is a bet with a distribution of outcomes. Expected value is the sum of each outcome times its probability. Thinking this way does two useful things. It forces you to state probabilities (which exposes overconfidence when you score them later). And it separates a good bet from a good outcome: a bet with a 60% chance of gaining $100 and a 40% chance of losing $100 is worth taking every time, even though it will lose four times in ten.

For investors the practical form is: "What is the range of outcomes over my horizon, how likely is each, and can I survive the bad tail?" If the bad tail is ruin, the expected value is irrelevant — ruin ends the game. Survival first, expected value second.

## 8. Automation: the decision you never make

The most effective decision system is the one that removes the decision. Every study in [the-behavior-gap.md](the-behavior-gap.md) points the same way: investors who automate contributions and rebalancing earn closer to their funds' returns than investors who decide each time. Retirement plans with automatic enrollment and automatic escalation (Thaler and Benartzi's "Save More Tomorrow") raised savings rates dramatically without anyone exercising willpower.

Automate: contributions (monthly, on payday), allocation (a target-date or balanced fund, or a fixed split), rebalancing (calendar or threshold), and tax-loss harvesting where available. What remains — the annual review and the rare life-event revision — is small enough to handle with a checklist and a journal.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- Write a ten-item pre-purchase checklist this week and keep it with your [investment policy statement](building-an-investment-policy-statement.md). Run it on every position you already own.
- Do a pre-mortem on your current portfolio: "It is five years from now and this has gone badly. Why?"
- Start a decision journal. Even three entries a year — the annual review plus any discretionary trades — will change how you see yourself.
- Add a resulting grid to each journal review: which box did this decision land in?
- Before any forecast, write the base rate first. If you do not know it, look it up before proceeding.
- Keep an inversion list — the ten ways to destroy wealth — and check every proposed action against it.
- Automate everything that can be automated. The decisions left over are the only ones worth your attention.

## Key takeaways

- Investing is a "wicked" learning environment: slow, noisy feedback means experience breeds confidence, not skill. Structure substitutes for the missing feedback.
- Checklists work because experts skip steps under pressure, not because they lack knowledge.
- Pre-mortems ("it failed — why?") surface specific risks that "what could go wrong?" misses.
- A decision journal with probabilities is the only real defense against hindsight and self-attribution bias.
- Judge process, not outcomes; short-run investing results are mostly luck, and the paradox of skill makes them more so.
- Start every forecast from the base rate for that class of thing; adjust modestly.
- Invert: avoiding the short list of reliable wealth-destroyers does most of the work.
- Automation removes the decision entirely, which is the highest form of decision-making.
