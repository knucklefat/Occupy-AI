# Prospect Theory and Loss Aversion

**In one sentence:** Kahneman and Tversky showed that people judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point rather than as final wealth, that losses hurt about twice as much as gains please, and that these two facts explain why investors sell winners, cling to losers, and suffer more the more often they look.

## The model in plain language

Classical economics assumed people evaluate risky choices by their effect on total wealth and weigh each outcome by its true probability. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's 1979 paper "Prospect Theory" showed, with simple experiments, that real people do something different. Three findings matter most for investors.

### 1. You judge relative to a reference point

Nobody feels "I have $412,000." They feel "I'm up $12,000 from January" or "I'm down $30,000 from the peak." The reference point is usually your purchase price, a recent high, or what a neighbor has. Because the reference point is arbitrary, the same portfolio can feel like a triumph or a disaster depending on what you compare it with — and the feeling, not the fact, drives your behavior.

### 2. Losses loom larger than gains

The value function is steeper for losses than for gains. In experiments, most people refuse a coin flip that pays $150 on heads and costs $100 on tails, even though its expected value is positive. To accept a 50/50 bet, people typically need the potential gain to be roughly twice the potential loss. This ratio — often quoted as about 2 to 2.5 — is called the **loss-aversion coefficient**. It varies by person and by situation, but the asymmetry is one of the most replicated results in psychology.

### 3. Diminishing sensitivity on both sides

Going from $0 to $100 feels bigger than going from $1,000 to $1,100. The same applies to losses: the first $1,000 lost hurts more than the tenth. This produces a curious pattern: people are **risk-averse for gains** (they take the sure $500 rather than a 50% shot at $1,000) but **risk-seeking for losses** (they gamble to avoid a sure loss of $500). An investor who is behind will take bigger risks to get back to even — the same psychology as the gambler doubling down.

A fourth element, **probability weighting**, says we overweight small probabilities (which is why lottery tickets and insurance both sell) and underweight moderate-to-high ones. It explains the appeal of lottery-like stocks and options.

Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize in economics largely for this work; Tversky had died in 1996.

## Why it matters: three portfolio consequences

### The disposition effect

Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman named it in 1985; Terrance Odean documented it in 1998 with the trading records of 10,000 brokerage accounts. Investors were far more likely to sell a stock showing a gain than one showing a loss — in Odean's data, the proportion of gains realized was about 50% higher than the proportion of losses realized. Worse, the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept over the following year. The one exception was December, when tax motives temporarily overrode the psychology.

Prospect theory explains it neatly. A stock above your purchase price sits in the "gain" region, where you are risk-averse: lock it in. A stock below sits in the "loss" region, where you are risk-seeking: hold on, it might come back, and selling would convert a paper loss into a real one — a "realized" loss that finally hurts.

The cost is double. You cut the compounding of your best ideas short, and you keep capital tied up in your worst ones. Odean estimated the effect cost investors several percentage points a year on the affected trades, before taxes made it worse (realizing gains creates a tax bill; realizing losses creates a deduction — the disposition effect gets even this backward).

### Break-even-itis and doubling down

Because sensitivity to losses diminishes, an investor already down 40% barely feels the next 10%. That is why people ride stocks to zero. It is also why losing traders increase position sizes — the desperate gamble to get back to the reference point. Casinos and brokerages both profit from this.

### Myopic loss aversion: the cost of looking

In 1995 Shlomo Benartzi and Richard Thaler combined loss aversion with **mental accounting** — the habit of closing the books and evaluating results at intervals — to answer a long-standing puzzle: why have stocks historically returned so much more than bonds (the "equity premium"), far more than reasonable risk aversion would justify?

Their answer: investors are **myopically loss-averse**. They feel each loss about twice as strongly as each gain, *and* they check their results frequently. Over a single day, stocks fall roughly 47% of the time. Over a year, about 25–30% of the time. Over ten years, rarely. An investor who looks daily experiences a near-endless stream of painful losses, each weighted double. An investor who looks once a decade experiences almost none.

Benartzi and Thaler calculated that the historical equity premium is exactly what you would expect if the typical investor evaluated their portfolio about **once a year**. Their model implied that an investor who could bring themselves to evaluate every ten or twenty years would happily hold far more stocks — and would earn the premium others pay for their impatience.

The practical translation: the *risk* of stocks does not change with how often you check, but the *felt pain* does, and felt pain drives selling. Checking frequency is a risk factor.

## How often should you look?

Consider the rough odds of seeing a loss in a broadly diversified stock portfolio:

| Holding period | Approximate chance the period ends down |
|---|---|
| One day | ~46–47% |
| One month | ~37–40% |
| One year | ~25–30% |
| Five years | ~10–12% |
| Ten years | ~5% |
| Twenty years | ~0% (historically, for US stocks) |

Weight each loss you see at twice its size and it becomes obvious why the daily checker feels like they are losing money most of the time in a market that goes up most of the time. Benartzi and Thaler's own findings, plus later experiments (Gneezy and Potters, Thaler et al.), consistently show that people given less frequent feedback choose riskier and higher-returning allocations and stick with them.

Practical guidance:

- **Accumulators** (adding money monthly, decades from needing it): quarterly or less. Many successful investors look once a year, at rebalancing time.
- **Near-retirees**: quarterly, plus a scheduled annual review of the withdrawal plan.
- **Anyone during a crash**: checking more will not change what you should do, which is written in your [investment policy statement](building-an-investment-policy-statement.md). It will only raise the chance you violate it.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- **Change the reference point.** Compare against your plan, not the peak. A portfolio down 20% from a high that is still ahead of its ten-year plan is a success. Record the plan's target value for each year and compare against that instead.
- **Reframe losses as the price of admission.** A 10% dip is not a loss; it is the periodic fee stocks charge for returning more than cash. Morgan Housel's framing: volatility is a fee, not a fine.
- **Sell decisions ignore cost basis.** Write it into your rules: "Purchase price is not an input to any sell decision." The only question is whether you would buy at today's price.
- **Harvest losses deliberately.** Tax-loss harvesting converts the most painful action (realizing a loss) into a cash reward, which trains the habit.
- **Reduce evaluation frequency.** Remove portfolio apps from your phone. Turn off balance emails. Let statements pile up. Pick a review date and keep it.
- **Look at the whole, not the parts.** Loss aversion attaches to each holding separately (narrow framing). Evaluate the total portfolio return only; individual line items are noise.
- **Pre-commit.** Automatic contributions and rebalancing execute regardless of how the last month felt. The best defense against the pain of losses is never being asked whether to act on it.
- **Use the loss region wisely.** Since you are risk-seeking when down, that is precisely when to be most suspicious of any urge to make a "bold" move.

## Key takeaways

- People judge gains and losses from a reference point, not from total wealth, and the reference point is arbitrary and manipulable.
- Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please — the loss-aversion coefficient of about 2.
- We are risk-averse when ahead and risk-seeking when behind, which is why investors lock in small wins and gamble on losers.
- The disposition effect — selling winners, holding losers — is well documented and costs real return and real tax efficiency.
- Myopic loss aversion means the more often you look, the more losses you experience, the more pain you feel, and the more likely you are to sell.
- The equity premium is consistent with investors evaluating about once a year; less frequent evaluation is a source of return.
- Cost basis is irrelevant to whether to hold; the only question is whether you would buy today.
