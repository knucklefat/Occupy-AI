# Market Bubbles and Manias

**In one sentence:** Every bubble in four centuries has followed the same script — a genuine innovation, easy money, a contagious story, a wave of new participants convinced "this time is different," and then a collapse that wipes out the late arrivals — and your defense is not predicting the top but refusing to depend on it.

## A short history of losing everything

| Bubble | Peak | What people believed | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dutch tulips** | Feb 1637 | Rare bulbs were a store of wealth that could only appreciate; futures contracts let anyone speculate | Single bulbs traded for the price of a house; prices collapsed over weeks when buyers stopped showing up at auctions |
| **South Sea Company** | Aug 1720 | A government-backed monopoly on trade with South America would generate limitless profits | Shares rose roughly eightfold in months, then fell back to their starting level by year end; Isaac Newton reportedly lost a fortune |
| **US stocks, 1929** | Sep 1929 | A "new era" of permanent prosperity; buying on 10% margin was prudent | The Dow fell ~89% from 381 to 41 by July 1932; it did not regain its 1929 high until 1954 |
| **Nifty Fifty** | 1972 | "One-decision stocks" (Polaroid, Xerox, Avon, Disney) could be bought at any price because their growth was certain | Many traded at 50–90 times earnings; several fell 70–90% in 1973–74. The businesses mostly survived; the prices did not |
| **Japan, 1989** | Dec 1989 | Japanese management, land, and finance were a superior system; land under the Imperial Palace was "worth more than California" | The Nikkei peaked at 38,916 and did not regain that level until February 2024 — 34 years later |
| **Dot-com** | Mar 2000 | The internet changes everything; profits are irrelevant; "eyeballs" and "clicks" are the new metrics | The Nasdaq fell ~78% from 5,048 to about 1,114 by October 2002 and took 15 years to recover. The internet did change everything |
| **US housing / credit** | 2006 | House prices never fall nationally; securitization has eliminated risk; ratings agencies are reliable | National home prices fell about a quarter; the S&P 500 fell 57%; Lehman, Bear Stearns, and hundreds of lenders vanished |
| **2021 everything bubble** | Nov 2021 | Stimulus, zero rates, and commission-free apps made retail investors "unstoppable"; SPACs, meme stocks, NFTs, and crypto were the new frontier | Bitcoin fell ~77% from $69,000; ~600 SPACs went public in 2021 and most later traded below their $10 issue price; GameStop fell more than 80% from its January 2021 spike; ARK Innovation fell ~80% |

Notice two things. First, the underlying innovation was often real — the internet, the railroad, securitization, even Japanese manufacturing. Bubbles form *around* real things. Second, the businesses frequently survived; it was the prices, and the people who paid them, that did not.

## The anatomy of a bubble

Economist Hyman Minsky and historian Charles Kindleberger (in *Manias, Panics, and Crashes*) described a five-stage pattern that fits every case above.

1. **Displacement.** Something genuinely new appears — a technology, a policy change, a war ending, interest rates falling — that changes the profit outlook for some sector.
2. **Boom.** Prices rise; credit expands; early investors are rewarded, which attracts more investors. Media coverage begins. So far, this is rational.
3. **Euphoria.** Prices detach from any plausible estimate of value. Leverage rises. Newcomers with no experience enter in large numbers; "this time is different" becomes the consensus explanation for why old valuation rules no longer apply. Kindleberger's tell: there is nothing so disturbing to one's well-being as watching a friend get rich.
4. **Profit-taking.** Insiders, founders, and the most sophisticated players begin selling to the crowd. Warning signs (a failed IPO, a rate hike, a fraud exposed) appear but are dismissed.
5. **Panic.** Prices fall; leveraged buyers are forced to sell; the selling drives prices lower; credit dries up; the story flips overnight from "unlimited upside" to "worthless." Revulsion follows: the asset class is shunned for years, often when it is finally cheap.

Minsky's contribution was to show that this cycle is *endogenous* — stability itself breeds instability, because long calm periods encourage borrowers and lenders to take on more leverage until the system becomes fragile. Bubbles do not need an external shock; they need only time and confidence.

## Shiller: bubbles are stories that go viral

Robert Shiller, who won the 2013 Nobel Prize partly for showing that markets swing far more than fundamentals justify, argues in *Irrational Exuberance* and *Narrative Economics* that a bubble is best understood as an **epidemic of a story**. The story spreads through conversation, media, and social proof the way a virus spreads through contact. It usually has these features:

- A **plausible kernel of truth** (the internet really is transformative).
- A **moral or identity component** (early adopters are visionaries; skeptics are dinosaurs).
- A **feedback loop**: rising prices are taken as proof the story is true, which attracts more believers, which raises prices.
- **Contagion through new participants**: every bubble is marked by people who have never invested before opening accounts. In 1929 it was shoeshine boys and elevator operators; in 1999 it was day-traders quitting jobs; in 2021 it was Robinhood users trading on stimulus checks.

Shiller's price-to-earnings ratio adjusted for ten-year average earnings (the "CAPE") does not time bubbles, but it does reliably indicate when future returns are likely to be poor. It exceeded 40 in 2000 and was above 35 in late 2021 — both preceding sharp drawdowns.

## How to recognize one

No signal is decisive, but a cluster of these should raise your guard:

- **Valuation extremes**: CAPE above roughly 30; companies with no profits valued in the tens of billions; price-to-sales multiples that require decades of perfect growth.
- **Novel valuation metrics** invented to justify prices (eyeballs, "total addressable market," token velocity).
- **Leverage everywhere**: margin debt at records; retail options volume exploding; "buy now, pay later" for stocks.
- **Issuance floods**: IPOs, SPACs, and secondary offerings surge because insiders are cashing out. Six hundred SPACs in one year was a supply signal, not a demand signal.
- **New-participant surges**: record account openings, first-timers boasting about returns, your dentist offering stock tips.
- **Celebrity and media saturation**: the asset on magazine covers; athletes and pop stars endorsing it; professionals who question it treated as fools.
- **"This time is different"** articulated explicitly, often by people whose income depends on the story continuing.
- **Fraud surfacing at the edges**: bubbles are the natural habitat of Ponzi schemes and accounting games, which are exposed only when the money stops.

## What to do

The honest answer is that you cannot time the top — bubbles routinely go further and last longer than any rational analysis predicts. Being early and being wrong feel identical for years. What you can do:

- **Do not need to be right.** Size any position in a "story" asset so that its total loss would not change your life. Then you can be wrong about the bubble in either direction.
- **Rebalance mechanically.** If tech goes from 25% to 40% of your portfolio because of a rally, a rule-based rebalance sells some of it without requiring you to call a top. Rebalancing is a built-in bubble harvester.
- **Never use leverage in a mania.** Leverage converts being *early* into being *wrong*. Almost every bubble fortune lost was lost by someone who was right about the long run but borrowed against the short run.
- **Ignore the newcomers' returns.** Their gains are real but were bought with risk they do not understand. You are not competing with them.
- **Watch supply.** When insiders sell and issuers rush to market, they are telling you something about what they think the asset is worth.
- **Prepare for revulsion.** The best time to buy the innovation is usually two to three years after the collapse, when the survivors are cheap and no one wants them. Amazon fell over 90% in 2000–2001.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- Write in your investment policy statement a maximum weight for any single sector, theme, or speculative asset, and a rebalancing rule that enforces it.
- Keep a "mania checklist" (the recognition signs above) and review it annually. When four or more are present, stop adding to that area.
- Maintain a permanent core (broad, diversified, boring) that is never touched by bubble decisions; confine speculation to a small, separate "sandbox" account.
- Refuse leverage entirely, or cap it at a level where a 50% decline cannot force a sale.
- When you feel the urge to buy something because a friend got rich, wait 30 days and re-read this file.

## Key takeaways

- Bubbles are as old as markets and follow a recognizable pattern: displacement, boom, euphoria, profit-taking, panic.
- They form around real innovations; the technology usually survives, the late buyers' capital does not.
- Stability breeds instability (Minsky): long calm periods invite the leverage that makes the crash.
- A bubble is a viral story (Shiller); watch for contagion signs — new participants, novel metrics, media saturation, issuance floods.
- No one reliably times the top; being early looks like being wrong for years.
- Your defense is structural: position size, mechanical rebalancing, no leverage, and a core portfolio insulated from speculation.
- The best entry into the underlying innovation usually comes in the revulsion phase, years after the peak.
