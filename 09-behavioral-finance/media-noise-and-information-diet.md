# Media Noise and the Information Diet

**In one sentence:** Almost everything published about markets is produced to capture attention rather than to improve decisions, the forecasters it features have track records no better than chance, and the single cheapest improvement most investors can make is to consume far less of it, far less often.

## Why financial news hurts returns

Financial media is not in the business of making you money. It is in the business of holding your attention long enough to sell it to advertisers. That is not a moral criticism; it is a description of the incentives, and the incentives produce predictable content:

- **Urgency without importance.** Every day must have a story, so every day's random wiggle is assigned a cause ("stocks fall on rate fears") and a call to action. Most days nothing happened. Over a year, the daily move of the S&P 500 exceeds 2% roughly one day in twenty; the rest is noise that gets reported as signal.
- **Fear sells better than calm.** Viewership and clicks spike during declines. A network that told you "nothing to see, hold your funds" would lose its audience to the one running "MARKETS IN TURMOIL" banners. Notice that the turmoil banner has appeared before nearly every great buying opportunity of the last thirty years.
- **Attention drives trading.** Barber and Odean ("All That Glitters," 2008) showed that individual investors are net buyers of whatever is in the news — stocks with unusual volume, big moves, or headlines. Attention-driven buying underperforms. The news does not just inform your trades; it *causes* them, and the trades it causes are bad.
- **Confidence is rewarded; accuracy is not.** Guests are invited back for being interesting and certain. No one tracks whether they were right. Philip Tetlock found that the more famous the expert, the *worse* the forecasting record — because fame comes from bold, simple, confident claims, which are exactly the claims most likely to be wrong.
- **More information raises confidence, not accuracy.** In a classic study, horse-race handicappers given more data points became much more confident but not more accurate. Investors reading their fifth article on a company feel they understand it; they mostly do not.

The result is a feedback loop. News creates emotion, emotion creates trading, trading creates the behavior gap documented in [the-behavior-gap.md](the-behavior-gap.md).

## The forecasting record

### Wall Street strategists

Every December, the major banks publish year-end targets for the S&P 500. Bespoke Investment Group tallied the consensus against reality for 2000–2024:

- The average miss was about **14 percentage points** per year.
- The consensus predicted a **gain in all 25 years**. The market fell in 7 of them. Strategists have never, as a group, forecast a down year.
- Recent examples: for 2022 the consensus called for roughly +4%; the market fell about 19%. For 2023 it called for +6%; the market rose 24%. For 2024 it called for +2%; the market rose 23%.

Targets also get revised through the year to follow the market, so the December forecast and the "final" forecast are different things. In other words, the number is a marketing product, not a prediction.

### Pundits and experts generally

Tetlock's twenty-year study of expert political and economic forecasting (*Expert Political Judgment*, 2005) collected roughly 28,000 predictions from 284 experts. The aggregate result: experts were barely more accurate than simple extrapolation and only modestly better than chance — the origin of the "dart-throwing chimp" line. Two findings matter for investors:

1. **Hedgehogs versus foxes.** Experts who organized the world around one big idea (hedgehogs) forecast worse than experts who drew on many small ideas and updated constantly (foxes). Financial media overwhelmingly features hedgehogs — the permabear, the permabull, the gold guy — because a single big idea is a better television format.
2. **Forecasting *is* a skill, just not the one on TV.** Tetlock's later Good Judgment Project found "superforecasters" who beat intelligence analysts with access to classified information by about 30%. Their traits: probabilistic thinking, constant small updates, willingness to say "I don't know," and rigorous scoring of their own past calls. None of these traits gets you invited on air.

### Economists and recessions

The International Monetary Fund and others have documented that professional forecasters almost never predict recessions in advance; of the many recessions across countries in recent decades, very few were forecast a year ahead. Interest-rate forecasts have a similarly dismal record — the consensus expected rates to rise for most of the 2010s while they stayed near zero, then missed the 2022 surge.

### Magazine covers

The "magazine cover indicator" is a joke with a real point. Business Week's 1979 cover "The Death of Equities" preceded the greatest bull market in history. Covers celebrating an asset or a CEO tend to mark peaks, because a story reaches the cover only when it is already universally believed — and universally owned.

## Building a low-noise information diet

The goal is not ignorance; it is to consume information at the frequency at which it can actually change a good decision. For a long-term investor, that frequency is low.

**Cut:**
- Daily and intra-day market coverage of any kind, including price tickers and push notifications.
- Forecasts, targets, and "outlooks" — annual, quarterly, or otherwise.
- Anyone who is not scored. If there is no track record, it is entertainment.
- Social media investment content, especially anything featuring screenshots of gains.
- Portfolio apps on your phone.

**Keep, at low frequency:**
- **Primary sources** for anything you own: annual reports, fund fact sheets, the actual text of a policy change.
- **Long-form, slow content**: books; a few thoughtful writers who publish weekly or less (Morgan Housel, Howard Marks's memos, Ben Carlson, Jason Zweig, the Morningstar and Vanguard research teams).
- **Base-rate data**: long-run return tables, drawdown histories, valuation series. This is the reference material that makes news legible.
- **Your own records**: your investment policy statement, your decision journal, your annual review.

**Frequency guidance:**

| Activity | Suggested frequency |
|---|---|
| Check portfolio balance | Quarterly (monthly at most); weekly during a crash if you must |
| Read market news | Never on purpose; skim a weekly summary if you enjoy it |
| Read about companies or funds you own | At annual report time, plus any material event |
| Read forecasts and year-end targets | Never |
| Rebalance and review the plan | Semi-annually or annually, on fixed dates |
| Read investing books and long-form thinking | Continuously; this is where actual improvement comes from |

A useful test for any piece of content: *if this is true, what would I do differently?* If the answer is "nothing," because your plan already accounts for it, you have finished reading. The vast majority of financial content fails this test for a diversified long-term investor.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- Delete brokerage and market apps from your phone; access accounts on a computer, on scheduled dates.
- Turn off all price alerts and financial push notifications.
- Unfollow or mute every account that posts predictions, screenshots, or daily market commentary.
- Replace the time with one long-form source per week and one investing book per quarter.
- Keep a "forecast log": whenever you cannot resist noting a prediction you saw, write it down with the date and check it a year later. Nothing builds skepticism faster than a scored record.
- Write in your IPS that forecasts and headlines are not valid triggers for any action.
- If you enjoy the news as entertainment, keep it — but keep it in the same mental category as sports coverage, with no connection to your money.

## Key takeaways

- Financial media optimizes for attention, which means urgency, fear, and confident narrative — none of which improves decisions.
- Attention causes trading, and attention-driven trades underperform (Barber and Odean).
- Strategists' year-end targets have missed by about 14 points a year and have never called a down year; treat them as marketing.
- Tetlock: experts barely beat chance; fame predicts worse accuracy; hedgehogs lose to foxes.
- Real forecasting skill exists but looks nothing like TV — probabilistic, humble, constantly updated, and scored.
- Consume information at the frequency at which it could change a good decision: for a long-term investor, that is rarely.
- The test for any content: "if true, what would I do differently?" Usually nothing.
