# Handling Market Crashes

**In one sentence:** Crashes are a recurring, survivable feature of owning stocks — the US market has fallen 20% or more about once every six years and has recovered every time — so the job is not to avoid them but to have decided, before one arrives, exactly what you will do and not do.

## What crashes have actually looked like

The table below uses the S&P 500 (price only, not counting dividends, in nominal dollars, on a daily-closing basis; figures rounded). Counting reinvested dividends shortens every recovery; adjusting for inflation lengthens the older ones (except 1929–32, when deflation helped). The same figures appear in [risk-and-return.md](../01-foundations/risk-and-return.md).

| Crash | Peak to trough | Decline | How long the fall took | Time to regain prior peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1929 Great Depression** | Sep 1929 – Jun 1932 | about −86% | ~34 months | ~25 years to 1954 (roughly 15 years with dividends reinvested) |
| **1973–74 oil shock / stagflation** | Jan 1973 – Oct 1974 | about −48% | ~21 months | ~7.5 years (about 3.5 with dividends; longer after inflation) |
| **1987 Black Monday** | Aug 1987 – Dec 1987 | about −34% (−20% in a single day, Oct 19) | ~3 months | ~2 years |
| **2000–02 dot-com bust** | Mar 2000 – Oct 2002 | about −49% | ~31 months | ~7 years, to 2007 — then it fell again |
| **2007–09 financial crisis** | Oct 2007 – Mar 2009 | about −57% | ~17 months | ~5.5 years to 2013 (about 4 with dividends) |
| **2020 pandemic** | Feb 19 – Mar 23, 2020 | about −34% | 33 days | ~6 months |
| **2022 inflation / rate shock** | Jan 3 – Oct 12, 2022 | about −25% | ~9 months | ~2 years |

Some things to notice:

- **Frequency.** Since 1929 the S&P 500 has had roughly two dozen declines of 20% or more (counting intra-year drawdowns) — about one every five to six years. Declines of 10% happen in most years. If you invest for 40 years you should expect six to eight bear markets, and a couple of them will cut your portfolio roughly in half.
- **Duration.** Modern bear markets have averaged about a year from peak to trough; recoveries have taken anywhere from months (2020) to years (2000, 2008). The horror stories — 1929, Japan 1989 — involved extreme starting valuations plus policy failures.
- **The market bottoms before the news does.** In every case, the low came while the headlines were still terrible: March 2009 was months before unemployment peaked; March 2020 was days into lockdowns. Waiting for "things to look better" guarantees you miss the turn.
- **Recovery is faster than it feels.** The average investor's memory of 2008 is the fall; the record shows the S&P 500 was back at its peak in about four years with dividends and had more than tripled from the low within a decade.

## What to actually do

The correct actions in a crash are few, boring, and mostly written down in advance in your [investment policy statement](building-an-investment-policy-statement.md).

1. **Re-read your plan.** It exists for this moment. If you do not have one, the next best thing is to do nothing until you have written one.
2. **Keep contributing.** Every dollar invested during a 30% decline buys roughly 43% more shares than it did at the peak. Dalbar and Morningstar data both show that investors who kept automatic contributions through 2008–09 and 2020 came out far ahead of those who paused.
3. **Rebalance into fear.** If stocks fell from 60% to 48% of your portfolio, your rule says buy stocks back to 60% with money from bonds. This is the hardest instruction in investing to obey — it means buying the thing that is destroying you — and it is precisely the mechanism by which balanced portfolios recover faster than the market. Use thresholds (e.g., any asset 5 points off target) so the rule fires automatically.
4. **Harvest tax losses** in taxable accounts: sell a fund at a loss and immediately buy a similar-but-not-identical fund, keeping your market exposure while banking a deduction. This turns the crash into a tax asset and, usefully, gives your action bias something harmless to do.
5. **Reduce your checking frequency.** Weekly at most. Every look during a decline is an invitation to violate the plan (see [prospect-theory-and-loss-aversion.md](prospect-theory-and-loss-aversion.md)).
6. **Confirm your cash buffer.** The reason you can hold is that you do not need to sell. If your emergency fund is intact, nothing about the crash requires action.
7. **Write a journal entry.** How you feel, what you are tempted to do, what you expect. In two years it will be the most useful document you own.

## What not to do

- **Do not sell to "wait for clarity."** Clarity arrives only after prices have recovered. The realized loss is permanent; the paper loss was not.
- **Do not go to cash "temporarily."** Investors who sold in 2008 mostly did not return until 2012 or later, missing the doubling. Getting out requires one correct decision; getting back in requires a second, and it is much harder.
- **Do not add leverage or buy options** to "make it back." You are in the loss region of the value curve, where you are most risk-seeking and least clear-headed.
- **Do not overhaul the allocation.** A crash reveals whether your risk tolerance was what you thought. If it was not, fix it *after* recovery, in calm, in writing, and slightly. Cutting stocks at the bottom locks in the loss and repeats the mistake in reverse.
- **Do not consume more news.** Financial media's revenue rises with fear. Nothing you read during a crash will change what your plan says to do.
- **Do not confuse a bear market with a bubble.** A 30% fall from reasonable valuations is a sale; a 30% fall from absurd valuations may be halfway. The difference is the starting valuation, not the size of the drop.

## The cost of missing the best days — and the caveat

The most quoted chart in investing shows what happens if you miss the market's best days. J.P. Morgan's version for the 20 years to early 2025: a fully invested S&P 500 holder earned about 10.6% a year; missing just the 10 best days cut that to about 6.4%; missing the 20 best cut it to about 3.7%; missing the 30 best left roughly 1.5%; and in the *Guide to Retirement* edition of the same chart (2005–2024), missing the 40 best turned the return slightly negative. Seven of the ten best days fell within 15 days of the ten worst. Bank of America's longer-run version (since 1930) found that skipping each decade's ten best days reduced a cumulative gain of roughly 20,000% to under 50%.

**The honest caveat:** the mirror-image chart is just as dramatic. Missing the ten *worst* days each decade would have produced returns vastly better than buy-and-hold, and missing both the best and the worst days would have beaten buy-and-hold slightly. Critics rightly point out that nobody misses only the best days.

**Why the chart still matters:** the best and worst days are not separable in practice. J.P. Morgan found that seven of the ten best days in 2005–2024 occurred within two weeks of one of the ten worst. Six of the ten best days of 2020 came in March, the worst month. Burton Malkiel and colleagues showed that extreme up and down days cluster so tightly that rules avoiding most of the bad days still failed to beat buy-and-hold. In other words, the person who sells after a terrible day to avoid the next one is standing exactly where the best days happen. The chart is not an argument that timing is impossible in principle; it is an argument that the specific thing panicked investors do — sell after the worst days — is the single most reliable way to miss the best ones.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- Write the crash section of your IPS now; include the expected drawdown in dollars, not percentages, so it is not a surprise.
- Set rebalancing thresholds that force buying after a decline (e.g., rebalance whenever stocks are 5 points below target).
- Hold enough cash and short-term bonds outside the portfolio that no crash can force a sale for 1–3 years of needs.
- Automate contributions so that continuing through a crash requires no decision.
- Pre-write a "crash playbook" card: re-read plan; no news; contribute; rebalance if triggered; harvest losses; journal; do not sell.
- Give someone you trust the authority to stop you: a spouse's signature or an advisor's call before any sale over a set size.
- Keep this file's table somewhere visible. The next crash will feel unprecedented. It will not be.

## Key takeaways

- Bear markets of 20%+ arrive roughly every five to six years; expect several in an investing lifetime, including one or two that halve your portfolio.
- Every US crash has been followed by full recovery; typical recoveries take one to five years, with dividends shortening them.
- Markets bottom while the news is still terrible; waiting for clarity means missing the turn.
- The correct actions are few: re-read the plan, keep contributing, rebalance into stocks, harvest losses, look less.
- Selling to "wait it out" requires a second, harder decision to get back in; most never make it in time.
- Missing the best days is devastating because they cluster around the worst days — selling after a bad day is how you miss them.
- Structure — cash buffer, automation, thresholds, a witness — does what willpower cannot.
