# Building an Investment Policy Statement

**In one sentence:** An investment policy statement (IPS) is a short written contract with your future self — drafted when you are calm — that specifies what you own, why, and exactly what you will and will not do when markets make you feel otherwise.

## What an IPS is

Professional money managers are required to have one. Pension funds, endowments, and foundations all run on a document that sets the goals, the allowed investments, the target allocation, the rebalancing rules, and the criteria for change. Individuals almost never write one, which is strange, because individuals are the ones whose emotions actually get to touch the money.

An IPS is not a financial plan (which covers taxes, insurance, estate, and cash flow) and it is not a spreadsheet. It is one to three pages of plain rules. Its purpose is to move decisions from the moment of stress to the moment of clarity.

## Why calm-time rules protect you in panics

Every bias in this section — loss aversion, recency, herding, action bias — is strongest when prices are moving fast. That is precisely when you are least able to think clearly and most likely to act. Behavioral researchers call the gap between how you reason now and how you will reason under pressure the **hot-cold empathy gap**: the calm version of you cannot imagine how the panicked version will feel, and the panicked version cannot remember what the calm version knew.

Ulysses solved this by having himself tied to the mast before the Sirens sang. An IPS is your mast. It works because:

- **It converts a judgment into a lookup.** "Should I sell?" becomes "what does page two say?"
- **It creates a burden of proof.** Deviating requires you to articulate, in writing, why the rule is wrong — which is hard to do honestly when the real reason is fear.
- **It adds friction and delay.** Most panic decisions are regretted within weeks. A required waiting period lets the feeling pass.
- **It records your reasoning.** When you later wonder why you own bonds, or international stocks, or why you hold no gold, the answer is there in your own words.
- **It enlists others.** A spouse, adult child, or advisor who has a copy can hold you to it.

Evidence that rules beat discretion is everywhere in this library: automated contributors show the smallest behavior gap; investors with written plans are markedly less likely to sell in downturns; simple rebalancing rules beat most attempts at tactical timing.

## The template

Fill in every bracket. Keep the whole thing short enough to re-read in five minutes.

---

### 1. Purpose and goals

> This portfolio exists to [fund retirement beginning in approximately 2045 / provide college funding for two children beginning 2034 / build financial independence]. The primary goal is [a real (after-inflation) return of about 4–5% per year over rolling 15-year periods], which is sufficient to [reach $X by year Y at current contribution rates]. Secondary goals: [keep annual costs under 0.25%; minimize taxes; require less than four hours of attention per year].

### 2. Time horizon and liquidity

> Money in this portfolio will not be needed for at least [15] years. Separate from this portfolio, I hold [6 months] of expenses in cash and [any cash needed within 3 years] in short-term bonds or savings. Because of this, I will not sell portfolio assets to raise cash under any circumstances short of [job loss lasting more than six months / medical emergency].

### 3. Risk tolerance and capacity

> I understand that a portfolio of [70% stocks / 30% bonds] has historically fallen [about 35%] from peak to trough in severe bear markets and that such declines occur roughly [every 7–10 years] and last [1–3 years] before recovering. I have decided in advance that a decline of this size is acceptable and expected, not a signal to change course. If the portfolio falls 35%, my plan is: [do nothing except rebalance per section 6].
>
> My income is [stable / variable] and my human capital is [large / limited], which [supports / constrains] this level of risk.

### 4. Target allocation

> | Asset class | Target | Allowed range | Vehicle |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | US total stock market | 45% | 40–50% | [fund name / ticker] |
> | International developed + emerging stocks | 25% | 20–30% | [fund name / ticker] |
> | US investment-grade bonds | 25% | 20–30% | [fund name / ticker] |
> | Cash / short-term | 5% | 0–10% | [account] |
>
> No single stock, sector fund, or speculative asset may exceed [5%] of the portfolio. Employer stock is capped at [5%]. Anything outside the table above goes in the sandbox account (section 8) or is not owned.

### 5. Investment selection criteria

> I invest only in [broadly diversified, low-cost index funds or ETFs with expense ratios below 0.20% and at least $1 billion in assets]. I do not buy individual stocks, leveraged or inverse funds, options, or any product I cannot explain in two sentences to a 14-year-old. I do not invest in anything based on a tip, a forum post, or a news story.

### 6. Contribution and rebalancing rules

> I contribute [$X] on the [1st] of every month automatically, allocated to targets. Contributions continue regardless of market conditions; in a decline of 20% or more I will [increase contributions by X% if cash flow permits].
>
> I rebalance to targets on [January 15 and July 15] each year, or whenever any asset class moves outside its allowed range, whichever comes first. New contributions are directed to the most underweight asset first. Rebalancing means selling what has risen and buying what has fallen; I acknowledge this will feel wrong every time.

### 7. What I will do in a crash

> When the market is down 20% or more from its high, I will: (1) re-read this document; (2) not check balances more than once a week; (3) continue contributions; (4) rebalance per section 6 if a range is breached; (5) harvest tax losses in taxable accounts where a suitable replacement fund exists; (6) not read financial news for the duration; (7) not sell.
>
> Any sale of more than [10%] of the portfolio, or any change to section 4, requires: a written explanation of what has changed in *my circumstances* (not in the market), a [30-day] waiting period, and the agreement of [spouse / named person].

### 8. Sandbox (optional)

> I keep a separate speculative account capped at [5%] of net worth for individual stocks, crypto, or other bets. It is never refilled from the core portfolio. If it goes to zero, it stays at zero until the next annual review.

### 9. Monitoring

> I check balances [quarterly]. Once a year, on [date], I review: whether goals or circumstances have changed; whether costs are still low; my money-weighted return versus fund returns (the behavior gap); whether any rule was broken and why.

### 10. Review triggers

> This document is revisited only on the annual date or when one of these happens: marriage or divorce; birth of a child; job change or loss; inheritance or windfall greater than [X]; being within [5] years of the goal date; a change in tax law materially affecting the accounts. A change in market prices, headlines, forecasts, or my mood is explicitly *not* a trigger.

### Signature

> Signed: ___________ Date: ___________ Witnessed by: ___________

## Writing it well

- **Write the crash section first.** It is the only section that will ever be tested. Be concrete: what you will do, what you will not, how often you will look, who you will call.
- **State the expected pain in numbers.** "I know this can fall 35%" is far more protective than "I have a moderate risk tolerance." Look up the actual worst drawdown of your allocation and write it down.
- **Make circumstances the only valid reason to change.** Markets change constantly; your life changes rarely. Tie every allowed revision to the latter.
- **Add a waiting period to everything discretionary.** Thirty days kills most bad ideas.
- **Include a named witness.** Even the act of explaining your plan to someone else tests it, and having to tell them you broke it is a real deterrent.
- **Sign it.** It sounds silly. It works.

## When to revisit

Once a year on a fixed date, and only otherwise for the life events listed in section 10. The annual review should take under an hour and answer three questions: Did anything about *me* change? Did I follow the rules? If not, is the problem the rule or the follower? Rules that were broken should be tightened or automated, not dropped.

Resist the urge to revise after a bad year. A rule that only survives good years is not a rule.

## Countermeasures / How to apply

- Draft the ten sections today, even roughly; a rough IPS beats a perfect one that does not exist.
- Print it. Keep one copy where you will see it during a crash (taped inside a desk drawer works) and give one to your witness.
- Set calendar reminders for the rebalancing dates and the annual review now.
- Automate everything section 6 allows: contributions, allocation, and (if your brokerage supports it) rebalancing.
- Re-read it at the first sign of a 10% decline, before the 20% decline arrives.

## Key takeaways

- An IPS moves decisions from the moment of stress to the moment of clarity — Ulysses and the mast.
- The calm you and the panicked you cannot understand each other; the document is the bridge.
- The crash section is the only part that will ever really be tested; write it first and in numbers.
- Changes in your life are valid reasons to revise; changes in prices, headlines, or moods are not.
- Waiting periods, a witness, and a signature add the friction that turns intentions into behavior.
- Review annually; tighten rules that were broken rather than discarding them.
- A rough IPS written today beats a perfect one written never.
