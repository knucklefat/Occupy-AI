# Academic Papers and Research

**In one sentence:** Thirty papers that changed how investing is understood — from Markowitz's diversification math to Morningstar's evidence on investor behavior — each with a link, a plain-language summary of what it found, and a note on why it still matters.

You do not need to read these papers in full; most have a summary on Alpha Architect or a Ben Felix video. But knowing what they established, and who established it, inoculates you against people who cite "research" that does not exist. Links go to the free version where one exists (author page, SSRN, NBER) and to the journal's DOI otherwise. Journal links may require a library login for full text; the abstract is always free.

## Portfolio theory and market efficiency

| Paper | Authors, year | What it found | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| ["Portfolio Selection"](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2975974) | Harry Markowitz, 1952 (*Journal of Finance*) | Risk should be measured at the portfolio level, and combining assets that do not move together reduces risk without reducing expected return. | The mathematical foundation of diversification and of every "efficient frontier" chart. Nobel Prize 1990. |
| ["Capital Asset Prices"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1964.tb02865.x) | William Sharpe, 1964 (*Journal of Finance*) | In equilibrium, an asset's expected return depends only on its sensitivity to the market ("beta"); other risk is diversifiable and unrewarded. | The CAPM. Wrong in detail, foundational in structure; everything after it is a correction to it. |
| ["Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work"](https://doi.org/10.2307/2325486) | Eugene Fama, 1970 (*Journal of Finance*) | Defined weak, semi-strong, and strong-form efficiency and surveyed the evidence that prices reflect available information. | The efficient market hypothesis in its canonical statement. The reason index funds work even though markets are not perfectly efficient. |
| ["The Arithmetic of Active Management"](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/active/active.htm) | William Sharpe, 1991 (*Financial Analysts Journal*) | Before costs, the average active dollar earns the market return; after costs, it must earn less. This is arithmetic, not an empirical claim. | Three pages. The single most important argument for indexing, and unanswerable. |
| ["The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville"](https://business.columbia.edu/insights/chazen-global-insights/superinvestors-graham-and-doddsville) | Warren Buffett, 1984 (Columbia Business School) | Nine managers who learned from Graham all beat the market over long periods, which is hard to square with pure luck. | Not peer-reviewed, but the most-cited rebuttal to strong-form efficiency, and a model of how to argue with data. |
| ["Determinants of Portfolio Performance"](https://doi.org/10.2469/faj.v42.n4.39) | Brinson, Hood & Beebower, 1986 (*Financial Analysts Journal*) | Asset allocation policy explained over 90% of the variation in pension fund returns over time. | Widely misquoted as "90% of returns"; what it shows is that the stock/bond mix dominates the swings, which is still the point. |

## Factors: what predicts returns

| Paper | Authors, year | What it found | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| ["The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04398.x) | Fama & French, 1992 (*Journal of Finance*) | Beta alone does not explain returns; size and book-to-market (value) do. | The paper that broke the CAPM and launched factor investing. |
| ["Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds"](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(93)90023-5) | Fama & French, 1993 (*Journal of Financial Economics*) | Formalized the three-factor model (market, size, value) and showed it explains most diversified portfolio returns. | The model every fund's "factor exposure" is measured against; the data is on the French library. |
| ["Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb04702.x) | Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993 (*Journal of Finance*) | Stocks that rose over the past 3–12 months keep rising for the next 3–12 months. | Momentum: the anomaly efficient-market theorists find hardest to explain, and it has persisted since publication. |
| ["On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03808.x) | Mark Carhart, 1997 (*Journal of Finance*) | Adding momentum to the three-factor model explains nearly all mutual fund "persistence"; skill is rare and costs matter. | The four-factor model, and strong evidence that past fund performance is mostly factor exposure and fees. |
| ["The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium"](https://rnm.simon.rochester.edu/research/OSoV.pdf) | Robert Novy-Marx, 2013 (*Journal of Financial Economics*) | Gross profits to assets predicts returns about as well as book-to-market, and profitable value stocks do best of all. | Put "quality" on the same footing as value and gave Buffett-style investing an academic basis. |
| ["A Five-Factor Asset Pricing Model"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2287202) | Fama & French, 2015 (*Journal of Financial Economics*) | Adding profitability and investment factors to the three-factor model improves it; value becomes partly redundant. | The current standard model; the basis for how Dimensional and Avantis funds are built. |
| ["Value and Momentum Everywhere"](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Value-and-Momentum-Everywhere) | Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013 (*Journal of Finance*) | Value and momentum premiums exist in stocks, bonds, currencies, and commodities across countries, and are negatively correlated with each other. | Evidence that the factors are not a US-stock data-mining artifact, and that combining them is powerful. |
| ["Betting Against Beta"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2013.10.005) | Frazzini & Pedersen, 2014 (*Journal of Financial Economics*) | Low-beta assets earn higher risk-adjusted returns than high-beta ones, because leverage-constrained investors overpay for high beta. | Explains the low-volatility anomaly and why boring stocks do so well. |
| ["Buffett's Alpha"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19681) | Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, 2013 (NBER; *FAJ* 2018) | Berkshire's record is largely explained by exposure to cheap, safe, high-quality stocks plus 1.6x leverage from insurance float, held with discipline. | Shows Buffett is not magic but is a system that can be described and, in principle, copied. |
| ["Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information"](https://doi.org/10.2307/2672906) | Joseph Piotroski, 2000 (*Journal of Accounting Research*) | A nine-point fundamental score (the F-Score) separates winning from losing cheap stocks. | The original quantitative-quality screen, still used by GuruFocus and others. |
| ["...and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns"](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhv059) | Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016 (*Review of Financial Studies*) | Hundreds of "factors" have been published; with proper multiple-testing corrections, most are probably false. | The "factor zoo" warning. Demand a t-statistic above 3 and a plausible reason before believing any new factor. |
| ["Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?"](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12365) | McLean & Pontiff, 2016 (*Journal of Finance*) | Anomaly returns fall by about a third after publication as investors trade on them. | Why past factor premiums overstate future ones, and why costs and capacity matter. |

## Investor behavior

| Paper | Authors, year | What it found | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| ["Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk"](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185) | Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (*Econometrica*) | People weigh losses about twice as heavily as gains, evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, and overweight small probabilities. | The founding paper of behavioral economics and the explanation for most investing mistakes. Nobel Prize 2002. |
| ["Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth"](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf) | Barber & Odean, 2000 (*Journal of Finance*) | Among 66,000 brokerage households, the most active traders earned 11.4% a year versus 17.9% for the market; overconfidence drives trading and trading drives underperformance. | The definitive evidence that activity hurts. Their companion paper, ["Boys Will Be Boys"](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355301556400) (2001), shows men trade 45% more than women and earn less. |
| ["Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?"](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00072) | Terrance Odean, 1998 (*Journal of Finance*) | Investors sell winners far more readily than losers (the disposition effect), even though the losers go on to underperform. | The tax-inefficient, return-reducing habit almost everyone has; naming it helps. |
| ["Myopic Loss Aversion and the Equity Premium Puzzle"](https://doi.org/10.2307/2118511) | Benartzi & Thaler, 1995 (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*) | Investors who check their portfolios frequently experience more losses and therefore demand a higher premium to hold stocks. | The academic case for looking at your portfolio less often. |
| ["Mind the Gap"](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap) | Morningstar (Jeffrey Ptak and team), annual since 2005; [2025 edition PDF](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/blt2c5c4d9171638c42/689b424311f3880edc4b4813/US_Mind_the_Gap_2025.pdf) | Investors' dollar-weighted returns trail their funds' reported returns by roughly 1 percentage point a year, more in volatile and niche funds, because of badly timed buying and selling. | The behavior gap quantified. A [2026 critique](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4904652) argues the gap is smaller than the headline; read both. |
| [Dalbar QAIB](https://www.dalbar.com/QAIB) | Dalbar, annual since 1994 (paid report; summaries are widely reported) | Claims the average equity fund investor has trailed the S&P 500 by several points a year for decades. | Widely cited, methodologically contested (its return calculation has been criticized by Wade Pfau and others). Cite Morningstar instead. |

## Valuation, expected returns, and retirement

| Paper | Authors, year | What it found | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| ["Valuation Ratios and the Long-Run Stock Market Outlook: An Update"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8221) | Campbell & Shiller, 2001 (NBER) | The cyclically adjusted P/E (CAPE) predicts 10-year real stock returns reasonably well, and poorly predicts anything shorter. | The basis of every "the market is expensive" argument; also the reason it is useless for timing. Shiller's data is free on his Yale page. |
| ["Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2900447) | Hendrik Bessembinder, 2018 (*Journal of Financial Economics*) | Since 1926, just 4% of US stocks account for all net wealth creation; the median stock underperformed T-bills over its life. | Why concentrated stock-picking is so hard and diversification so valuable: returns are driven by a few enormous winners. |
| ["Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data"](https://www.financialplanningassociation.org/sites/default/files/2021-04/MAR04%20Determining%20Withdrawal%20Rates%20Using%20Historical%20Data.pdf) | William Bengen, 1994 (*Journal of Financial Planning*) | A retiree with 50–75% stocks could withdraw 4% of the initial portfolio, inflation-adjusted, for at least 30 years in every historical period. | The "4% rule." Bengen has since revised the figure upward for diversified portfolios, but the method is the point. |
| ["Retirement Savings: Choosing a Withdrawal Rate That Is Sustainable"](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/retirement-savings-choosing-a-withdrawal-rate-that-is-sustainable) | Cooley, Hubbard & Walz, 1998 (*AAII Journal*) — the "Trinity Study" | Success rates for withdrawal rates from 3% to 12% across stock/bond mixes and horizons, 1926–1995. | Turned Bengen's finding into the success-rate tables everyone uses. Updated by the authors in 2011. |
| ["Reducing Retirement Risk with a Rising Equity Glide Path"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2324930) | Pfau & Kitces, 2013 (*Journal of Financial Planning*) | Starting retirement with a lower stock allocation and increasing it over time improves outcomes versus the conventional declining glide path. | Counterintuitive, influential, and the origin of the "bond tent" idea. |
| ["Stocks, Bonds, Bills, and Inflation: Year-by-Year Historical Returns (1926–1974)"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jnlbus/v49y1976i1p11-47.html) | Ibbotson & Sinquefield, 1976 (*Journal of Business*) | Built the first systematic long-run record of US asset class returns. | The origin of the "stocks return 10%" statistic and of the annual SBBI yearbook. |

## Where to find more

- [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/) — free preprints of most finance papers; search by author.
- [NBER Working Papers](https://www.nber.org/papers) — economics and finance preprints, free.
- [Alpha Architect blog](https://alphaarchitect.com/blog/) — readable summaries of new papers several times a week, sorted by topic.
- [Kenneth French Data Library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html) — the factor data to test any claim yourself.
- [Rational Reminder podcast](https://rationalreminder.ca/) — interviews with many of the authors above (Fama, French, Cochrane, Bessembinder, Pfau).
- [Ben Felix on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@BenFelixCSI) — ten-minute videos, each built on a handful of these papers with citations on screen.
- [AQR research library](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research) — AQR's published papers, most in the journals above, free to download.
- [Dimensional research](https://www.dimensional.com/us-en/insights) — DFA's plain-language pieces built on the Fama-French work.

## Key takeaways

- The chain is Markowitz (diversify) → Sharpe (beta) → Fama-French (size, value, then profitability and investment) → Jegadeesh-Titman and Carhart (momentum); everything factor funds do descends from it.
- Sharpe's 1991 "Arithmetic" and Bessembinder's 2018 skewness result together explain why indexing works and why stock-picking is harder than it looks.
- Behavioral papers (Kahneman-Tversky, Barber-Odean, Odean, Benartzi-Thaler) and Morningstar's Mind the Gap document the same thing from different angles: the investor is the biggest risk to the portfolio.
- Retirement math rests on three papers — Bengen 1994, the Trinity Study, and Pfau-Kitces on glide paths — plus Shiller's data; read those before trusting any calculator.
- Harvey-Liu-Zhu and McLean-Pontiff are the antidote to every new "factor" you will be sold: most are noise, and the real ones shrink after publication.
