# Regulators and Official Guides

**In one sentence:** The US government publishes free, impartial, and legally accurate guides to investing, taxes, retirement accounts, and fraud — and free databases that let you check any advisor's record in two minutes — so this page collects the ones worth bookmarking and explains what each is for.

Official sources have two advantages over everything else in this section: they are not selling anything, and they are the final word on what the rules actually are. Their disadvantage is that they are organized for lawyers, not for you. The tables below point to the specific page you need rather than the agency's home page. All links are US-focused and were checked in September 2026; the FTC's fraud reporting site was temporarily offline during a government shutdown at the time of checking.

## Investor education and protection

| Resource | Agency | What it is for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/) | Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) | The SEC's plain-English site for individual investors: how markets work, product explainers (ETFs, annuities, options), calculators, and fraud alerts | The best starting point on this page. The [compound interest calculator](https://www.investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator) is the one to send to a teenager. The [Introduction to Investing](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing) section is a short, impartial course. |
| [FINRA for Investors](https://www.finra.org/investors) | Financial Industry Regulatory Authority | Education, tools, and complaint handling for anyone dealing with a broker-dealer | FINRA is the self-regulator for brokerage firms, funded by the industry but with real enforcement power. Its [Fund Analyzer](https://tools.finra.org/fund_analyzer/) shows the dollar cost of fund fees over time, and the [file a complaint](https://www.finra.org/investors/need-help/file-a-complaint) page is the route when a broker has done something wrong. |
| [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) | CFPB | Banking, credit cards, mortgages, student loans, and debt collection; a public complaint database | Not an investing regulator, but the place to go when a bank or lender misbehaves. Its consumer complaint database is searchable and companies are required to respond. Staffing and scope have been reduced since 2025; the complaint portal still operates. |
| [CFTC Learn and Protect](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect) | Commodity Futures Trading Commission | Futures, options on futures, forex, and much of crypto; fraud advisories and a complaint portal | If a pitch involves commodities, forex trading systems, or "crypto trading bots," this is the regulator. Its fraud advisories are specific and current. |
| [SIPC](https://www.sipc.org/) | Securities Investor Protection Corporation | What happens to your securities if your brokerage fails | Covers up to $500,000 per customer (including $250,000 in cash) against a broker's failure — not against investment losses. Check that your broker is a member; almost all are. |
| [FDIC deposit insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/) | Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation | What bank deposits are insured, up to $250,000 per depositor per bank per ownership category | Use the [BankFind](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind) tool to confirm a bank is insured before opening a high-yield account, and note that brokerage cash "sweep" programs may spread deposits across several banks. |
| [Employee Benefits Security Administration](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa) | Department of Labor | Oversight of 401(k) and other employer retirement plans under ERISA | Where to go if your employer is late depositing your 401(k) contributions or your plan's fees look wrong. The publication "A Look at 401(k) Plan Fees" is here. |
| [my Social Security](https://www.ssa.gov/myaccount/) | Social Security Administration | Your earnings record and benefit estimates at different claiming ages | Create the account now, whatever your age, to check your earnings record for errors and to prevent someone else from creating it in your name. |

## Checking an advisor, broker, or firm

This is the most important thing on the page. Anyone who manages money or sells securities in the US must be registered, and their disciplinary history is public. Check before you hand over a dollar, and check again every few years.

| Tool | What it covers | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| [FINRA BrokerCheck](https://brokercheck.finra.org/) | Brokers (people who sell securities) and brokerage firms; also links to advisor records | Type the name. You get employment history, licenses, and "disclosures": customer complaints, arbitrations, regulatory actions, terminations, and bankruptcies. One disclosure is worth reading about; several is a pattern. |
| [Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD)](https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/) | Registered investment advisers (RIAs) and their representatives, SEC- and state-registered | Search the firm, then read its Form ADV Part 2 ("the brochure"), which must disclose fees, conflicts of interest, and disciplinary history in plain English. If an advisor will not give you their ADV, walk away. |
| [Investor.gov "Check Out Your Investment Professional"](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/working-investment-professional/check-out-your-investment-professional) | A single search box that queries both databases above | The quickest route. Also explains what the results mean. |
| [NASAA: find your state securities regulator](https://www.nasaa.org/contact-your-regulator/) | State-registered advisers (generally those under $100 million in assets), and state-level enforcement | Smaller advisers register with the state, not the SEC, and states are often faster at responding to complaints. Every state has a securities division; this page lists them. |
| [CFP Board verification](https://www.cfp.net/verify-a-cfp-professional) | Whether someone actually holds the CFP certification and whether the Board has disciplined them | Credentials are easy to claim on a business card. This checks the most common one; the [CFA Institute member directory](https://directory.cfainstitute.org/) does the same for charterholders. |

What to look for: whether the person is a **fiduciary** (RIAs are; brokers generally are not, despite "best interest" rules), how they are paid (fees from you, commissions from products, or both), and whether their disclosures include customer disputes. The Form ADV Part 2 answers all three.

## Taxes: the IRS publications investors actually need

IRS publications are dry, but they are free, complete, and updated every year, and they settle arguments that forums cannot. Read the relevant one before a major decision (selling a business, converting to a Roth, inheriting an IRA).

| Publication | Covers | When you need it |
|---|---|---|
| [Publication 550: Investment Income and Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) | Interest, dividends (qualified vs. ordinary), capital gains and losses, wash sales, cost basis, worthless securities, and how to report all of it | Every year at tax time if you have a taxable brokerage account. The wash-sale and cost-basis sections are the ones people get wrong. |
| [Publication 590-A: Contributions to IRAs](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) | Traditional and Roth contribution limits, income phase-outs, deductibility, and excess-contribution penalties | Before contributing, especially if you or a spouse has a workplace plan or your income is near the phase-out. |
| [Publication 590-B: Distributions from IRAs](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b) | Required minimum distributions, the 10% early-withdrawal penalty and its exceptions, inherited IRA rules, and Roth ordering rules | Before any withdrawal, any Roth conversion, and when you inherit an IRA. The post-SECURE Act inherited-IRA rules are here and are not intuitive. |
| [Publication 560: Retirement Plans for Small Business](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p560) | SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, and solo 401(k) limits and rules | If you are self-employed or run a small business and want to shelter more than an IRA allows. |
| [Publication 525: Taxable and Nontaxable Income](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525) | What counts as income, including stock options, RSUs, and ESPPs | If you receive equity compensation. |
| [Publication 523: Selling Your Home](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523) | The $250,000/$500,000 home-sale exclusion and how to compute basis | Before selling a primary residence. |
| [Topic 409: Capital Gains and Losses](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) | A two-page summary of holding periods, rates, and the $3,000 loss limit | The quick reference when you do not need all of Pub 550. |
| [Publication 17: Your Federal Income Tax](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p17) | The whole individual tax system in one book | When you want to understand how it all fits together. |

## How to spot a scam

The SEC, FINRA, and state regulators agree on the warning signs. From [Investor.gov's fraud pages](https://www.investor.gov/protect-your-investments/fraud/types-fraud) and [FINRA's investor alerts](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights):

1. **Guaranteed or unusually consistent returns.** Nothing that returns more than Treasuries is guaranteed; anything that never has a down month (Madoff's signature) is fabricated.
2. **Pressure to act now.** Legitimate investments are available next week. "Limited time" and "only a few spots left" are sales tactics, and in investing they are fraud tactics.
3. **Unregistered people or products.** Run BrokerCheck and IAPD. Check whether the security itself is registered using [EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/); many frauds are "private placements" that are exempt from registration, which is legal but a red flag when combined with the other signs.
4. **Complexity used as a selling point.** "Proprietary algorithm," "arbitrage strategy," or "trading bot" without a clear explanation of where the return comes from.
5. **Difficulty getting your money out.** Delays, excuses, or requests for more money to "unlock" a withdrawal are the final stage of a Ponzi scheme or a "pig butchering" crypto scam.
6. **Contact that came to you.** Cold calls, social-media DMs, and romance-then-investment pitches are how nearly all retail fraud begins. Affinity fraud — pitches through a church, ethnic community, or professional group — works because the trust is borrowed.
7. **Celebrity or "finfluencer" endorsement.** Paid promotion of securities must be disclosed; most is not.

If you have been targeted: report to the [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/submit-tip-or-complaint), [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/need-help/file-a-complaint), your [state regulator](https://www.nasaa.org/contact-your-regulator/), the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/), and, for online fraud, the FBI's [IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/). Reporting rarely recovers money but it does stop the next victim.

## Key takeaways

- Investor.gov is the impartial starting point for any investing question; the IRS publications are the final word on any tax question.
- Before hiring anyone, check BrokerCheck and IAPD, read their Form ADV Part 2, and confirm whether they are a fiduciary and how they are paid.
- Publications 550, 590-A, and 590-B cover almost everything an individual investor needs to know about taxes on brokerage and retirement accounts.
- SIPC and FDIC protect you against a firm failing, not against losses; know the limits and which one applies to each account.
- Every scam has the same signature — guaranteed returns, urgency, unregistered sellers, and unsolicited contact — and every regulator publishes the same list because it keeps working.
