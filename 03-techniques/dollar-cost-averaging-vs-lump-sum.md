# Dollar-Cost Averaging vs. Lump Sum

**In one sentence:** If you have a pile of cash to invest, investing it all at once has beaten spreading it out about two-thirds of the time historically, but dollar-cost averaging is a legitimate choice for anyone whose real risk is panicking after a bad first month.

## Definitions

**Lump-sum investing (LS)** means putting the whole amount into your target allocation immediately.

**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** — Vanguard calls it "cost averaging" — means investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals (say, $10,000 a month for twelve months) regardless of price. Because a fixed dollar amount buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high, your average cost per share ends up below the average price over the period.

One important distinction: investing your paycheck every two weeks into a 401(k) is not really a DCA *decision* — you are investing money as soon as you have it, which is lump-sum behaviour applied to each paycheck. The DCA-vs-LS question only arises when you already have cash sitting idle: an inheritance, a bonus, a home sale, or a rollover.

## What the evidence says

Vanguard's original 2012 study (Shtekhman, Tasopoulos and Wimmer) compared investing $1 million at once versus over 12 months in a 60/40 portfolio, using rolling 10-year periods in the U.S., U.K. and Australia. Lump sum won about two-thirds of the time, and by an average of roughly 2.3% over the ten years in the U.S.

Vanguard refreshed the work in 2023 (Finlay and Zorn, "Cost averaging: Invest now or temporarily hold your cash?"), extending the data to 1976–2022 and adding Canada, Europe and emerging markets. The result held: lump sum beat cost averaging roughly **68% of the time** on a one-year horizon. For a $100,000 investment in a 60/40 portfolio, the median one-year outcome was about **$109,360 for lump sum versus $107,453 for cost averaging** — a gap of about 1.8%. The more stock-heavy the target portfolio, the bigger the lump-sum edge.

Why? Markets go up more often than they go down. Every month you keep money in cash rather than in your target allocation, you are, on average, giving up expected return. DCA is a form of temporary market timing that bets on the next few months being worse than average.

| Horizon / condition | Lump sum wins about… |
|---|---|
| Any 1-year period, 60/40 (global, 1976–2022) | 68% of the time |
| Any 10-year period, 60/40 (U.S., 1926–2011) | 67% of the time |
| Any period, 100% stocks | Slightly more often, by a larger margin |
| Periods starting in a bear market | Less often — DCA does better when prices are falling |

## When DCA is still the right call

The Vanguard authors are explicit that the lump-sum edge is an average, not a guarantee, and that cost averaging suits investors with "very high aversion to both risk and losses." Concretely, DCA makes sense when:

- **Regret is your real risk.** If you invest $500,000 on Monday and it is worth $400,000 by Friday, would you sell? If yes, a plan you can survive beats a plan with a higher expected value. Lump sum's edge is roughly 1–2% in the typical year; a panic sale costs far more.
- **The money is life-changing.** A windfall that represents ten years of savings is different from a $5,000 bonus. The larger the sum relative to your existing portfolio, the more defensible spreading it out.
- **You are moving from a very different allocation.** Switching from 100% cash to 90% stocks is a big jump. Some investors DCA into the *stock* portion and put the bond portion in immediately.
- **You need time to decide on the allocation anyway.** DCA over three to six months gives you a deadline without paralysis.

DCA does *not* make sense as a permanent strategy — if you are still "averaging in" 24 months later, you are just holding cash. Nor does it make sense to DCA because you think the market is "high." Vanguard's data includes plenty of all-time highs; lump sum still won.

## Worked example

You inherit $120,000 and your target is 80/20.

**Option A – Lump sum.** Invest $96,000 in stock index funds and $24,000 in bonds today. Expected one-year return using long-run averages (~8% for the mix): roughly $129,600.

**Option B – 12-month DCA.** Invest $10,000 a month. On average, half the money is out of the market for half the year, so you forfeit about half a year of expected return on the average dollar: roughly 0.5 × 8% × $120,000 ≈ $4,800, giving an expected ~$124,800. In a year where stocks fall 20% in the first six months and recover, DCA can end thousands ahead; in a year where they rise steadily, it finishes thousands behind.

**Option C – Hybrid.** Invest 50% now, then DCA the remainder over six months. This captures most of the expected-return edge while capping how much of the windfall can be hit by a crash in week one. It is a popular compromise and not a bad one.

## How to actually do it

1. Decide your target allocation first (see [asset-allocation.md](asset-allocation.md)). DCA is about *how fast* you get there, not *where*.
2. Ask the regret question honestly: if the market fell 25% the month after you invested everything, would you hold? If yes, lump sum.
3. If no, pick a DCA schedule of **3 to 12 months**. Shorter is better; beyond a year the expected cost grows and the psychological benefit fades.
4. Automate it. Set recurring transfers and buys on the same date each month so you cannot "wait for a better price."
5. Park the uninvested cash in a money-market fund or high-yield savings account, not a checking account.
6. Never pause the schedule because of news. The whole point is that you decided in advance.
7. Once fully invested, switch to normal paycheck investing and a rebalancing rule.

## Key takeaways

- Lump sum has beaten DCA roughly 68% of the time across global markets since 1976, by ~1–2% in the median year.
- The edge exists because markets rise more often than they fall; holding cash has an expected cost.
- DCA's value is behavioural insurance against regret and panic selling, which cost far more than the lump-sum edge.
- The bigger the windfall relative to your net worth, the more reasonable it is to spread it over 3–12 months.
- A 50%-now / 50%-over-six-months hybrid captures most of the upside with a smaller worst case.
- Regular paycheck investing is not "DCA" in this sense — invest it as it arrives.
- Whatever you choose, automate it and finish within a year.
