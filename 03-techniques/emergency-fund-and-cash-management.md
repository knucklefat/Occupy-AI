# Emergency Fund and Cash Management

**In one sentence:** Keep three to six months of essential expenses in cash you can reach within days, hold it somewhere that pays close to Treasury-bill rates (which in September 2026 means roughly 3.6–4.1%), and never let it drift into either a 0.01% checking account or the stock market.

## How much

The standard advice is **3–6 months of essential expenses** — not income, and not your full lifestyle. Essential means rent or mortgage, food, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, transport, and childcare. A household spending $6,000 a month on everything but $4,200 on essentials needs $12,600–$25,200.

Adjust the multiple:

| Lean toward 3 months | Lean toward 6–12 months |
|---|---|
| Two stable incomes | Single income or single earner |
| Salaried, in-demand field | Self-employed, commissioned, or in a cyclical industry |
| No dependents, renting | Dependents, homeowner (roofs, furnaces) |
| Strong family safety net | Health issues, older, or in early retirement |
| Large taxable portfolio as backstop | Everything is in retirement accounts |

Two special cases. If you are carrying **credit-card debt** at 20%+, hold a small starter fund (one month, or $1,000–$2,000) and throw everything else at the debt — a $10,000 cushion earning 4% while you pay 24% on $10,000 costs you $2,000 a year. If you are **retired**, this file overlaps with the cash bucket in [withdrawal-strategies-and-safe-withdrawal-rate.md](withdrawal-strategies-and-safe-withdrawal-rate.md): one to two years of withdrawals in cash-like assets.

## Where to hold it: the 2026 yield landscape

Rates below are as of **September 8–11, 2026**, with the federal funds rate at about 3.63% after the Fed's 2025–26 cuts. They will move; the *relationships* between them are what matter.

| Vehicle | Yield (Sept 2026) | Liquidity | Taxes | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big-bank savings (national avg.) | ~0.62% | Instant | Fully taxable | Avoid — this is where most emergency funds die |
| High-yield savings account (HYSA) | 3.5–4.10% | 1–3 days | Fully taxable | FDIC-insured to $250k; rates change without notice |
| Government money-market fund (e.g., VMFXX, SPAXX) | ~3.3–3.6% | 1 day | Partly state-exempt | Not FDIC-insured but holds only government paper |
| Treasury money-market fund (e.g., VUSXX) | ~3.7% | 1 day | Mostly state-tax-exempt | Best after-tax option in high-tax states |
| 3-month Treasury bill | ~3.80% | Sellable any day | State-tax-exempt | Buy at TreasuryDirect or any broker |
| 6-month / 1-year T-bill | ~3.90% / ~3.96% | Sellable any day | State-tax-exempt | Small pickup for locking in |
| Series I savings bond | 4.26% (0.90% fixed + 3.36% inflation) | Locked 12 months; 3-month interest penalty if cashed before 5 years | Federal only, deferrable | Rate resets every May and November; $10k/person/year |
| Short-term bond ETF (e.g., SGOV, BIL) | ~3.7–3.8% | Sellable any day, settles next day | State-tax-exempt (Treasury interest) | Behaves like a T-bill ladder in one ticker |

Two takeaways from the table. First, the gap between the national average savings rate and the best options is about **3.5 percentage points** — on a $20,000 fund that is $700 a year for filling out one form. Second, all of the "good" options cluster within about half a point of each other, so don't agonise; pick one you'll actually use.

**State taxes matter.** Treasury interest (T-bills, Treasury money-market funds, SGOV, I bonds) is exempt from state and local income tax. For someone in California's 9.3% bracket, a 3.70% Treasury fund is worth about the same as a 4.08% HYSA. Note that Vanguard's VMFXX and Fidelity's SPAXX hold repurchase agreements, so only part of their income is state-exempt; VUSXX and SGOV are nearly 100% Treasuries.

## Structuring it: tiers and ladders

A single HYSA is fine for most people. If you want to squeeze a little more yield or safety, use tiers:

- **Tier 1 (1 month):** checking or HYSA — instant.
- **Tier 2 (2–5 months):** Treasury money-market fund or a **T-bill ladder** — buy equal amounts of 4-, 8-, 13-, 17- and 26-week bills so one matures every few weeks. Reinvest each maturity automatically ("auto-roll" at TreasuryDirect, Fidelity or Schwab) until you need it. Ladders let you lock in today's rates for part of the fund while keeping regular access.
- **Tier 3 (months 6–12, optional):** I bonds bought a year ahead of when you might need them. After 12 months they are fully liquid (with a three-month interest penalty until year five), inflation-protected, and tax-deferred. Many people build a permanent I-bond layer $10,000 a year.

**Worked example — a $24,000 fund, $4,000/month essentials:**

| Tier | Amount | Where | Approx. yield |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $4,000 | HYSA at 4.0% | $160/yr |
| 2 | $12,000 | T-bill ladder, six rungs of $2,000 | ~$460/yr |
| 3 | $8,000 | I bonds (bought at least 12 months ago) | ~$340/yr |
| **Total** | **$24,000** | | **~$960/yr (~4.0%)** |

versus $150 a year in a typical bank savings account.

## What *not* to do

- **Don't invest it in stocks or bond funds with duration.** In March 2020 a 60/40 portfolio fell ~20% in three weeks — exactly when layoffs hit. The fund's job is to be there, not to grow.
- **Don't rely on credit as the fund.** A HELOC or credit card is a fine *backup*, but lenders cut lines in recessions (2008–09 saw mass HELOC freezes) and you may not be approved when unemployed.
- **Don't count retirement accounts.** Early 401(k)/IRA withdrawals cost taxes plus a 10% penalty. (Roth IRA *contributions* can be withdrawn penalty-free and are a reasonable last-resort backstop.)
- **Don't over-fund it.** Beyond 12 months, cash is a drag; the excess belongs in your investment portfolio.
- **Don't chase teaser rates monthly.** A 0.15% difference on $20,000 is $30 a year. Switch when the gap exceeds ~0.5%.

## How to actually do it

1. Add up monthly *essential* expenses from three months of bank statements. Multiply by 3–6 (use the table to pick).
2. If you carry high-interest debt, set the target at one month for now.
3. Open a HYSA or a brokerage money-market position separate from your checking account — friction is a feature.
4. Automate a transfer every payday until the target is hit; treat it as a bill.
5. Once funded, optionally move months 2–6 into a Treasury money-market fund or a T-bill ladder with auto-roll; in a high-tax state prefer Treasuries.
6. Optionally buy I bonds each January to build a tier-3 layer over time.
7. Review yearly: expenses change, rates change, and after any withdrawal, refill before resuming extra investing.

## Key takeaways

- Target 3–6 months of *essential* expenses; more if income is unstable, less (temporarily) if you're paying 20%+ debt.
- As of September 2026, HYSAs pay up to ~4.1%, Treasury money-market funds ~3.7%, T-bills ~3.8–4.0%, I bonds 4.26% — versus ~0.6% at typical banks.
- Treasury interest is state-tax-free, which makes T-bills and Treasury funds the best after-tax choice in high-tax states.
- A tiered setup (HYSA → T-bill ladder → I bonds) adds yield and inflation protection without sacrificing access.
- Never hold the fund in stocks, and never treat credit lines or retirement accounts as the fund.
- Keep it in a separate account and automate contributions; refill after any use before investing more.
- Don't over-optimise: the good options are within half a point of each other.
