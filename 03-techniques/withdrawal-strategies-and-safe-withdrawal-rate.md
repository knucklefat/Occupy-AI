# Withdrawal Strategies and the Safe Withdrawal Rate

**In one sentence:** The "4% rule" — withdraw 4% of your starting portfolio in year one, then raise that dollar amount with inflation — has survived every 30-year U.S. retirement since 1926, but for longer horizons, today's valuations, or a retiree who can't cut spending, the honest number is closer to 3.3–3.9%, and flexible rules that adjust to markets beat any fixed percentage.

## Where the 4% rule came from

**Bengen (1994).** Financial planner William Bengen tested every 30-year retirement starting from 1926 using 50–75% stocks and found that a 4% initial withdrawal, adjusted for inflation each year, never ran out of money — the worst case being a 1966 retiree. He called it "SAFEMAX." He later raised it to about 4.5% by adding small-cap stocks, and in his 2025 book argues for roughly **4.7%** with a more diversified portfolio.

**The Trinity Study (1998).** Three Trinity University professors (Cooley, Hubbard, Walz) reframed the question as a success *rate*: with a 50/50 portfolio and inflation-adjusted withdrawals over 30 years, 4% succeeded in about **95%** of historical periods; 5% succeeded roughly 80% of the time; 3% succeeded 100%.

Both studies share assumptions worth spelling out: a 30-year horizon, U.S.-only historical returns, no fees, no taxes, no spending changes, and "success" meaning at least $1 left at the end.

## What current research says

**Morningstar** re-runs the analysis every year with forward-looking return forecasts rather than history. Its "safe" starting rate — the highest rate with a 90% chance of lasting 30 years for a 30–50% stock portfolio — has moved with bond yields and stock valuations:

| Morningstar report (for the following year) | Safe starting rate |
|---|---|
| 2021 | 3.3% |
| 2022 | 3.8% |
| 2023 | 4.0% |
| 2024 | 3.7% |
| 2025 (for 2026 retirees) | **3.9%** |

Morningstar also found a 30-year TIPS ladder could support about 4.5% inflation-adjusted withdrawals at late-2025 yields — with zero flexibility and nothing left after 30 years.

**Early Retirement Now (Karsten Jeske)**, whose Safe Withdrawal Rate Series is the most thorough public treatment, focuses on 50–60-year early-retirement horizons. His findings: the 4% rule fails materially more often over 60 years than 30; **every historical failure of 4% happened when the Shiller CAPE** (a price-to-earnings ratio using ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings) **was elevated**, as it is in 2026; and a rate of **3.25–3.5%** would have survived even the worst starting dates for very long horizons. He also finds Social Security and pensions, which most calculators ignore, can justify meaningfully higher rates.

## Sequence-of-returns risk

Two retirees can earn the same *average* return over 30 years and end up with completely different outcomes depending on the *order*. Bad returns in the first five to ten years, while you are selling shares to live on, do permanent damage: you sell more shares when they are cheap, and those shares are gone when the recovery arrives. Good early returns build a cushion that makes later crashes survivable.

**Example.** Two $1,000,000 portfolios, both withdrawing $40,000 in year one and raising it 3% a year for inflation. Both experience exactly the same 30 annual returns (average 6.7%) — just in a different order:

| | Retiree A: −30%, −20%, then +9%/yr for 28 years | Retiree B: +9%/yr for 28 years, then −20%, −30% |
|---|---|---|
| Balance after year 2 | ~$505,000 | ~$1,096,000 |
| Outcome | Runs out of money in year 21 | Ends year 30 with ~$2.5 million |

This is why every strategy below is really a way of managing the first decade.

## Strategies that manage sequence risk

**Guardrails (Guyton–Klinger, 2006).** Start at a higher rate (the original paper suggests roughly 5–5.5% with 65–80% stocks) but follow rules:
- *Capital preservation rule:* if the current withdrawal rate (this year's withdrawal ÷ current balance) rises 20% above the initial rate (e.g., from 5% to 6%), cut spending 10%.
- *Prosperity rule:* if it falls 20% below the initial rate, raise spending 10%.
- *Inflation rule:* skip the inflation raise after a year the portfolio lost money.
- No cuts in the final 15 years of the plan.

Guardrails let you spend more on average but demand real cuts in bad times. Kitces and ERN both note the cuts can be severe and long-lasting — Kitces' backtests show spending reductions of 28–54% in the worst historical periods. Newer "risk-based" guardrails (Kitces, Income Lab) trigger on the plan's *probability of success* rather than the raw withdrawal rate and produce smaller, earlier adjustments.

**Bucket strategy (Evensky; popularised by Morningstar's Christine Benz).** Hold 1–2 years of spending in cash (Bucket 1), 5–8 years in bonds (Bucket 2), and the rest in stocks (Bucket 3). Spend from cash; refill it from bonds or from stock gains in good years; never sell stocks in a crash. Mathematically it is just an asset allocation with a spending rule attached, and ERN's tests find it does not outperform a plain rebalanced portfolio — but many retirees find it psychologically far easier to hold stocks when they know the next five years of groceries are in bonds. That behavioural benefit is real.

**Bond tent / rising equity glide path (Pfau & Kitces, 2013).** Lower stock exposure to roughly 30–50% in the five years *before* and after retirement — the "retirement red zone" where sequence risk peaks — then let stocks rise gradually back to 60–70% over the following 10–15 years. Backtests show this slightly improves worst-case outcomes versus a constant allocation, because you are least exposed exactly when a crash hurts most.

**Other tools.** Delay Social Security to 70 (an inflation-protected annuity you can't outlive). Buy a single-premium immediate annuity with part of the portfolio to cover fixed expenses. Keep 1–2 years of expenses in cash so a crash never forces a sale. Use a percentage-of-portfolio rule (withdraw 4% of *current* balance each year) if you can tolerate income swings — it can never run out, but income can fall 30–40% after a crash. The IRS RMD table (balance ÷ life expectancy) is a similar, slightly more generous version.

## A pragmatic starting point

| Situation | Reasonable initial rate |
|---|---|
| 30-year horizon, will cut spending 10–20% in a bad market, has Social Security | 4.0–4.5% |
| 30-year horizon, rigid inflation-adjusted spending | 3.5–3.9% |
| 40–60-year early retirement, high CAPE | 3.25–3.5% |
| Wants zero market risk on essentials | TIPS ladder / annuity for essentials, 4–5% on the rest |

## How to actually do it

1. Split spending into **essential** and **discretionary**. Cover essentials with Social Security, pension, annuity or a TIPS ladder where possible.
2. Set the initial rate from the table above based on horizon and flexibility.
3. Choose one rule and write it down: fixed-inflation-adjusted, guardrails (with your trigger levels), or bucket refill logic.
4. Hold 1–2 years of withdrawals in cash or a money-market fund; refill it annually from whatever has done best.
5. Consider a bond tent: 40–50% stocks around the retirement date, drifting up toward 60% over a decade.
6. Once a year, recompute your current withdrawal rate (this year's spending ÷ portfolio). If a guardrail is breached, act.
7. Every five years, or after a major market move, re-run a Monte Carlo or historical tool (FICalc, cFIREsim, ERN's toolbox, Portfolio Visualizer) with your actual numbers.

## Key takeaways

- 4% inflation-adjusted lasted every historical 30-year U.S. retirement; the Trinity study put it at ~95% success for 50/50.
- Forward-looking estimates (Morningstar) say 3.7–4.0% recently; 3.9% for 2026.
- For 50–60-year horizons at high valuations, 3.25–3.5% is the historically safe figure (ERN).
- Sequence risk — bad returns in the first decade — is the main danger; average return matters less than order.
- Guardrails allow higher starting rates but can demand painful cuts; buckets help behaviour more than math.
- A bond tent (lower stocks around the retirement date, rising after) targets the exact window where crashes hurt most.
- Flexibility is worth about 0.5–1 percentage point of withdrawal rate — the more you can cut, the more you can start with.
- Social Security, pensions and annuities are the cheapest sequence-risk insurance you can buy.
