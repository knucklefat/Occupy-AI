# Rebalancing

**In one sentence:** Rebalancing is periodically selling what has grown and buying what has lagged to restore your target allocation — its main job is to control risk, not to boost returns, and the best method is a simple rule you will actually follow.

## Why bother

Suppose you set a 60/40 portfolio and never touch it. Stocks usually outgrow bonds, so the portfolio slowly turns into something you never chose. Vanguard's research gives a concrete example: a 60/40 portfolio built at the end of 2003 and never rebalanced had drifted to **about 80% stocks by the end of 2022**. That investor was carrying a third more equity risk than planned, right before whatever the next crash is.

Rebalancing forces the opposite of what feels natural. After a great year for stocks it makes you sell some; after a crash it makes you buy. That discipline is the main benefit. Any return "bonus" is secondary and unreliable.

## The evidence on a rebalancing bonus

A rebalancing bonus exists when two assets have similar expected returns, high volatility, and low correlation — you harvest the zig-zag. Between stocks and bonds, which have very different expected returns, the bonus is small and often negative: over long bull markets, the unrebalanced portfolio wins because it lets the winner run. The Bogleheads blog series "The Elusive Rebalancing Bonus" and Vanguard's 2022 paper "Rational rebalancing" both land here: rebalancing typically *lowers* the return of a stock/bond portfolio a little while lowering its risk a lot more, which is a good trade on a risk-adjusted basis. Rebalancing between similar-risk assets (U.S. vs. international stocks, or the five slices of a Golden Butterfly) is where a modest bonus is more plausible.

Vanguard's conclusion on frequency: the optimal method is "neither too frequent … nor too infrequent." Monthly or quarterly calendar rebalancing adds costs and taxes without improving risk control; waiting two-plus years lets drift get too large. Annual rebalancing, or threshold rebalancing checked periodically, sits in the sweet spot.

## Calendar vs. threshold

**Calendar rebalancing**: on a fixed date (your birthday, January 2), reset to target. Simple, predictable, easy to automate. Weakness: it ignores what the market did; you may trade when nothing has drifted, or miss a big mid-year swing.

**Threshold (band) rebalancing**: trade only when an asset class strays beyond a band. The most cited version is Larry Swedroe's **5/25 rule**: rebalance an asset class when it is off target by **5 absolute percentage points *or* 25% of its target weight, whichever is smaller**.

| Target weight | 5% absolute band | 25% relative band | Rule that applies | Rebalance when outside |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60% (U.S. stocks) | 55–65% | 45–75% | 5% absolute | 55–65% |
| 20% (international) | 15–25% | 15–25% | tie | 15–25% |
| 10% (REITs) | 5–15% | 7.5–12.5% | 25% relative | 7.5–12.5% |
| 5% (gold) | 0–10% | 3.75–6.25% | 25% relative | 3.75–6.25% |

The logic: a 5-point drift in a 60% holding is a modest change in risk, but a 5-point drift in a 5% holding means it has doubled or vanished. The relative band catches small positions.

**Hybrid (most practical)**: check the portfolio on a calendar schedule (quarterly or annually) and rebalance only if a threshold is breached. Vanguard's target-date research found this "time-and-threshold" approach gave the best balance of risk control and cost.

## Tax-efficient rebalancing

Rebalancing in a taxable account by selling winners triggers capital gains tax. Do it in this order:

1. **Direct new money.** If you are still contributing, point every contribution at the most underweight asset. A 40-year-old adding $2,000 a month to a $300,000 portfolio can correct a 5-point drift in a few months without selling anything. Turn off automatic dividend reinvestment and route dividends the same way.
2. **Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts.** Sales in an IRA or 401(k) have no tax consequence. If your bonds live in your 401(k), you can rebalance the whole household by trading only there (see [asset-location.md](asset-location.md)).
3. **Use withdrawals.** Retirees drawing on the portfolio can sell the overweight asset for spending money.
4. **Pair with tax-loss harvesting.** In a crash, selling a losing lot to buy the underweight asset locks in a deductible loss (see [tax-loss-harvesting.md](tax-loss-harvesting.md)).
5. **Sell in taxable last**, choosing the highest-cost lots (specific-ID) and preferring long-term gains over short-term.

## Worked example

Target: 60% stocks / 40% bonds on $500,000. After a strong year stocks are $360,000 (72%) and bonds $140,000 (28%). Stocks are 12 points over — well outside a 5% band.

To get back to 60/40 on the new total of $500,000, you need $300,000 in stocks and $200,000 in bonds: **sell $60,000 of stocks and buy $60,000 of bonds.** If $250,000 of the stock is in a 401(k), do the entire trade there and pay no tax. If everything is taxable and you contribute $3,000 a month, you could instead direct all contributions to bonds for 20 months — slower, but free.

Now reverse it. A crash takes stocks to $240,000 and bonds stay at $200,000 (55/45). Stocks are 5 points under. Rebalancing means selling $24,000 of bonds to buy stocks near the bottom — emotionally hard, mechanically simple. This is where the rule earns its keep.

## What to skip

- **Rebalancing every month.** Costs and taxes with no risk benefit.
- **Rebalancing tiny drifts.** A 61/39 portfolio does not need trading.
- **Rebalancing into a plan you no longer believe in.** If you find yourself unwilling to buy stocks in a crash, the problem is the allocation, not the rule — revisit [asset-allocation.md](asset-allocation.md).
- **Ignoring the whole household.** Rebalance across all accounts combined, not each account separately.

## How to actually do it

1. Write your target allocation and bands into a one-page investment policy statement (e.g., "60/40; rebalance when any class breaches 5/25").
2. Pick a check date (quarterly or your birthday) and put it in your calendar.
3. On check day, total every account in a spreadsheet, compute current percentages, and compare to bands.
4. If inside the bands, do nothing.
5. If outside, first redirect contributions and dividends, then trade in tax-advantaged accounts, then (only if needed) sell high-cost lots in taxable.
6. Log what you did and why. Repeat next check date.
7. If you own a single target-date or balanced fund, you are done — it rebalances itself.

## Key takeaways

- Rebalancing controls risk; the return "bonus" between stocks and bonds is small and often negative.
- An unrebalanced 60/40 became roughly 80/20 over 2003–2022 — drift is real and large.
- Annual, or a periodic check against 5/25 bands, is the evidence-backed sweet spot; monthly is too often.
- The 5/25 rule: trade when a class is off by 5 absolute points or 25% of its weight, whichever is smaller.
- Rebalance with new contributions and inside tax-advantaged accounts before selling anything taxable.
- Treat the household as one portfolio.
- The rule matters most in crashes, when it tells you to buy — that is the moment it pays for itself.
