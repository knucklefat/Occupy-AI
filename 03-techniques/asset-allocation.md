# Asset Allocation

**In one sentence:** Asset allocation — the split between stocks, bonds, and cash you hold — explains most of the variation in a portfolio's returns and nearly all of its risk, so it deserves more of your attention than any individual fund or stock pick.

## Why allocation matters more than picking

A famous 1986 study by Brinson, Hood and Beebower found that roughly 90% of the variation in returns across large pension funds was explained by their policy allocation, not by security selection or market timing. Later work argued about the exact number, but nobody disputes the core point: whether you are 90% stocks or 40% stocks will matter far more over 30 years than whether you picked Fund A or Fund B inside the stock sleeve.

Two forces are in tension. Stocks have the highest expected return but the deepest and longest drawdowns (a drawdown is the decline from a portfolio's previous high to its low). Bonds and cash earn less but cushion the fall. Your allocation is where you set the dial.

## Historical returns and drawdowns by mix

The table below uses Vanguard's long-run data on U.S. stock/bond mixes (1926–2019, stocks = S&P 500 and predecessors, bonds = high-quality U.S. bonds). These are nominal figures and include the Great Depression, so they are conservative on the downside.

| Stocks / Bonds | Avg. annual return | Best year | Worst year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 / 100 | 5.3% | +32.6% | −8.1% |
| 20 / 80 | 6.6% | +29.8% | −10.1% |
| 40 / 60 | 7.8% | +27.9% | −18.4% |
| 50 / 50 | 8.3% | +32.3% | −22.5% |
| 60 / 40 | 8.8% | +36.7% | −26.6% |
| 70 / 30 | 9.2% | +41.1% | −30.7% |
| 80 / 20 | 9.6% | +45.4% | −34.9% |
| 100 / 0 | 10.3% | +54.2% | −43.1% |

Notice the shape: going from 60/40 to 100/0 adds about 1.5 points of annual return but nearly doubles the worst-year loss. Peak-to-trough drawdowns are worse than single calendar years — a 100% U.S. stock portfolio fell roughly 50% in 2007–09 and took about four years to recover; a 60/40 fell about 30% and recovered in roughly two.

## Age-based rules of thumb

The oldest rule is "100 minus your age in stocks." As lifespans lengthened, advisers shifted to **110 or 120 minus age**. A 35-year-old using 120-minus-age holds 85% stocks; a 65-year-old holds 55%.

These rules are crude but useful because they encode the one thing that really should change with age: **human capital**. When you are young, your future paychecks are a huge, bond-like asset, so your investment portfolio can be almost all stocks. As you approach retirement that stream shrinks, and the portfolio has to carry the load.

Bogleheads' older heuristic — "age in bonds" — is now generally considered too conservative for most people given longer retirements, but the underlying question is the right one: how much of a crash can you absorb without changing your plan?

## Glide paths and target-date funds

A **glide path** is a schedule that lowers stock exposure over time. **Target-date funds** (TDFs) automate this: you buy the fund labeled with the year you plan to retire and it rebalances and de-risks for you.

Vanguard's Target Retirement series, the largest in the U.S., holds about 90% stocks until roughly 25 years before the target date, then slides to about 50% at the target year and lands at roughly 30% stocks seven years later, where it stays. Its stock sleeve is fixed at 60% U.S. / 40% international, and it adds short-term TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) from about age 60. Fidelity and T. Rowe Price glide paths are somewhat more aggressive; Schwab's is similar.

TDFs are the best default for anyone who does not want to think about this. Their weaknesses: one-size-fits-all risk, mild tax inefficiency in taxable accounts (they hold bonds), and the "2045" label tells you nothing about the fund's actual risk — always look at the glide path.

## Model portfolios worth knowing

| Portfolio | Allocation | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **60/40** | 60% stocks, 40% bonds | The institutional benchmark; balanced risk |
| **80/20** | 80% stocks, 20% bonds | Common for accumulators under ~50 |
| **Three-Fund (Bogleheads)** | Total U.S. stock, total international stock, total bond — in any ratio | Simplest complete portfolio; e.g. 50/30/20 |
| **Swensen (Yale)** | 30% U.S. stocks, 15% developed intl., 5% emerging, 20% REITs, 15% Treasuries, 15% TIPS | Heavy real assets, two kinds of bonds for two kinds of crises |
| **Golden Butterfly** | 20% each: U.S. total stock, U.S. small-cap value, long Treasuries, short Treasuries, gold | Built for consistency across inflation/deflation/boom/bust |
| **Permanent Portfolio (Browne)** | 25% each: stocks, long Treasuries, cash, gold | Lowest drawdowns, lowest growth |

Portfolio Charts' data (1970–2025) shows why some people accept lower expected returns for smoother rides: the Golden Butterfly returned about 6.3% a year after inflation with a deepest drawdown of only ~18% and never spent more than five years underwater — versus a 100% stock portfolio's ~50% drawdowns. The price is lagging badly in long bull markets like 2010–2021.

## How to pick

Ask three questions:

1. **Need** — What return do you need to hit your goal? If a 60/40 gets you there, you don't need 90/10.
2. **Ability** — How long until you need the money, and how stable is your income? A tenured professor can hold more stock than a commissioned salesperson.
3. **Willingness** — What is the largest one-year loss you could watch without selling? Take the worst-year column above, multiply by your portfolio in dollars, and look at the number. If seeing "−$180,000" makes you sick, you are not an 80/20 investor no matter what a calculator says.

The most expensive mistake is not a slightly wrong allocation; it is picking an aggressive one and abandoning it in a crash. An allocation you will hold through 2008 beats an "optimal" one you sell in the bottom.

## How to actually do it

1. Write down your goal, horizon, and the max drawdown (in dollars) you can stomach.
2. Start from a rule of thumb (110 or 120 minus age) and adjust for job stability, pension or Social Security coverage, and temperament.
3. Choose a structure: a single target-date fund, or a three-fund portfolio with your chosen stock/bond split and roughly 20–40% of stocks international.
4. Implement with low-cost index funds (expense ratios under ~0.10% are standard).
5. Write a one-page investment policy statement recording the allocation and your rebalancing rule (see [rebalancing.md](rebalancing.md)).
6. Revisit only on life changes (marriage, retirement, inheritance) or every five years — not after a headline.

## Key takeaways

- Your stock/bond split, not fund selection, drives most of your long-run return and nearly all of your risk.
- Every extra 10% in stocks has historically added roughly 0.4–0.5% per year and about 4 points of worst-year loss.
- Rules like "120 minus age" are starting points that encode declining human capital, not laws.
- Target-date funds are an excellent default; check the glide path, not the year on the label.
- Simple three-fund portfolios capture nearly all the benefit of complex ones.
- Diversified "all-weather" mixes (Golden Butterfly, Permanent Portfolio) trade growth for far shallower drawdowns.
- The right allocation is the most aggressive one you will actually hold through a 40% crash.
