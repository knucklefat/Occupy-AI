# Market Crash Playbook

**In one sentence:** A step-by-step "break glass" plan for a 20%+ market decline — do nothing first, then re-read your plan, then execute the small number of actions that actually add value (rebalance, harvest losses, convert to Roth, buy from a pre-written list), while avoiding the media and behaviors that cost investors the most.

**Read this now, while nothing is wrong.** You will not read it carefully during a crash, so the instructions are short and numbered. The people who came out of 2000–02, 2008–09, March 2020, and 2022 ahead did not predict them; they simply followed a plan like this one.

**Some context to keep on hand:** Since 1950, the US stock market has dropped 20% or more roughly once every 6–7 years, and 10% or more about once a year. Every single decline has been followed by a new high. The average bear market lasts about a year; the average recovery to prior highs, about two. Nobody has reliably timed the bottom, including the professionals who are paid to.

---

## Phase 0 — Before the crash (do this today)

- [ ] Your IPS is written and includes a "what I will do in a crash" section. If not, write it now: [investment-policy-statement-template.md](investment-policy-statement-template.md).
- [ ] Your emergency fund is 3–6 months of expenses in cash. This is what makes "do nothing" possible.
- [ ] Money needed in the next 5 years is not in stocks.
- [ ] You know your rebalancing bands and which accounts to trade in.
- [ ] You have identified tax-loss-harvesting pairs for every taxable holding (e.g., one total-market fund ↔ another provider's).
- [ ] You have a **buying list**: 3–5 broad funds (or, if you pick stocks, a short list of businesses you'd love to own at a lower price, with the price written down).
- [ ] You've told your partner/family what the plan is so nobody panics on your behalf.
- [ ] You've written down, in one sentence, how you felt during the last crash and what you did. Keep it with this playbook.

---

## Phase 1 — The first 48 hours: do nothing

*Why:* The most expensive trades in history were placed on the worst days. Selling after a drop locks in the loss and requires you to make a *second* correct decision (when to get back in) that almost nobody makes. The urge to act is real; the action is not required.

1. **Do not log in to your brokerage.** Balance-checking during crashes is the single biggest driver of panic selling.
2. **Do not sell anything.** Not "just a little," not "to have some dry powder."
3. **Do not read market news.** See "What not to read," below.
4. **Do something physical.** Walk, exercise, cook. Decision quality is terrible while stressed.
5. **Re-read your IPS**, especially the "never" list and the crash section.
6. **Re-read this playbook.** Then close the laptop.

Set a calendar reminder for 48 hours from now labeled "Phase 2." Until then, this page is finished.

## Phase 2 — Days 3–14: the small set of useful actions

Work through these in order. Each one is a *rule*, not a judgment call; you decided them in advance.

### Step 1: Confirm your plan is unchanged
- [ ] Goals, horizon, and risk tolerance are the same as they were a month ago. (The market changed; your life didn't.) If your *life* changed — job loss, health event — that's a legitimate IPS review, done in writing.
- [ ] Every automatic contribution is still running. If anything, this is when to *raise* it.

### Step 2: Check rebalancing bands
- [ ] Calculate current allocation across all accounts. A 25% stock drop usually pushes a 75/25 portfolio to roughly 69/31 — often past a ±5% band.
- [ ] If outside the band: rebalance **into** stocks, in tax-advantaged accounts first. This is the mechanism that makes crashes profitable for disciplined investors: you are systematically buying what fell.
- [ ] If not outside the band: do nothing here. Don't invent a reason to trade.

### Step 3: Harvest tax losses (taxable accounts only)
*Why:* A crash is the best harvesting opportunity you'll get. Realized losses offset future gains and up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year, and carry forward indefinitely — all while you stay fully invested.
- [ ] For each taxable holding with a meaningful loss (say, over $1,000 or 5%), sell it and immediately buy its pre-identified partner fund.
- [ ] Turn off automatic dividend reinvestment on the sold fund for 31 days; check that no automatic purchase of the same fund happens in *any* account (IRA, 401(k), spouse's account) 30 days before or after — that's a wash sale and disallows the loss.
- [ ] Log every harvest: date, fund, loss amount, replacement. You'll need it at tax time and for the 30-day window.
- [ ] Don't harvest tiny losses; the spread and complexity aren't worth it.

### Step 4: Consider a Roth conversion
*Why:* Converting pre-tax IRA/401(k) money to Roth means paying income tax on the amount converted. When balances are down 30%, you convert the same *shares* for 30% less tax, and all the recovery happens tax-free. Most valuable if your income is also low this year.
- [ ] Estimate this year's taxable income and the room left in your current bracket.
- [ ] If there's room and you have cash *outside* the IRA to pay the tax, convert up to the top of the bracket. Conversions are irreversible — don't overshoot into a higher bracket.
- [ ] Check side effects: ACA subsidies, IRMAA (two-year lookback), financial aid.

### Step 5: Deploy the buying list
- [ ] If you have new cash beyond the emergency fund (bonus, savings above target), invest it now, per your allocation. Not "half now, half if it drops more" — unless that's the plan you wrote in advance. Lump-sum investing has historically beaten waiting about two-thirds of the time.
- [ ] If you hold individual stocks: check the buying list. Any names below your written buy price? Run the [pre-trade-checklist.md](pre-trade-checklist.md) and size per IPS. A crash doesn't suspend your position limits.
- [ ] Do **not** raid the emergency fund to buy. That's what turns a market crash into a personal crisis if a layoff follows (they often do).

### Step 6: Lock it down
- [ ] Write a three-line note: the date, what you did, and how you felt. Next crash, this is the most valuable paragraph you'll read.
- [ ] Set the next check-in date (below). Then stop.

## Phase 3 — Weeks 3 onward: waiting well

- **Check-in cadence:** monthly, on a fixed date, 15 minutes: bands, harvest opportunities, automation. Nothing else. If the market drops another 10%+ between check-ins, that's a *rebalancing* signal at the next check-in, not an emergency.
- **New lows are normal.** Bear markets have multiple legs down and sharp rallies that fail. None of that changes your rules.
- **The recovery will feel wrong.** Markets usually turn up while the news is still terrible. If you wait for "clarity," you will miss the best days — and missing the 10 best days over 20 years has historically cut returns roughly in half. The best days cluster right next to the worst ones.
- **Raise contributions if you can.** Every dollar invested down 30% buys 43% more shares than it did at the top.
- **Do the annual checkup on schedule.** Crashes don't move the calendar.

## What not to read, watch, or do

- **Financial TV and market-crash headlines.** Their business is attention; volatility sells. No actionable information for a long-term investor appears there.
- **Social media investment content**, including "I told you so" threads, doom charts, and whoever's calling for a further 50% drop. Confident forecasters are wrong about as often as coin flips and are never held accountable.
- **Your account balance more than once a month.** Loss aversion means each check hurts roughly twice as much as an equivalent gain feels good.
- **Forecasts, "where's the bottom" articles, or comparisons to 1929.** Nobody knows. Your plan doesn't require knowing.
- **Anyone selling a product that "protects against the next leg down"** — annuities, structured notes, gold newsletters, crypto, hedge strategies. Crashes are peak season for fear-based sales. See [red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md](red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md).
- **Trading "to feel in control."** Moving to cash, buying puts, or switching to a "safer" fund mid-crash are all ways of selling low.
- **Debating with people who sold.** They need to be right; you don't.

**What to read instead:** your IPS, this playbook, a history of past bear markets and recoveries, and something entirely unrelated to money.

## If you already sold

It happens. The recovery plan:

1. Don't wait for a "better re-entry." Every day out of the market is a coin flip you're not being paid for.
2. Re-invest per your allocation — all at once if you can stomach it, or on a fixed short schedule (e.g., a third every two weeks) that you write down and follow no matter what.
3. Add the experience to the "never" list in your IPS, with the date and the dollar cost.
4. Reduce your stock allocation *at the next scheduled review* if the experience revealed that your willingness to take risk is lower than you thought — not now.

---

## The one-page summary to tape inside the cupboard

1. Don't log in. Don't sell. Don't read. Wait 48 hours.
2. Re-read the IPS.
3. Rebalance if bands are breached (buy stocks, tax-advantaged first).
4. Harvest losses in taxable; swap to partner funds; watch the 30-day rule.
5. Roth-convert if income is low and cash is available for the tax.
6. Invest new cash per plan. Never the emergency fund.
7. Write three lines about what you did and felt.
8. Check again in one month. Not before.
