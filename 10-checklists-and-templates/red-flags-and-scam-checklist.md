# Red Flags and Scam Checklist

**In one sentence:** How to recognize the handful of patterns that nearly every investment scam shares — guaranteed returns, urgency, secrecy, unverifiable people — and the three free government databases (BrokerCheck, IAPD, EDGAR) that let you check any person, firm, or security in five minutes before you send a dollar.

Investment fraud is not a niche risk. FINRA Foundation research finds that most adults are exposed to at least one fraudulent pitch a year, and the SEC notes that unregistered, unlicensed people commit most of the investment fraud in the United States. The scams change costume — boiler rooms, then email, then Ponzi hedge funds, then crypto, then "AI trading bots" — but the underlying script is remarkably stable. Learn the script and you're protected against versions that haven't been invented yet.

**The single most useful habit:** never invest with anyone you haven't looked up in the databases in Section 4, and never invest in anything you can't find in them.

---

## Section 1 — Universal red flags (any one is enough to walk away)

*Why this matters:* These are the tells that regulators (SEC, FINRA, NASAA) list over and over because they appear in nearly every enforcement case. Legitimate investments don't need them.

- [ ] **Guaranteed or "risk-free" returns.** All investing involves risk. Anyone guaranteeing a return above what Treasuries pay is either lying or doesn't understand what they're selling. Both are disqualifying.
- [ ] **Returns that are too consistent.** A strategy that goes up every month, through every market, is the signature of a Ponzi scheme (Madoff's returns were famously smooth). Real strategies have bad months.
- [ ] **High return with low risk.** These move together. A pitch claiming to have broken that link is claiming to have broken finance.
- [ ] **Urgency.** "Only three spots left," "closes Friday," "get in before the announcement." Pressure exists to stop you from checking. A real opportunity is still there next week.
- [ ] **Secrecy or exclusivity.** "Don't tell anyone," "invitation only," "the banks don't want you to know." Secrecy prevents the second opinion that would expose the scheme.
- [ ] **Unsolicited contact.** Cold calls, DMs, texts to the wrong number that turn friendly, "accidental" WhatsApp messages, LinkedIn connection requests from "portfolio managers." Legitimate professionals don't prospect this way.
- [ ] **Complexity you can't understand.** "Proprietary algorithm," "arbitrage across liquidity pools," "structured yield." If they can't explain it simply, either they don't understand it or they don't want you to.
- [ ] **The person selling it also holds your money.** Real advisors use an independent custodian (Schwab, Fidelity, Pershing, etc.) that sends you statements directly. If checks go to the advisor's own company or a personal account, stop.
- [ ] **Asked to pay in crypto, gift cards, wire transfer to an individual, or through a payment app.** These are irreversible. No regulated investment is bought this way.
- [ ] **Trouble withdrawing.** Delays, new "fees" or "taxes" required to release your money, or pressure to "roll it over" instead of cashing out. This is the moment the scam becomes visible — do not send more money to "unlock" funds.
- [ ] **Recruitment.** You're rewarded for bringing in others. That's a pyramid, whatever the product.
- [ ] **Claims of special access, connections, or insider knowledge.** Trading on real inside information is a crime; claiming to is a scam.
- [ ] **Credential soup.** Impressive-sounding titles or certifications you've never heard of. Real ones (CFP, CFA, CPA) are verifiable at the issuing body.
- [ ] **It found you through a celebrity, an ad, or a deepfake video.** Public figures' likenesses are routinely faked in investment ads. Assume any celebrity-endorsed investment pitch is fraudulent until proven otherwise.

## Section 2 — Field guide to common scam types

### Pump-and-dump (and its crypto cousin, the "rug pull")
Promoters quietly accumulate a thinly traded stock or token, then flood social media, newsletters, and chat groups with hype ("massive contract coming," "next 100×"). Retail buyers push the price up; promoters sell into the demand; the price collapses. **Tells:** microcap or penny stock, sudden coordinated enthusiasm from accounts you don't know, no revenue, promotional press releases, a "community" that attacks skeptics. **Defense:** never buy anything because of a chat group, and check EDGAR for the company's actual filings — most have none or show near-zero revenue.

### Ponzi scheme
Returns to earlier investors are paid from new investors' money, not from any real profit. Works until inflows stop. **Tells:** smooth positive returns, vague strategy, difficulty withdrawing, the promoter's own custody of funds, no audited statements from a real firm. **Defense:** verify the custodian and the auditor independently; if you can't, don't invest.

### Affinity fraud
The fraudster is (or pretends to be) a member of your community — church, ethnic group, profession, alumni network, military, retirement community — and uses that trust as the pitch. Often the early investors are real and enthusiastic because they're being paid with later money. **Tells:** the recommendation comes through trust rather than documentation; "he's one of us"; group pressure. **Defense:** check the person exactly as you would a stranger. Fraudsters target communities *because* members skip verification.

### Romance / "pig butchering" scams
A stranger builds a relationship over weeks or months (dating app, wrong-number text, social media), then introduces a "sure thing" investment — usually a crypto platform they "trade on." A fake app shows fake profits. Withdrawals are blocked until you pay "taxes." Losses are frequently life savings. **Tells:** the relationship moves to investing; the platform isn't a known exchange; they coach you on how to send money. **Defense:** never invest based on a relationship that began online; never download a trading app from a link someone sent you.

### Unregistered advisors and fake firms
Someone calling themselves a "wealth manager" or "fund manager" who isn't registered anywhere, or a website cloning a real firm's name and branding with a slightly different URL. **Defense:** Section 4. Every legitimate US advisor, broker, and firm appears in IAPD or BrokerCheck. Call the real firm using the number on its official site, not the one on the pitch.

### Crypto and forex "gurus" and signal sellers
Sellers of "signals," "bots," "copy trading," or "managed accounts" promising outsized returns, usually with screenshots of lifestyle and P&L. Forex and crypto retail trading are zero- or negative-sum after costs; the guru's income is your subscription, not their trading. **Tells:** lifestyle marketing, unverifiable track record, "DM me to start," refund resistance, a Discord/Telegram funnel. **Defense:** ask for an audited or third-party-verified track record over 5+ years; there won't be one.

### "Trading academies," MLM-style courses, and mentorship funnels
A cheap webinar leads to a paid course, which leads to a "mastermind," which leads to recruiting others for commissions. The product is the sales funnel. Several such "academies" have been shut down by regulators as pyramid schemes. **Tells:** compensation for recruiting, escalating tiers, emphasis on mindset over method, no verifiable trading results, testimonials from people who are also sellers. **Defense:** if income comes from recruiting, it's an MLM regardless of the "education" wrapper.

### Precious metals, "self-directed IRA" pushes, and fear marketing
Aggressive advertising (often around market crashes or political events) to move retirement accounts into overpriced coins, collectibles, or illiquid private deals via a "self-directed IRA." **Tells:** fear-based pitch, high markups hidden in the spread, storage fees, pressure to move an entire IRA. **Defense:** compare the price to spot metal prices; check the dealer's complaint record; ask what it would cost to sell tomorrow.

### AI-flavored fraud
Regulators (SEC, FINRA, NASAA) have issued specific alerts: "AI-powered" trading platforms with fake performance, deepfake videos of executives or celebrities endorsing products, and voice-cloned calls impersonating family members or advisors. **Defense:** the AI label changes nothing about the checklist above. Guaranteed returns are still fake; unregistered platforms are still unregistered.

### Advance-fee and "recovery" scams
After a loss, you're contacted by someone offering to recover your money for an upfront fee. This is usually the same scammers (or buyers of their victim list). **Defense:** legitimate recovery goes through law enforcement and regulators, none of whom charge fees.

## Section 3 — Softer red flags in legitimate-looking advice

These aren't crimes, but they cost investors real money. Treat them as reasons to slow down.

- [ ] An advisor who won't put in writing that they're a **fiduciary** (legally required to act in your interest) at all times.
- [ ] Compensation via **commissions** on products rather than a flat fee, hourly rate, or a disclosed percentage — creates an incentive to sell you what pays them.
- [ ] Recommending **annuities, whole/universal life insurance, non-traded REITs, structured notes, or private placements** as core holdings for an ordinary investor. Each has a niche use and a large commission.
- [ ] **Performance-chasing pitches:** "this fund returned 40% last year." Last year's return is one of the weakest predictors of next year's.
- [ ] **Vague or missing fee disclosure.** Ask for the total annual cost, in dollars, of everything. A professional can answer.
- [ ] **Discouraging you from a second opinion** or from reading the prospectus.
- [ ] **Newsletter and "stock-picking service" marketing** built on a handful of cherry-picked past calls.

## Section 4 — How to verify (do this every time)

*Why this matters:* These databases are free, public, and take minutes. The SEC's own investor bulletin puts "check registration" as the single most effective anti-fraud step.

### Verify a person or firm
- [ ] **FINRA BrokerCheck** — [brokercheck.finra.org](https://brokercheck.finra.org/). Covers brokers and brokerage firms: registration status, employment history, licenses, and disclosures (customer complaints, arbitrations, regulatory actions, criminal matters, bankruptcies).
- [ ] **SEC IAPD (Investment Adviser Public Disclosure)** — [adviserinfo.sec.gov](https://www.adviserinfo.sec.gov/). Covers investment advisers and their firms. Read the firm's **Form ADV Part 2** ("brochure"): it discloses fees, conflicts, disciplinary history, and what they actually do. Part 2B covers the individual.
- [ ] **Investor.gov search** — [investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/) has a combined search that queries both, plus a hotline (800-732-0330).
- [ ] **State securities regulator** — find yours via [NASAA](https://www.nasaa.org/). States license many smaller advisors and have complaint records not in federal databases.
- [ ] **Credential check:** CFP at [cfp.net/verify](https://www.cfp.net/verify-a-cfp-professional); CFA at [cfainstitute.org](https://www.cfainstitute.org/); CPA at the state board of accountancy.
- [ ] **What to look for:** does the person exist in the database *at all*? Does the firm name match exactly? Are there disclosures? (One old customer dispute isn't necessarily disqualifying; a pattern is.) Is the address real? Does the phone number on the official filing match the one you were given?

### Verify a security or fund
- [ ] **SEC EDGAR** — [sec.gov/edgar](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/). Every public company and registered fund files here: annual reports (10-K), quarterly reports (10-Q), prospectuses, insider transactions (Form 4). If a "public company" has no filings, it's not public in any meaningful sense. If a "fund" has no registration statement, it's not a registered fund.
- [ ] **Registered offerings vs private placements:** private offerings (Form D) are legal but only for accredited investors, have no SEC review, and are the vehicle for most affinity and Ponzi fraud. Be extremely careful.
- [ ] **Ticker check:** a real listed stock has a ticker on a major exchange (NYSE, Nasdaq). "OTC Pink" or "Expert Market" tickers carry near-zero disclosure requirements and are the home of pump-and-dumps.
- [ ] **Fund lookup:** any mutual fund or ETF should appear in EDGAR and on Morningstar with a prospectus and expense ratio.

### Verify a crypto platform or product
- [ ] Is the exchange registered with FinCEN as a money services business and licensed in your state? Is it a known, long-established name?
- [ ] Did you find it yourself, or did someone send you a link or an app? (The latter is the pig-butchering pattern.)
- [ ] Can you withdraw a small test amount immediately, with no "fee" first?
- [ ] Check the SEC and CFTC enforcement pages and the state regulator's list of unregistered crypto entities.

### Check the warning lists
- [ ] [SEC Investor Alerts and Bulletins](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins)
- [ ] [FINRA Investor Insights and alerts](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights)
- [ ] [NASAA investor alerts](https://www.nasaa.org/investor-education/investor-alerts/)
- [ ] The SEC's **PAUSE list** of unregistered entities and impersonators, searchable on sec.gov.

## Section 5 — Protecting your accounts

- [ ] **Two-factor authentication** (app-based or hardware key, not SMS) on every financial account and the email tied to them.
- [ ] **Credit freeze** at all three bureaus — [Equifax](https://www.equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-freeze/), [Experian](https://www.experian.com/freeze/center.html), [TransUnion](https://www.transunion.com/credit-freeze). Free, reversible, and it stops most identity-based fraud.
- [ ] **IRS Identity Protection PIN** and a **my Social Security** account, claimed before a fraudster claims them.
- [ ] **Trusted contact person** on each brokerage account — someone the firm can call if they suspect exploitation.
- [ ] **Account alerts** for logins, password changes, address changes, and withdrawals.
- [ ] **Never act on an inbound call or message about your account.** Hang up, and call the number on the back of your card or on the official website.
- [ ] **Talk before you send.** Tell one trusted person about any investment over a threshold you set (say, $5,000) before you make it. Scams depend on isolation.

## Section 6 — If you've been scammed

1. Stop sending money — including to anyone offering to "recover" it.
2. Document everything: names, numbers, URLs, wallet addresses, screenshots, transaction records.
3. Report to: [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/tcr), [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/have-problem/file-complaint), [FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/), [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/), your state securities regulator, and your bank/broker (some wires can be recalled if reported within hours).
4. If your identity was used: freeze credit, file at [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/).
5. Be aware you are now on a "sucker list" that gets sold; expect more pitches and recovery scams.
6. Don't let shame delay the report. These schemes are engineered by professionals; being fooled by one says nothing about your intelligence.
