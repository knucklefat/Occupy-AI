# ETF and Fund Selection Checklist

**In one sentence:** Twenty-eight questions to ask before buying any ETF or mutual fund, so that you own exactly the exposure you intended at the lowest total cost — because the difference between two funds that "track the same thing" can quietly cost you a percent a year.

**Jargon defined once:** An *ETF* (exchange-traded fund) trades on an exchange like a stock; a *mutual fund* is bought and sold once a day at its closing value. An *index fund* is either of these that mechanically tracks a published list of securities (the index). *Expense ratio* is the annual fee as a percent of assets. *Tracking difference* is how far the fund's actual return fell short of its index over a period; *tracking error* is how much that gap bounces around. *AUM* is assets under management — the fund's total size. *Bid-ask spread* is the gap between what buyers pay and sellers receive on the exchange — a hidden cost every time you trade.

For most portfolios the right answer is a handful of broad, cheap index funds, and this checklist is fast. It gets slower — and more important — the narrower or more exotic the fund.

---

## Part A — What does it actually hold?

*Why this matters:* Two funds with "Emerging Markets" in the name can differ on whether they include South Korea, how much China they hold, and whether small companies are in. You are buying the index, not the name.

- [ ] 1. **I've identified the exact index** the fund tracks (e.g., "CRSP US Total Market" vs "S&P 500" vs "MSCI USA") and read the one-page index methodology.
- [ ] 2. **The index is broad and rules-based** — many holdings, a transparent inclusion rule, market-cap weighted (or I have a specific reason to want something else). Narrow or "thematic" indexes are marketing products first.
- [ ] 3. **Number of holdings and top-10 concentration** are what I expect. A "diversified" fund with 40% in its top 10 names is a concentrated bet.
- [ ] 4. **Sector, country, and company-size weights** match my intent. I checked, not assumed.
- [ ] 5. **Overlap with what I already own** is low. Two funds that both hold Apple, Microsoft, and Nvidia at the top are not diversification. (Free overlap tools exist; use one.)
- [ ] 6. **Replication method:** full physical replication (owns every security) is simplest. "Sampling" is fine for large bond indexes. *Synthetic* (swap-based) funds carry counterparty risk and need a reason.
- [ ] 7. **The index rebalances on a sensible schedule** (quarterly/annually) and doesn't churn — high index turnover means higher trading costs and, in taxable accounts, capital gains distributions.

## Part B — What does it cost?

*Why this matters:* Cost is the single most reliable predictor of a fund's future relative return, which is why Morningstar's own research found expense ratio to be its best single screen. Over 30 years, a 1% annual fee consumes roughly a quarter of your ending wealth; see [useful-formulas-and-rules-of-thumb.md](useful-formulas-and-rules-of-thumb.md).

- [ ] 8. **Expense ratio** is competitive for the category: broad US stock or bond index funds under 0.10%; international under 0.15%; specialty or factor funds under 0.35%; anything above 0.50% needs a written justification.
- [ ] 9. **Tracking difference over 3, 5, and 10 years** is close to the expense ratio (that's the expected shortfall) — not much worse. A fund that lags its index by 0.5% with a 0.10% fee is leaking money somewhere.
- [ ] 10. **No loads, 12b-1 fees, or transaction fees** at my brokerage. Check the fund's prospectus fee table and your broker's fee schedule — "no commission" and "no transaction fee" are different things for mutual funds.
- [ ] 11. **Bid-ask spread** (for ETFs) is a few basis points for large funds. If the spread is regularly over 0.10%, every round trip costs real money — matters most if you trade often or rebalance in taxable accounts.
- [ ] 12. **Premium/discount to NAV** is normally near zero. Persistent large premiums or discounts (common in illiquid bond or international ETFs) mean you might pay more than the holdings are worth.
- [ ] 13. **Securities lending:** the fund lends out holdings and earns income. I checked (a) whether the income goes to fund holders or the manager, and (b) how the collateral is invested. Lending that returns most revenue to the fund can *offset* the expense ratio; lending with aggressive collateral reinvestment adds a small hidden risk.

## Part C — Is it big and liquid enough to survive?

*Why this matters:* Small funds get closed. A closure forces a sale — a taxable event in a brokerage account and a disruption in any account. State Street's own due-diligence checklist puts provider stability and liquidity near the top.

- [ ] 14. **AUM is above roughly $100 million** (many closures happen below $50M) and the fund has existed at least 3 years, ideally through a bear market.
- [ ] 15. **Average daily trading volume** is high enough that my typical order is a tiny fraction of it. For ETFs, remember that the liquidity of the *underlying* holdings matters more than the ETF's own volume — a low-volume ETF on large-cap US stocks is fine; a low-volume ETF on frontier-market bonds is not.
- [ ] 16. **The provider is established** (Vanguard, iShares/BlackRock, Schwab, Fidelity, State Street/SPDR, Invesco, Dimensional, Avantis, etc.) with a track record of not closing funds or hiking fees.
- [ ] 17. **Fund flows are stable or positive.** Sustained outflows raise closure risk and can force selling that creates capital gains distributions.

## Part D — Tax efficiency (taxable accounts)

*Why this matters:* In an IRA or 401(k) none of this section matters. In a taxable account it can matter as much as the expense ratio. ETFs are generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds because of the in-kind creation/redemption mechanism, which lets them avoid distributing capital gains.

- [ ] 18. **Capital gains distribution history:** the fund has distributed little or no capital gains over the last 5–10 years. (Look at the fund's "distributions" page. Actively managed mutual funds are frequent offenders.)
- [ ] 19. **Structure:** for taxable accounts I prefer an ETF, or a Vanguard mutual fund (which shares an ETF share class and gets the same tax treatment), over a conventional mutual fund.
- [ ] 20. **Dividend character:** most dividends are "qualified" (taxed at lower long-term capital gains rates). REIT, high-yield bond, and some international funds throw off non-qualified income and belong in tax-advantaged accounts.
- [ ] 21. **Foreign tax credit:** for international funds held in taxable accounts, I can claim the credit for foreign taxes withheld (you cannot in an IRA — a modest argument for holding international in taxable).
- [ ] 22. **A tax-loss-harvesting partner exists:** a similar-but-not-identical fund I could swap into during a downturn without triggering the wash-sale rule.

## Part E — Structure and fine print

*Why this matters:* A few structures behave in ways that surprise people. None of these are common in a simple index portfolio, but every one has cost investors real money.

- [ ] 23. **It's a plain '40 Act fund** (registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940), not an ETN (an unsecured bank note with credit risk), a commodity pool (K-1 tax forms, different tax treatment), a grantor trust, or a leveraged/inverse product.
- [ ] 24. **Not leveraged or inverse.** These reset daily and decay over time; they are trading tools, not investments, regardless of what the name implies.
- [ ] 25. **Not a "covered call," "buffered," "defined outcome," or "income" product** unless I fully understand what return I'm giving up in exchange for what I'm getting. These usually cap upside and charge more for it.
- [ ] 26. **If actively managed:** the manager has a long tenure, a clearly stated process, a fee that reflects the low odds of outperformance, and I have a specific reason to expect this fund to beat its cheap index alternative after fees. (Morningstar's research: most don't over 10+ years.) Passing this item honestly is rare.
- [ ] 27. **I've read the summary prospectus** — specifically the principal risks and the fee table — and nothing surprised me.
- [ ] 28. **It fits a role in my IPS.** I can name which target allocation bucket this fund fills and confirm I'm not adding a fourth fund that does the job of one I already own.

## Quick decision table

| If you need… | Typical good choice | Expense ratio ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| US stocks, one fund | Total US Stock Market index (or S&P 500) | 0.05% |
| International stocks | Total International Stock index (developed + emerging) | 0.15% |
| Everything in one | Total World Stock index | 0.10% |
| Bonds | Total Bond Market index (or Treasury-only) | 0.05% |
| One-decision portfolio | Target Date index fund | 0.20% |
