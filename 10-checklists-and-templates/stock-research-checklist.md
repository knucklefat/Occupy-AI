# Stock Research Checklist

**In one sentence:** A 55-item checklist, drawn from the working checklists of Buffett, Munger, Pabrai, Spier, and Lynch, that forces you to answer the questions that actually predict whether an individual stock purchase will work out — before you own it, not after.

**How to use it:** Work through every section for any stock you're seriously considering. A single "no" in the Business, Financials, or Valuation sections is usually disqualifying. The point is not to find reasons to buy — it's to find the reason *not* to, before the market finds it for you. Guy Spier built his checklist from post-mortems of his own losses; Mohnish Pabrai's runs to about 100 items built the same way. Start with this one and add an item every time you lose money for a reason you could have caught.

**A reminder before you begin:** the default for most people is a low-cost index fund. Picking individual stocks is optional, difficult, and only worth it if you enjoy the work and cap the money at risk. If you're doing this with more than 10–20% of your portfolio, re-read your IPS first.

## Section 1 — Can I understand this business? (Circle of competence)

*Why this matters:* Buffett's first acquisition criterion is "simple businesses" — his reasoning is that if you can't understand how the company makes money, you can't judge whether it will keep making it. Lynch's version: you should be able to explain the "story" to a 10-year-old in two minutes. Most losses in individual stocks trace back to owning something the buyer never actually understood.

- [ ] 1. I can write, in three plain sentences, what the company sells, who pays for it, and why they choose it over alternatives.
- [ ] 2. I can name the company's three largest competitors and explain why customers pick one over another.
- [ ] 3. I know what the business will look like in 10 years with reasonable confidence (a "slow-changing" industry, in Pabrai's phrase). If the honest answer is "no idea," stop here.
- [ ] 4. I understand how the company gets paid: one-time sales, subscriptions, commissions, interest spread, advertising, etc. — and which of these is growing.
- [ ] 5. I have read the most recent annual report (10-K) — at least the business description, risk factors, and management discussion sections — not just a summary of it.
- [ ] 6. I have used, seen, or can observe the product or service, or talked to someone who has (Lynch's "invest in what you know" — used as a starting point for research, never a substitute for it).

## Section 2 — Is there a durable competitive advantage? (Moat)

*Why this matters:* High returns attract competitors. A business earning great margins without a moat will see those margins competed away; the stock price will follow. Buffett's ideal is a business whose advantage would survive even a mediocre manager. Morningstar identifies five moat sources: intangible assets (brands, patents, licenses), switching costs, network effects, cost advantages, and efficient scale.

- [ ] 7. I can name the specific moat source(s) and explain the mechanism — not "great brand" but "customers pay 30% more for this brand and have for 20 years."
- [ ] 8. Evidence of the moat shows up in the numbers: return on invested capital consistently above ~12–15%, gross margins stable or rising over 10 years, market share stable or growing.
- [ ] 9. The company has pricing power: it has raised prices faster than inflation without losing customers. (Spier added this item after a loss in a company that couldn't.)
- [ ] 10. The moat is getting wider, or at least not narrowing. What would a well-funded competitor need to do to take these customers? Why haven't they?
- [ ] 11. The business creates value for everyone in its ecosystem (customers, suppliers, employees), not just for shareholders at their expense — Spier's "win-win" test, because businesses that extract from their partners eventually lose them.
- [ ] 12. No single customer, supplier, or government contract accounts for more than ~10–15% of revenue.

## Section 3 — Financial strength

*Why this matters:* Berkshire's criteria specify "good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" and "consistent earning power" — not projections. Debt is what turns a bad year into bankruptcy; consistent past earnings are the only evidence you have that the business can earn through a cycle. Read at least 10 years of numbers; a 3-year window flatters almost anyone.

- [ ] 13. Revenue has grown over 10 years, and I understand *why* (volume, price, acquisitions, currency).
- [ ] 14. The company has been profitable in at least 8 of the last 10 years, including through the last recession.
- [ ] 15. Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures — the cash the business actually generates for owners) is positive and roughly tracks reported net income over time. Large persistent gaps between earnings and cash flow are a red flag.
- [ ] 16. Return on equity is above 15% (or return on invested capital above 12%) on average, achieved without excessive leverage.
- [ ] 17. Net debt is less than about 3× EBITDA and interest is covered at least 5× by operating earnings — or better, the company has net cash. Understand the debt maturity schedule.
- [ ] 18. Gross and operating margins are stable or rising; if they're falling, I know why and believe it's temporary.
- [ ] 19. Share count is flat or shrinking. A rising share count means dilution — my slice of the business is getting smaller.
- [ ] 20. Working capital behaves sensibly: receivables and inventory aren't growing much faster than sales (a classic early sign of trouble).
- [ ] 21. Accounting is conservative: revenue recognized when earned, few "one-time" charges that recur every year, no frequent restatements, and the auditor's opinion is clean.
- [ ] 22. I've checked for off-balance-sheet obligations: leases, pension deficits, guarantees, litigation reserves.

## Section 4 — Management and capital allocation

*Why this matters:* Buffett buys businesses with "management in place" and judges them by how they deploy cash. Over a decade, a management team reinvests roughly the entire market value of the company; whether they do it well is the whole game. Spier's costliest mistakes were management-related, so his checklist asks about executives' personal circumstances and history of self-dealing.

- [ ] 23. Managers have a track record of rational capital allocation: reinvesting when returns are high, buying back stock when it's cheap (not just when it's expensive), paying dividends when nothing better exists, and *not* overpaying for acquisitions.
- [ ] 24. Insiders own a meaningful stake bought with their own money (not just granted options), and recent insider transactions are net buying or neutral.
- [ ] 25. Executive pay is reasonable and tied to per-share value creation or returns on capital, not to revenue growth or the stock price alone.
- [ ] 26. Management's past guidance was roughly accurate. Read old annual letters: did they do what they said they'd do? Do they admit mistakes?
- [ ] 27. No history of self-dealing, related-party transactions, accounting scandals, or governance structures that entrench management against shareholders.
- [ ] 28. Nothing in the key executives' personal or professional circumstances suggests distraction or desperation (Spier's item, added after a CEO's personal crisis sank an investment).
- [ ] 29. The company's culture appears healthy: low executive turnover, reasonable employee reviews, no pattern of regulatory or ethical breaches.

## Section 5 — Risks and what could go wrong

*Why this matters:* Pabrai's frame is "heads I win, tails I don't lose much." The analysis that matters is downside first: what's the probability of a permanent loss of capital, and how large? Charlie Munger's advice — "invert, always invert" — means listing the ways this fails before listing the ways it succeeds.

- [ ] 30. I have written down the three most likely ways this investment loses money permanently, with a rough probability for each.
- [ ] 31. I've read the 10-K risk factors and identified which two or three are real (not boilerplate).
- [ ] 32. Technological disruption: is there a plausible technology that makes this product unnecessary within 10 years?
- [ ] 33. Regulatory and political risk: does the business depend on a license, a subsidy, a tariff, or a legal gray area?
- [ ] 34. Dependence on factors outside the company's control (commodity prices, interest rates, a single geography, weather) — Spier's checklist item on external vulnerability.
- [ ] 35. Is the industry in structural decline, and if so, is the price low enough to compensate?
- [ ] 36. Is the balance sheet strong enough to survive two bad years without diluting shareholders or defaulting?
- [ ] 37. Who is on the other side of this trade, and why might they be right? What does the smartest bear case say?

## Section 6 — Valuation

*Why this matters:* A great business bought at too high a price is a bad investment. Pabrai's checklist asks whether the stock is "cheap enough" and whether you are paying for today's reality or for an optimistic future. Buffett wants a *margin of safety* — buying well below your estimate of intrinsic value so that being somewhat wrong doesn't hurt.

- [ ] 38. I have estimated intrinsic value using at least two methods (e.g., a simple discounted-cash-flow, and a multiple of normalized earnings or free cash flow) and they roughly agree. See [useful-formulas-and-rules-of-thumb.md](useful-formulas-and-rules-of-thumb.md).
- [ ] 39. My assumptions are conservative: growth rate no higher than the company's 10-year history, margins no higher than the 10-year average, and a discount rate of at least 8–10%.
- [ ] 40. The current price is at least 25–30% below my intrinsic value estimate (a margin of safety), and the gap widens if I use pessimistic inputs.
- [ ] 41. Sanity checks: free cash flow yield (FCF ÷ market cap) is attractive relative to bond yields; P/E relative to growth (PEG) is reasonable; enterprise value ÷ EBITDA is not far above the company's own history and its peers'.
- [ ] 42. I've compared today's valuation to the company's own 10-year range and to the market. If it's the most expensive it has ever been, I know exactly why that's justified.
- [ ] 43. I'm not paying for a turnaround or a story. Buffett's criteria exclude turnarounds; Lynch found they rarely turn. If earnings must double for this to work, be honest that you're speculating.
- [ ] 44. I have asked Spier's psychological question: am I buying this because the analysis says so, or because owning it would make me feel smart, contrarian, or part of a story?

## Section 7 — Catalysts and timing

*Why this matters:* A cheap stock can stay cheap for years. You don't need a catalyst to buy, but knowing what might close the gap tells you how long you'll need to wait — and whether your patience matches your time horizon.

- [ ] 45. I can name at least one plausible reason the market would re-rate this stock within 2–3 years (earnings recovery, buybacks, spin-off, index inclusion, end of a temporary overhang) — or I've accepted that the return may come purely from the business compounding and dividends.
- [ ] 46. The reason the stock is cheap is something I believe is temporary or misunderstood, not permanent. I've written down the difference.
- [ ] 47. I understand *why* this opportunity exists: forced selling, a scary headline, an out-of-favor sector, a complicated structure. If I can't explain why the market is wrong, the market is probably right.

## Section 8 — Position sizing and portfolio fit

*Why this matters:* Pabrai and Buffett bet heavily when odds are overwhelmingly favorable — but "heavily" for a professional with decades of experience is different from "heavily" for you. Sizing errors, not selection errors, are what turn a mistake into a catastrophe.

- [ ] 48. The position, at cost, is no more than the maximum single-stock size in my IPS (commonly 3–5% of the portfolio for most people; 10% only with real conviction and experience).
- [ ] 49. Total individual-stock exposure stays within my IPS limit, and this stock doesn't push sector or country concentration past my limits.
- [ ] 50. I could lose 100% of this position without changing any life plans.
- [ ] 51. I've decided whether to buy all at once or in tranches, and written down the plan (e.g., one-third now, one-third if it falls 15%, one-third after next earnings).
- [ ] 52. I've considered where to hold it: taxable account (for long-hold, low-dividend stocks) vs tax-advantaged (for anything I may trade or that pays high dividends).

## Section 9 — Exit plan

*Why this matters:* Nearly everyone buys with a thesis and sells with an emotion. Writing the exit rules *before* buying converts future panic or greed into a pre-decided action.

- [ ] 53. I've written the thesis in one paragraph, dated it, and listed the two or three specific facts that would prove it wrong (falling market share, margin collapse, a debt covenant breach, a management change I distrust). If those happen, I sell regardless of price.
- [ ] 54. I've written a sell price or valuation level at which the stock is fully valued, and what I'll do then (trim to a smaller position, sell entirely, or hold if the business keeps improving — Lynch's point that you shouldn't sell a winner just because it doubled).
- [ ] 55. I've set a calendar reminder to re-run this checklist annually (and after every earnings surprise) and to record the result in [portfolio-review-template.md](portfolio-review-template.md). Complete [pre-trade-checklist.md](pre-trade-checklist.md) before placing the order.

## Scoring guide

Count your unchecked items:

- **0–3, none in Sections 1, 3, or 6:** a legitimate candidate. Size it per your IPS.
- **4–8:** either do more work, or pass. Most passes are correct.
- **9+ or any "no" in Sections 1/3/6:** pass. Add the stock to a watchlist with a note on what would change your mind.

Pabrai's observation is that an honest checklist rejects most ideas. That's the feature.
