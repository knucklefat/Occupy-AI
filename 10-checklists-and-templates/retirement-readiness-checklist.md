# Retirement Readiness Checklist

**In one sentence:** A checklist for the five years before and the first years after retirement — covering the 25× rule, Social Security timing, the healthcare bridge to Medicare, a written withdrawal plan, sequence-of-returns risk, Medicare and IRMAA, and estate documents — so that "can I retire?" becomes a set of answered questions rather than a feeling.

Retirement is less a single decision than a dozen small ones that interact: when to claim Social Security changes how much you withdraw, which changes your taxable income, which changes your Medicare premium and your Roth conversion room. Work through the sections in order; each builds a number the next one uses.

**Jargon defined once:** *Sequence-of-returns risk* is the danger that bad market years land right at the start of retirement, when withdrawals plus losses can permanently shrink a portfolio. *RMDs* (required minimum distributions) are forced taxable withdrawals from pre-tax accounts beginning at age 73 (75 for those born 1960 or later). *IRMAA* is an income-based surcharge on Medicare premiums, calculated from your tax return two years earlier. *FRA* is your Social Security full retirement age (67 for those born 1960 or later).

## Section 1 — Do I have enough? (The 25× rule)

*Why this matters:* The "4% rule" from the original Trinity/Bengen research found that a diversified portfolio has historically survived 30 years of inflation-adjusted withdrawals starting at 4% of the initial balance. Flipped around, that means you need about 25× your annual *portfolio-funded* spending. It's a starting estimate, not a guarantee — but it turns a vague worry into arithmetic.

- [ ] 1. **Annual spending in retirement, in today's dollars:** $______. Built from actual current spending, adjusted for what changes (no commute, no payroll taxes, no retirement contributions; more travel, more healthcare). Include taxes.
- [ ] 2. **Guaranteed income:** Social Security $______ + pension $______ + annuity $______ = $______ per year.
- [ ] 3. **Gap the portfolio must fund:** line 1 − line 2 = $______.
- [ ] 4. **Target portfolio:** line 3 × 25 = $______. (Use × 30 for a 40+ year retirement or a more conservative 3.3% rate; × 20–22 if you're flexible on spending or retiring late.)
- [ ] 5. **Current investable assets** (exclude home equity unless you plan to sell): $______.
- [ ] 6. **Ratio:** line 5 ÷ line 4 = ______. Above 1.0 with a cushion is ready. 0.8–1.0: close — a year or two more, or a modest spending cut. Below 0.8: keep working, or plan on part-time income.
- [ ] 7. **Stress test:** would a 30% portfolio drop in year one still leave you above 0.8? If not, see Section 5.
- [ ] 8. **Run at least one proper calculator** (FIRECalc, cFIREsim, or a planner's Monte Carlo tool) and record the historical success rate: ______%. Above 85–90% is typical comfort.

## Section 2 — Social Security timing

*Why this matters:* Claiming at 62 permanently cuts your benefit by up to 30% versus your full retirement age; waiting past FRA adds 8% per year until 70, for a benefit about 24% higher than at FRA (and roughly 77% higher than at 62). Social Security is inflation-adjusted, guaranteed, and lasts as long as you do — delaying is the cheapest longevity insurance available. But it's not automatic: health, spousal situations, and cash needs matter.

- [ ] 9. **Create a my Social Security account** (ssa.gov) and download your statement. Check your earnings history for errors — missing years lower your benefit.
- [ ] 10. **Record benefit at 62, FRA, and 70** from the statement: $______ / $______ / $______ per month.
- [ ] 11. **Health and family longevity:** if you reasonably expect to live past the late 70s/early 80s (the typical break-even), delaying pays. If not, claim earlier.
- [ ] 12. **Married couples:** the higher earner should generally delay to 70 — that larger benefit becomes the *survivor* benefit for whichever spouse lives longer. The lower earner can claim earlier. Spousal benefits max out at 50% of the other's FRA benefit and don't grow past FRA.
- [ ] 13. **Divorced (married 10+ years, currently unmarried):** you may claim on an ex-spouse's record without affecting them. Check.
- [ ] 14. **Bridge plan:** if delaying, which accounts fund the years between retirement and claiming? (Often the best Roth-conversion window — see Section 4.)
- [ ] 15. **Earnings test awareness:** if you claim before FRA and keep working, benefits are temporarily withheld above an earnings limit (repaid later via a higher benefit). Understand it before claiming early while employed.
- [ ] 16. **Decision recorded:** I plan to claim at age ______ (me) / ______ (spouse), for these reasons: ______.

## Section 3 — Healthcare bridge and Medicare

*Why this matters:* Healthcare is the most commonly underestimated retirement expense and the most common reason people can't retire before 65. Medicare has enrollment windows with permanent penalties for missing them, and its premiums depend on income you may have already earned.

**Before 65**
- [ ] 17. **Coverage plan for every year from retirement to 65:** employer retiree coverage / COBRA (usually 18 months, full premium) / spouse's plan / ACA marketplace / part-time job with benefits.
- [ ] 18. **ACA subsidy planning:** marketplace premium subsidies are based on modified adjusted gross income. Managing income (drawing from Roth or taxable basis rather than pre-tax accounts) in these years can be worth thousands. Model it.
- [ ] 19. **HSA strategy:** stop HSA contributions six months before Medicare enrollment (if claiming Social Security at 65+, Part A enrollment backdates six months, and HSA contributions during that window are penalized). Keep the HSA invested; after 65, it can pay Medicare premiums tax-free.
- [ ] 20. **Budgeted:** annual premium + expected out-of-pocket for the bridge years: $______.

**At 65**
- [ ] 21. **Initial enrollment window:** the 7-month window around your 65th birthday (3 months before, birth month, 3 months after). Missing Part B without creditable employer coverage means a *permanent* 10%-per-year premium penalty.
- [ ] 22. **Still working at 65 with employer coverage (20+ employees)?** You can delay Part B without penalty; enroll in the 8-month special enrollment period after that coverage ends. Confirm your employer plan counts as creditable.
- [ ] 23. **Part D (drugs):** enroll on time or face a lifetime penalty. If you have no drugs, a cheap plan still avoids the penalty.
- [ ] 24. **Medigap vs Medicare Advantage decision:** Medigap (supplement) with Original Medicare gives broad provider access and predictable costs at a higher premium; Advantage plans are cheaper up front with networks and prior authorizations. Note: Medigap is guaranteed-issue only in the first 6 months after Part B — switching later may require medical underwriting.
- [ ] 25. **IRMAA awareness:** Medicare premiums for a given year are set by your tax return from two years earlier. In 2026 the first surcharge tier begins at $109,000 (single) / $218,000 (married) of modified AGI. Large Roth conversions, capital gains, or RMDs at 63–64 raise premiums at 65–66. Plan the income spikes, or file for a reduction (Form SSA-44) if income dropped due to a life-changing event like retirement.

## Section 4 — Withdrawal plan and taxes

*Why this matters:* In accumulation, the question was "how much to save." In retirement it's "which dollar to spend first," and the order can change your lifetime tax bill by six figures. Write it down; it's the core of your retirement IPS.

- [ ] 26. **Inventory by tax type:** pre-tax (401(k), Traditional IRA) $______ / Roth $______ / taxable (with cost basis) $______ / HSA $______ / cash $______.
- [ ] 27. **Withdrawal rate chosen:** ______% initial, adjusted for inflation (4% is the classic; 3.5% for early retirees; or a *dynamic* rule — e.g., cut spending 10% after a bad year, or use "guardrails" that raise/lower withdrawals when the rate drifts past set bounds).
- [ ] 28. **Default sequencing written down.** A common starting framework: (a) spend RMDs first if applicable; (b) fill low tax brackets from pre-tax accounts each year; (c) draw taxable next, harvesting gains at 0% when eligible; (d) preserve Roth for last (tax-free growth, no RMDs, best inheritance). Customize with a planner or software — the "right" order depends on brackets, IRMAA, and legacy goals.
- [ ] 29. **Roth conversion window identified:** the years between retirement and Social Security/RMDs are usually the lowest-income years of your life. Model converting pre-tax money up to the top of the 12% or 22% bracket each year, weighing ACA subsidies (before 65) and IRMAA (63+).
- [ ] 30. **RMD projection:** at 73/75, what will your forced withdrawal be? If it pushes you into a higher bracket or IRMAA tier, that's the case for earlier conversions or Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs, available from 70½, up to an inflation-indexed annual limit).
- [ ] 31. **Tax withholding or quarterly estimates set up** — no employer withholding means you're responsible; underpayment penalties are avoidable.
- [ ] 32. **Paycheck replacement mechanics:** which account, what day, what amount, automatically transferred to checking each month. Retirement is easier when money shows up on a schedule.

## Section 5 — Sequence-of-returns risk and bucket setup

*Why this matters:* Two retirees with identical average returns can end up in wildly different places depending on whether the bad years came first or last. A 30% drop in year one of retirement, combined with withdrawals, can permanently impair a portfolio that would have been fine if the drop came in year 15. The first 5–10 years are the danger zone; the mitigations are cheap.

- [ ] 33. **Cash bucket:** 1–2 years of the *portfolio-funded* spending gap (line 3 in Section 1) held in a money market or high-yield savings. This is what you spend in a downturn instead of selling stocks low.
- [ ] 34. **Bonds bucket:** another 3–8 years of spending in short/intermediate high-quality bonds, Treasuries, TIPS, or CD ladders. Together with cash, this covers roughly 5–10 years — the historical length of the worst recoveries.
- [ ] 35. **Growth bucket:** everything else in diversified stock index funds, for spending that's 10+ years out.
- [ ] 36. **Refill rule written down.** Kitces' research shows buckets only help if you actually rebalance — e.g., annually, sell from whatever's up (stocks after good years, bonds after bad ones) to top up cash. Buckets without a refill rule become a slowly rising cash pile that drags returns.
- [ ] 37. **Allocation at retirement date:** many planners suggest holding the most conservative allocation (e.g., 50–60% stocks) around the retirement date, then gradually *increasing* stocks over the following decade — the "bond tent" or "rising equity glide path" — because sequence risk fades as you get further from the start.
- [ ] 38. **Flexibility plan:** which expenses could be cut 10–20% in a bad year without misery (travel, gifts, dining)? Write them down. Spending flexibility is the most powerful sequence-risk mitigation there is.
- [ ] 39. **Optional income floor:** consider whether a single-premium immediate annuity (SPIA) or delayed Social Security covers essential expenses, so the portfolio only funds discretionary spending. Peace of mind has value; shop by quote, never by salesperson.
- [ ] 40. **Part-time or consulting income** in the early years, even modest, cuts the withdrawal rate materially. Decide whether it's on the table.

## Section 6 — Estate, insurance, and admin

- [ ] 41. **Beneficiaries current** on every retirement account, life insurance, annuity, and HSA, with contingents. Retirement accounts pass by designation, not will.
- [ ] 42. **Will, durable financial power of attorney, healthcare directive/proxy, and HIPAA release** signed, current, and locatable by the people named in them.
- [ ] 43. **Revocable living trust** considered if you own property in multiple states, want privacy, or want to avoid probate — and if created, actually *funded* (accounts retitled).
- [ ] 44. **Inherited-IRA awareness for heirs:** most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty inherited pre-tax IRAs within 10 years — another argument for Roth conversions if your heirs are in high brackets.
- [ ] 45. **Long-term care plan:** self-fund from portfolio, hybrid life/LTC policy, traditional LTC insurance, or Medicaid planning. Decide deliberately in your late 50s/early 60s while policies are still obtainable.
- [ ] 46. **Life insurance review:** term policies can often lapse once no one depends on your income; keep any that serve estate liquidity or a pension survivorship gap.
- [ ] 47. **Umbrella liability** in place — retirees with assets and time are attractive litigation targets.
- [ ] 48. **Account consolidation:** roll old 401(k)s into one IRA (or the current plan) for simpler RMDs, fewer logins, and easier management by a spouse or POA. Check first for any plan-specific advantages (stable value funds, creditor protection, age-55 penalty-free withdrawals) before rolling.
- [ ] 49. **Simplification for a surviving spouse:** could your partner run this plan alone? If not, simplify until they could, and write a one-page "if I go first" guide.
- [ ] 50. **Fraud protection tightened:** retirees are the #1 target. Credit frozen, trusted contact named on every brokerage account, 2FA everywhere. See [red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md](red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md).

## Section 7 — The final go/no-go

- [ ] 51. Portfolio ≥ 25× the spending gap (or a calculator shows ≥ 85–90% historical success).
- [ ] 52. Healthcare covered every year to 65 and Medicare enrollment dates calendared.
- [ ] 53. Social Security claiming strategy decided for both spouses.
- [ ] 54. Withdrawal order, rate, and paycheck mechanics written.
- [ ] 55. 5–10 years of spending in cash + bonds, with a refill rule.
- [ ] 56. Estate documents and beneficiaries current.
- [ ] 57. Retirement IPS updated (see [investment-policy-statement-template.md](investment-policy-statement-template.md)) and the annual checkup calendared.
- [ ] 58. Spouse/partner has reviewed and agrees with all of the above.

If all eight are checked, you're not guessing anymore.
