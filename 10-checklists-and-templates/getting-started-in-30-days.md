# Getting Started in 30 Days

**In one sentence:** A day-by-day plan that takes a complete beginner from "I have no idea what I'm doing" to a funded, automated, diversified portfolio with a written plan — in about four weeks, spending roughly 30 minutes a day.

Every step below builds on the previous one, so do them in order. If a step is finished early, use the extra time to read the linked pages rather than skipping ahead. The plan assumes you are in the United States; account names differ elsewhere, but the sequence (safety net, then cheap tax-advantaged accounts, then a simple diversified fund, then automation) is universal.

**Jargon defined once:** an *index fund* is a mutual fund or ETF that simply holds every company in a market list (an "index") like the S&P 500, for a tiny fee. *Asset allocation* is the split between stocks (growth, volatile) and bonds (stability, lower return). An *expense ratio* is the annual fee a fund charges, expressed as a percent of what you have invested. *Tax-advantaged accounts* (401(k), IRA, HSA) let money grow without annual tax drag.

## Week 1 — Foundation and safety net

### Day 1: Take inventory
- [ ] List every account you have (checking, savings, old 401(k)s, any brokerage, any debt) with the balance and interest rate of each.
- [ ] Write down your monthly take-home pay and your typical monthly spending. Rough is fine; you'll refine later.
- [ ] Calculate your *net worth* (everything you own minus everything you owe). This is your starting line, not a judgment.

### Day 2: Kill high-interest debt first
- [ ] Any debt charging more than roughly 7–8% (credit cards, payday loans, most personal loans) is an emergency. Paying it off is a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to the interest rate — better than any investment you can reliably find.
- [ ] Exception: contribute enough to a 401(k) to get any employer match *even while* paying off debt, because a 50–100% match beats any interest rate.
- [ ] Decide: if you have high-interest debt, your "investing" for the next stretch is paying it down. Keep reading anyway so you're ready.

### Day 3: Build (or plan) the emergency fund
- [ ] Target: 3–6 months of essential expenses in a high-yield savings account or money-market fund. One month is an acceptable starter goal if you're starting from zero.
- [ ] Open a high-yield savings account at a separate bank from your checking account (friction is a feature).
- [ ] Set up an automatic transfer on payday, even if it's small.

### Day 4: Understand the three things that actually matter
- [ ] Read one page on each: *savings rate* (how much you invest matters far more than what you invest in early on), *costs* (fees compound against you), and *diversification* (own everything rather than guessing winners).
- [ ] Write one sentence in your own words explaining why a 1% annual fee can consume roughly a quarter of your final balance over 30 years. If you can't, read the fee section of [useful-formulas-and-rules-of-thumb.md](useful-formulas-and-rules-of-thumb.md).

### Day 5: Learn the account priority order
The standard order, popularized by the Bogleheads and the r/personalfinance wiki, is:
1. 401(k)/403(b) up to the employer match (free money).
2. Pay off high-interest debt.
3. Health Savings Account (HSA) if you have a high-deductible health plan (triple tax-advantaged).
4. IRA (Roth if your tax rate is low now; Traditional if it's high) up to the annual limit.
5. Back to the 401(k) up to the annual limit.
6. Taxable brokerage account for anything beyond that.
- [ ] Write down which of these apply to you and in what order.

### Day 6: Check what your employer offers
- [ ] Log in to your workplace retirement plan. Find: the match formula, the vesting schedule, the fund lineup, and each fund's expense ratio.
- [ ] Identify the cheapest broad index funds in the plan (look for "S&P 500 Index," "Total Stock Market Index," "Total International Index," "Total Bond Index," or a "Target Date 20XX" fund).
- [ ] If nothing in the plan is under about 0.50% expense ratio, note it — you'll still contribute to the match, but you'll prioritize an IRA after that.

### Day 7: Rest and review
- [ ] Re-read your Day 1 inventory. Adjust anything you got wrong.
- [ ] Decide your monthly investing amount. A common target is 15% of gross income for retirement (including the employer match); start with whatever you can and raise it 1% every raise.

## Week 2 — Accounts and allocation

### Day 8: Choose a brokerage
- [ ] Pick one of the large low-cost brokerages (Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard are the usual defaults; all offer commission-free index ETFs and no account minimums). The differences are small; the biggest mistake is spending weeks deciding.
- [ ] Confirm: no account fees, no commissions on ETFs, access to a broad index fund with an expense ratio under 0.10%.

### Day 9: Open your IRA
- [ ] Open a Roth IRA (or Traditional — see Day 5) online. It takes 15 minutes. You'll need your Social Security number, employer info, and a bank account to link.
- [ ] Name a beneficiary during setup. (Skipping this is one of the most common and most costly omissions.)
- [ ] Do not buy anything yet. Just link your bank and let the account settle.

### Day 10: Enroll in / adjust your 401(k)
- [ ] Set your contribution to at least the full match. If the plan offers auto-escalation (raising your contribution by 1% each year), turn it on.
- [ ] Choose Roth or Traditional 401(k) contributions if both are offered (same logic as the IRA: Roth when your tax rate is low, Traditional when it's high; when unsure, split).
- [ ] Update your beneficiary here too.

### Day 11: Pick your stock/bond split
- [ ] Rule-of-thumb starting points: "age in bonds" is conservative; "age minus 20 in bonds" is moderate; many young investors choose 80–100% stocks. The right answer is the one you can hold through a 40% drop without selling.
- [ ] Ask yourself honestly: "If my $10,000 became $6,000 next year, would I keep buying?" If no, add more bonds.
- [ ] Write your chosen split down (example: 80% stocks / 20% bonds).

### Day 12: Pick your US/international split
- [ ] Within stocks, decide how much goes to non-US companies. The global market is roughly 60% US / 40% international; common choices range from 20% to 40% international. Zero is a bet, not a default.
- [ ] Write it down (example: 70% US / 30% international within the stock portion).

### Day 13: Choose your funds — the simple path
Choose ONE of these, not all three:
- [ ] **Option A — one fund:** a Target Date fund matching the year you turn ~65. It holds a diversified mix and rebalances itself. Ideal in a 401(k). Check that its expense ratio is under 0.20%.
- [ ] **Option B — three funds:** a Total US Stock Market index fund, a Total International Stock index fund, and a Total Bond Market index fund, in the proportions you chose on Days 11–12. This is the "three-fund portfolio."
- [ ] **Option C — two funds:** a Total World Stock index fund plus a Total Bond Market index fund.
- [ ] Run each candidate through [etf-and-fund-selection-checklist.md](etf-and-fund-selection-checklist.md).

### Day 14: Write your first Investment Policy Statement (IPS)
- [ ] Fill in [investment-policy-statement-template.md](investment-policy-statement-template.md). A first draft takes 30 minutes. It doesn't need to be perfect; it needs to exist.
- [ ] Include the one line that matters most: what you will do when the market falls 30%. (Suggested answer: "Nothing, except rebalance per my rules and keep contributing.")

## Week 3 — Fund and automate

### Day 15: Make your first purchase
- [ ] Transfer your first contribution into the IRA. Buy the fund(s) you chose. Yes, today. Waiting for "a better time" is the most expensive habit in investing.
- [ ] If you have a lump sum from savings, most research shows investing it all at once beats spreading it out about two-thirds of the time — but if spreading it over 3–6 months lets you sleep, that's acceptable. Decide and write down the schedule.

### Day 16: Automate the IRA
- [ ] Set up an automatic monthly transfer from checking to the IRA (annual limit ÷ 12, or whatever you can afford).
- [ ] Set up automatic *investment* of that cash into your chosen fund, if the brokerage allows it (most do for mutual funds; many now do for ETFs). Cash sitting uninvested in a brokerage account is a common silent leak.
- [ ] Turn on dividend reinvestment.

### Day 17: Automate everything else
- [ ] Confirm the 401(k) contribution is coming out of your paycheck (check your next pay stub).
- [ ] Confirm the emergency fund transfer is running.
- [ ] If you're using an HSA, set contributions and choose an investment option inside it (many HSAs let cash sit idle by default).

### Day 18: Lock down security
- [ ] Turn on two-factor authentication (an app, not SMS where possible) on every financial account and the email address tied to them.
- [ ] Use a password manager; make each financial password unique and long.
- [ ] Freeze your credit at all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). It's free and takes 10 minutes each. See [red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md](red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md).

### Day 19: Consolidate old accounts
- [ ] List any old 401(k)s from previous employers. Decide whether to roll each into your current 401(k) (if its funds are cheap) or into an IRA. Never cash out.
- [ ] Start one rollover. They take a few weeks; the paperwork is mostly the receiving institution's job.

### Day 20: Set your rebalancing rule
- [ ] Choose a rule and add it to your IPS. Common choices: rebalance once a year on your birthday, or whenever any asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from target. Direct new contributions to whatever is underweight so you rarely need to sell.

### Day 21: Rest and check
- [ ] Log in to each account once. Confirm money arrived, purchases executed, and automations are scheduled. Then log out and resist the urge to check daily.

## Week 4 — Protection, habits, and the long game

### Day 22: Insurance check
- [ ] If anyone depends on your income, get term life insurance (10–12× income, 20–30 year term, from a highly-rated insurer). Skip whole life / universal life unless you have a specific estate reason.
- [ ] Confirm you have disability insurance (often through work). Your ability to earn is your largest asset.
- [ ] Confirm health insurance, auto/renter's/home liability limits are adequate. Consider an umbrella policy once net worth exceeds a few hundred thousand dollars.

### Day 23: Basic estate documents
- [ ] Beneficiaries on every account (retirement accounts pass by beneficiary designation, not by will).
- [ ] A simple will, plus a durable power of attorney and healthcare directive. Online services or a local attorney; cost is modest.
- [ ] Tell one trusted person where your account list and documents are.

### Day 24: Learn what NOT to do
- [ ] Read [red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md](red-flags-and-scam-checklist.md) and [pre-trade-checklist.md](pre-trade-checklist.md).
- [ ] Add to your IPS a "things I will never do" list: no individual stocks with money you need within 10 years (or none at all), no leverage, no crypto beyond a stated small percentage, no products you can't explain to a friend in two sentences.

### Day 25: Understand your taxes
- [ ] Learn the difference between a Traditional (pre-tax now, taxed later) and Roth (taxed now, tax-free later) account, and why holding bonds in tax-advantaged accounts and stock index funds in taxable accounts is generally more efficient ("asset location").
- [ ] Note the annual contribution limits for the current year and make sure your automation won't overshoot them.

### Day 26: Set up your review rhythm
- [ ] Put two recurring events in your calendar: a 15-minute quarterly review using [portfolio-review-template.md](portfolio-review-template.md), and a longer annual checkup using [annual-financial-checkup-checklist.md](annual-financial-checkup-checklist.md).
- [ ] Decide when you'll raise your contribution rate (every raise, every January, or both).

### Day 27: Build your information diet
- [ ] Unsubscribe from anything that emails you daily stock tips. Mute financial news notifications.
- [ ] Pick one or two slow sources (the Bogleheads wiki, a quarterly book) instead of dozens of fast ones. Information volume and returns are unrelated.

### Day 28: Prepare for your first crash
- [ ] Read [market-crash-playbook.md](market-crash-playbook.md) now, while calm.
- [ ] Write the date you expect the next 20%+ decline: "Sometime in the next 5 years, and I don't know when." Then write what you'll do. This sentence, read during the crash, is the highest-value thing you'll produce this month.

### Day 29: Optimize the edges (optional)
- [ ] If you're already maxing everything: look into the mega backdoor Roth, backdoor Roth (if over the income limit), I-bonds/TIPS for the bond portion, and tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts. None of these matter until the basics are done.

### Day 30: Graduate
- [ ] Re-read your IPS. Sign and date it.
- [ ] Confirm the final checklist:
  - [ ] Emergency fund started and automated
  - [ ] High-interest debt paid or on an aggressive payoff schedule
  - [ ] 401(k) capturing the full match, beneficiary named
  - [ ] IRA open, funded, invested, automated, beneficiary named
  - [ ] Diversified low-cost fund(s) selected with a written allocation
  - [ ] Two-factor authentication and credit freeze in place
  - [ ] Insurance and basic estate documents addressed
  - [ ] Review dates on the calendar
- [ ] Then do the hardest thing: leave it alone.

---

## What to do in month 2 and beyond

Keep contributing. Raise the rate when you can. Rebalance on schedule. Ignore forecasts. Every year, run the annual checkup. The plan you just built is roughly what a good fee-only advisor would set up for you — the value now comes from not undoing it.
