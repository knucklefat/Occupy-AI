# Key Financial Ratios

**In one sentence:** Ratios turn raw financial statements into comparable answers to five questions — is it cheap, is it profitable, is it safe, is it efficient, and can it pay its bills — but every ratio is only meaningful against the company's own history and its sector peers.

## The big reference table

| Ratio | Formula | Category | Reading it | Typical "reasonable" range* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P/E (trailing) | Price ÷ EPS (last 12 months) | Valuation | Years of current earnings you pay for | 10–25 for mature firms; S&P 500 long-run avg ~16–20 |
| Forward P/E | Price ÷ next-12-month consensus EPS | Valuation | Same, using estimates (which are often optimistic) | Usually 1–3 points below trailing |
| PEG | P/E ÷ expected annual EPS growth (%) | Valuation | Price relative to growth; ~1 is "fair" per Peter Lynch's rule of thumb | <1 cheap, 1–2 fair, >2 expensive |
| P/S | Market cap ÷ revenue | Valuation | Useful for unprofitable or cyclical firms | 0.5–2 retail/industrial; 5–15+ software |
| P/B | Market cap ÷ book equity | Valuation | Price vs. accounting net worth; best for banks and insurers | <1 potential deep value; 1–3 banks; meaningless for asset-light |
| EV/EBITDA | (Market cap + debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA | Valuation | Capital-structure-neutral price of operating cash profits | 6–12 mature; 15–25+ high growth |
| FCF yield | Free cash flow ÷ market cap (or ÷ EV) | Valuation | Cash return you would get if all FCF were paid out | 3–6% typical; >8% cheap or troubled |
| Dividend yield | Annual dividend per share ÷ price | Valuation/income | Cash paid to you now | 1–4% common; >7% often a warning |
| Gross margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue | Profitability | Pricing power and unit economics | 20–40% retail/industrial; 60–90% software |
| Operating margin | Operating income ÷ revenue | Profitability | Core business profit before financing and tax | 5–15% typical; 25%+ excellent |
| Net margin | Net income ÷ revenue | Profitability | Bottom line after everything | 3–10% typical; 20%+ elite |
| ROE | Net income ÷ average shareholders' equity | Profitability | Return on owners' capital (inflated by debt) | 10–15% okay; 15–20%+ good |
| ROA | Net income ÷ average total assets | Profitability | Return on everything the firm controls | 5%+ good for non-financials; 1%+ good for banks |
| ROIC | NOPAT ÷ (debt + equity − cash) | Profitability | Return on all capital invested, debt-neutral | >10–12% beats most firms' cost of capital |
| Debt/Equity | Total debt ÷ shareholders' equity | Leverage | Reliance on borrowed money | <1 conservative; >2 heavy (varies widely) |
| Net debt/EBITDA | (Debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA | Leverage | Years of cash profit needed to repay debt | <2 comfortable; 3–4 stretched; >5 distressed |
| Interest coverage | EBIT ÷ interest expense | Leverage | How many times profit covers interest | >5 safe; 2–3 concerning; <1.5 danger |
| Asset turnover | Revenue ÷ average total assets | Efficiency | Sales generated per dollar of assets | 0.3 utilities; 2+ grocers |
| Inventory turnover | COGS ÷ average inventory | Efficiency | Times per year stock is sold | 4–8 general; 10+ grocery |
| Days sales outstanding | Receivables ÷ revenue × 365 | Efficiency | Days to collect from customers | 30–60 typical; rising = warning |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | Liquidity | Short-term bills coverage | 1.2–2 healthy; <1 needs a look |
| Quick ratio | (Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | Liquidity | Same, excluding hard-to-sell stock | >1 comfortable |

*Ranges are broad rules of thumb for U.S. companies; treat them as starting points, not verdicts.

## Valuation ratios: what they hide

**P/E** is the most quoted and most abused ratio. Problems: EPS can be negative (no P/E), distorted by one-time items, or inflated by buybacks and debt. Use it alongside EV/EBITDA and FCF yield. Also know which "E" you are looking at — GAAP or "adjusted" — because the gap can be large.

**Forward P/E** uses analyst estimates, which tend to be too high at cycle peaks and too low at troughs.

**PEG** attempts to normalize P/E for growth. A stock at 30× earnings growing 30% a year has a PEG of 1; a stock at 15× growing 5% has a PEG of 3. The growth estimate is the weak link: use a multi-year forecast and be conservative.

**P/S** cannot go negative, so it works for money-losing companies, but it ignores margins: a 10% margin business and a 40% margin business at the same P/S are not equally priced.

**EV/EBITDA** uses enterprise value (what it would cost to buy the whole company including its debt, net of its cash) over earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It compares companies with different debt loads and tax rates fairly. Weakness: EBITDA ignores capex, so it flatters capital-intensive businesses. Charlie Munger famously called EBITDA-based thinking a way of ignoring real costs.

**FCF yield** is the ratio many professional investors trust most: it uses actual cash, after capex, over what you pay. Compare it with the 10-year Treasury yield — if a stable business yields 6% FCF and Treasuries yield 4%, you are being paid for the risk.

**Dividend yield** is only as good as the dividend's coverage. Check the **payout ratio** (dividends ÷ net income, or better, dividends ÷ FCF). A payout ratio above 80–100% is often unsustainable outside REITs and utilities.

## Profitability ratios: the DuPont link

ROE can be decomposed:

**ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier (Assets ÷ Equity)**

This tells you *how* a company earns its return. A 15% ROE from a 15% margin and modest leverage is far higher quality than a 15% ROE from a 3% margin and 5× leverage. Always look at ROE alongside debt/equity.

**ROIC** is the cleaner measure. NOPAT (net operating profit after tax) = Operating income × (1 − tax rate). Invested capital = debt + equity − excess cash (some analysts also subtract goodwill to see returns on the *operating* capital, which can be revealing). A company that consistently earns ROIC well above its cost of capital (usually 7–10%) is creating value; one earning below it destroys value no matter how fast it grows.

**Worked example.** Operating income $150M, tax rate 21% → NOPAT = $118.5M. Debt $400M, equity $600M, cash $100M → invested capital = $900M. ROIC = 13.2%. If the company's cost of capital is 9%, it earns a 4.2-point spread — good.

## Leverage ratios: can it survive a bad year?

**Net debt/EBITDA** is what lenders and rating agencies watch. Investment-grade industrial companies typically run below 2–3×; leveraged buyouts run 5–7×. **Interest coverage** tells you how much cushion exists before earnings can no longer service debt: at 2× coverage, a 50% earnings decline makes interest payments unaffordable.

Always check the notes for **off-balance-sheet** commitments (operating leases are now on-balance-sheet under current rules, but purchase commitments and guarantees may not be).

## Efficiency ratios

Efficiency ratios are best read as trends. **Days sales outstanding (DSO)** rising from 45 to 70 over three years means either customers are struggling or the company is extending credit to book sales. **Inventory days** rising means product is piling up. The **cash conversion cycle** = inventory days + receivable days − payable days; lower is better, and negative (as at Amazon or Costco) means suppliers are financing the business.

## Liquidity ratios

The **current ratio** and **quick ratio** matter most for smaller and more fragile companies. A current ratio below 1 is not automatically fatal — many excellent subscription and retail businesses run below 1 because they collect cash upfront — but for a manufacturer it deserves an explanation.

## Typical ranges by sector

| Sector | P/E | EV/EBITDA | Gross margin | Net debt/EBITDA | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Software | 25–50+ | 20–40 | 65–85% | Often net cash | Use P/S and FCF yield; SBC is a big cost |
| Consumer staples | 18–25 | 12–16 | 30–50% | 2–3 | Stable; dividend coverage matters |
| Banks | 8–14 | n/a | n/a | n/a | Use P/B, ROE, ROA, net interest margin |
| Utilities | 15–20 | 9–12 | n/a | 4–6 | Regulated; high debt is normal |
| Energy/materials | 6–15 | 4–8 | Volatile | 1–2 | Cyclical; low P/E at peak is a trap |
| Retail | 12–25 | 7–12 | 25–40% | 1–3 | Inventory turnover and same-store sales |
| Industrials | 15–22 | 10–14 | 25–35% | 1–3 | ROIC and order backlog |
| Healthcare/pharma | 15–25 | 10–15 | 60–80% | 1–3 | Pipeline risk; patent cliffs |
| REITs | n/a | 15–22 | n/a | 5–7 | Use P/FFO and dividend coverage |

These are approximate mid-cycle ranges; they shift with interest rates and sentiment.

## How to actually do it

1. Pick the five ratios that matter for the sector (e.g., for a bank: P/B, ROE, ROA, efficiency ratio, capital ratio; for software: revenue growth, gross margin, FCF margin, P/S, net retention).
2. Compute each ratio for the company for the last five to ten years using an aggregator like Stock Analysis, Koyfin, or Macrotrends, or from the 10-K.
3. Compute the same ratios for three to five direct competitors.
4. Put them in one table. Look for the company's ratio relative to its own history and relative to peers.
5. For any ratio that looks unusually good or bad, find the reason in the 10-K. A low P/E is either a bargain or a warning; the filing tells you which.
6. Cross-check valuation with three lenses (P/E, EV/EBITDA, FCF yield). If they disagree sharply, find out why — usually debt or capex.
7. Write down what the ratios imply about the *story*: is this a growing, profitable, safe business at a fair price, or not?

## Key takeaways

- No ratio means anything in isolation; compare against the company's own history and its sector peers.
- Use at least three valuation lenses (P/E, EV/EBITDA, FCF yield); they catch each other's blind spots.
- ROIC vs. cost of capital is the single best test of whether growth creates value.
- Decompose ROE with DuPont to see whether returns come from margins, efficiency, or debt.
- Net debt/EBITDA and interest coverage tell you whether a company can survive a downturn.
- Efficiency ratios (DSO, inventory days) are early warning systems; watch trends, not levels.
- Sector context is everything: a 4× net debt/EBITDA is normal for a utility and alarming for a retailer.
- A dividend is only as safe as its coverage by free cash flow.
