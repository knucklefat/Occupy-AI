# Chart Patterns

**In one sentence:** Chart patterns are recurring shapes in price that summarize a tug-of-war between buyers and sellers, and their value is not in predicting the future but in giving you a precise place to enter, a precise place to admit you are wrong, and a rough estimate of the reward if you are right.

## How to think about patterns

Every pattern is a story about supply and demand. A double bottom says sellers tried twice to push price through a level and failed both times. A flag says the crowd paused to catch its breath after a sharp move. The pattern itself is not magic; what makes it usable is that it defines a **trigger** (the breakout level), an **invalidation** (the level that proves the pattern failed), and a **measured move** (a target based on the pattern's height).

Thomas Bulkowski's *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* is the most thorough statistical study of these shapes across tens of thousands of examples. His data show that most patterns fail 10–30% of the time even under ideal conditions, that "throwbacks" and "pullbacks" (price returning to the breakout level after breaking out) happen roughly half the time, and that patterns behave differently in bull and bear markets. Treat all the numbers below as tendencies, not promises.

## Reversal patterns

### Head and shoulders (top)

Three peaks: a left shoulder, a higher head, and a right shoulder roughly level with the left. A **neckline** connects the two intervening lows. The pattern completes when price closes below the neckline, ideally on rising volume. Volume typically fades across the three peaks — buyers are running out of energy.

- **Trigger:** close below the neckline.
- **Target:** neckline minus the height from head to neckline.
- **Invalidation:** a close back above the right shoulder.
- **Example:** head at $120, neckline at $100. Break below $100 → target $80. Stop above the right shoulder at, say, $110. Risk $10 to make $20.

The inverse (head and shoulders bottom) mirrors this after a downtrend and is one of the more reliable bottoming patterns in Bulkowski's data, though throwbacks to the neckline happen roughly 45% of the time and reduce performance when they do.

### Double top / double bottom

Two peaks (or troughs) at roughly the same level, separated by a meaningful pullback (Bulkowski suggests at least 10% between the peaks and the trough). A double top completes on a close below the intervening low; a double bottom on a close above the intervening high. Volume on the second peak is usually lighter.

- **Target:** the breakout level plus or minus the pattern height.
- **Common mistake:** buying the second bottom before confirmation. Many "double bottoms" become descending channels. Wait for the close above the middle peak.

### Triple tops and bottoms, rounding bottoms

Variations on the same theme. A rounding bottom (saucer) is a slow, U-shaped base that suggests gradual accumulation; it is usually a long-term pattern on weekly charts.

## Continuation patterns

### Cup with handle

Popularized by William O'Neil. A rounded U-shaped base (the cup, ideally several weeks to months), followed by a short, shallow pullback (the handle) that drifts down on light volume. The buy point is a breakout above the handle's high on volume at least 40–50% above average. The handle should form in the upper half of the cup; a handle that drops too deep signals weakness.

- **Target:** breakout plus the cup's depth (a conservative alternative is a fixed 20–25% gain, O'Neil's typical sell rule).
- **Invalidation:** a close below the handle low, or a 7–8% loss from entry (O'Neil's stop rule).

### Flags and pennants

A sharp move (the flagpole) followed by a brief, tight consolidation that slopes against the trend (flag) or narrows into a small triangle (pennant), lasting days to a few weeks. Volume dries up during the consolidation and expands on the breakout in the direction of the original move.

- **Target:** the breakout level plus the length of the flagpole — flags are called "half-mast" patterns because they often mark the midpoint of a move.
- **Invalidation:** a close below the flag's lower boundary (for a bull flag).

### Triangles

- **Ascending:** flat top, rising lows. Buyers are increasingly aggressive; breakouts are usually upward.
- **Descending:** flat bottom, falling highs. Usually breaks down.
- **Symmetrical:** converging highs and lows. Direction is undecided; wait for the break.

Breakouts tend to happen in the final third of the triangle. A triangle that drifts all the way to its apex without breaking usually loses its power.

- **Target:** breakout plus the height of the triangle's widest part.

## Breakouts

A breakout is a close beyond a well-defined level (resistance, support, pattern boundary). Quality signals:

1. **Close, not intraday touch.** Use daily (or weekly) closes; intraday spikes are often stop hunts.
2. **Volume expansion**, ideally 1.5–2× the 50-day average.
3. **Follow-through:** the next one to three sessions hold above the level.
4. **Context:** breakouts in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend succeed far more often than counter-trend ones.
5. **Room to run:** check for overhead resistance between the breakout and the target.

## False breakouts and how to handle them

False breakouts (price pokes through a level, then reverses) are common enough that experienced traders build them into the plan.

- **Wait for confirmation.** A close beyond the level *plus* a follow-through day, or a retest that holds, filters many failures at the cost of a slightly worse entry.
- **Throwbacks and pullbacks are normal.** Bulkowski finds them in roughly half of all breakouts. A return to the breakout level that holds is a second, often better, entry. A return that slices through it is a failure.
- **Set the stop where the pattern is invalid, not at an arbitrary percentage.** For an upside breakout, that is usually just below the breakout level or below the most recent swing low within the pattern.
- **Failed patterns are themselves signals.** A bull flag that breaks *down* traps buyers whose forced exits accelerate the move — the "failed pattern" trade in the opposite direction can be strong.
- **Beware breakouts into news.** Earnings dates, Fed meetings, and index rebalances produce spikes that reverse.

## Risk management: the part that actually makes money

The pattern is the setup; the position sizing is the edge.

1. **Define the risk per trade** as a fixed fraction of capital — commonly 1% (aggressive: 2%).
2. **Position size = (capital × risk fraction) ÷ (entry − stop).** Example: $50,000 account, 1% risk ($500), entry $100, stop $95 → risk per share $5 → 100 shares.
3. **Require a reward-to-risk of at least 2:1** using the measured-move target. With a 40% win rate and 2:1 payoffs, you still make money; with 1:1 payoffs you need to be right more than half the time.
4. **Never move the stop further away.** Move it closer (trailing) as the trade works.
5. **Scale out** at the first target and let a portion run with a trailing stop under swing lows.
6. **Track outcomes** by pattern type. After 30–50 trades you will know which patterns work for you and which to drop.

## A worked trade

Stock consolidates in a bull flag after rising from $80 to $100 (flagpole $20). Flag lows at $95, flag highs at $98. Breakout close at $99 on double average volume.

- Entry: $99.
- Stop: $94 (below the flag low). Risk $5/share.
- Target: $99 + $20 = $119 (flagpole measure). Reward $20/share → 4:1.
- Account $50,000, 1% risk → 100 shares.
- If the stock throws back to $98 and holds, add nothing (already sized) but note the confirmation. If it closes below $94, exit; the loss is $500, about 1% of capital, and the pattern was wrong. If it reaches $109 (halfway), sell half and trail the stop to breakeven.

## How to actually do it

1. Confirm the higher-timeframe trend first. Favor patterns that resolve in its direction.
2. Identify the pattern and draw its boundaries and the breakout level on the chart.
3. Before the breakout, write the plan: entry level, stop level, target, share count.
4. Wait for a *close* beyond the level on above-average volume. Enter, or wait for the throwback if you prefer a better price.
5. Place the stop immediately. Do not wait.
6. Take partial profits at the measured target; trail the rest.
7. Log the trade with a screenshot. Review monthly.

## Key takeaways

- Patterns are supply/demand stories that hand you a trigger, a stop, and a target — that structure is their real value.
- Reversal patterns (head and shoulders, double top/bottom) need confirmation by a close through the neckline or middle pivot.
- Continuation patterns (flags, pennants, triangles, cup with handle) work best in the direction of the prevailing trend on expanding volume.
- Bulkowski's data: patterns fail 10–30% of the time even when textbook, and throwbacks occur about half the time — plan for them.
- Confirm breakouts with a daily close, volume, and follow-through; false breakouts are normal and can be traded in reverse.
- Position size from the stop distance, risk 1–2% per trade, and demand at least 2:1 reward-to-risk.
- Keep a trade log; your own statistics matter more than anyone's book.
