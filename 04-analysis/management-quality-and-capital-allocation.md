# Management Quality and Capital Allocation

**In one sentence:** Over a decade, the single biggest driver of a company's per-share value is not how well it runs its operations but how well its CEO deploys the cash those operations produce — and you can judge that from the record, the incentives, and the way management talks.

## Why capital allocation is the CEO's real job

A company generates cash. The CEO has five things to do with it: invest in the existing business, acquire other businesses, pay down debt, pay dividends, or buy back shares. And two ways to raise more: issue debt or issue shares. Every one of those seven decisions either creates or destroys value per share.

William Thorndike's *The Outsiders* (2012) studied eight CEOs — including Henry Singleton (Teledyne), Tom Murphy (Capital Cities), John Malone (TCI), Katharine Graham (Washington Post), Bill Stiritz (Ralston Purina), Dick Smith (General Cinema), Bill Anders (General Dynamics), and Warren Buffett (Berkshire) — whose companies outperformed the S&P 500 by roughly 20× over their tenures. None were operational geniuses in the conventional sense. What they shared was an unusual discipline about where cash went.

## Lessons from *The Outsiders*

1. **Capital allocation is the CEO's most important job.** Most CEOs rise through operations or sales and have never been trained in it.
2. **Per-share value is the metric that matters, not size, revenue, or headlines.** Singleton shrank Teledyne's share count by roughly 90% over a decade through buybacks when the stock was cheap.
3. **Cash flow, not reported earnings, determines value.** These CEOs ignored accounting earnings and focused on free cash flow.
4. **Decentralize operations, centralize capital allocation.** Run the businesses locally with lean headquarters; make the big money decisions personally.
5. **Be opportunistic, not programmatic.** Buy back stock aggressively when it is cheap and issue it when it is expensive — Singleton did both. Make big acquisitions rarely and only when the price is right; be willing to wait years.
6. **Independence from Wall Street.** They avoided guidance, rarely talked to analysts, and did not manage for quarterly numbers.
7. **Use leverage carefully and tax-efficiently.** Malone pioneered structures that minimized taxes; Murphy used debt for acquisitions but paid it down quickly.
8. **Patience with occasional boldness.** Long stretches of doing nothing, punctuated by a few very large, decisive bets.

## Buybacks vs. dividends vs. reinvestment

The right choice depends entirely on the return each option offers.

| Use of cash | Creates value when | Destroys value when |
|---|---|---|
| **Reinvest in the business** | Incremental ROIC on new projects exceeds the cost of capital | Growth for growth's sake at low returns; capex that just maintains position |
| **Acquisitions** | Bought at a price that yields returns above the cost of capital, with achievable synergies | Overpaying at the top of the cycle; "diversification" outside core competence; empire building |
| **Buybacks** | Stock trades below intrinsic value | Stock is expensive; funded with debt to offset option dilution; done to hit EPS targets |
| **Dividends** | No better internal use; investors value predictability | Paid from borrowing; payout exceeds free cash flow |
| **Debt paydown** | Leverage is uncomfortable or interest rates are high | Balance sheet is already fortress-like and shares are cheap |

Buffett's buyback test: repurchase only when the stock is below a conservative estimate of intrinsic value *and* the company retains ample liquidity. A company that buys back shares at 40× earnings and then issues them at 15× during a crisis has done the opposite of what it should.

A simple check on buyback quality: compare the average price paid (Item 5 of the 10-K reports quarterly repurchases) with the stock's valuation at the time. Also confirm the share count actually fell — many "buyback programs" merely offset stock-compensation dilution.

## How to judge management: the evidence

**1. The track record.** Pull ten years of data and ask:
- Did per-share book value, free cash flow, and (for dividend payers) dividends grow faster than the business as a whole? If revenue grew 8% a year but per-share FCF grew 3%, dilution or bad acquisitions ate the difference.
- What return did acquisitions earn? Compare goodwill on the balance sheet with the earnings of the acquired businesses. Repeated goodwill impairments are confessions of overpayment.
- Did the share count rise or fall, and at what prices?
- Did ROIC hold up as the company grew?

**2. Candor.** Read five years of shareholder letters and MD&A. Do they discuss mistakes plainly? Did they warn about problems before the numbers showed them? Do they use the same metrics every year, or do they switch to whatever looks best? Buffett's standard: report to shareholders the way you would want a partner to report to you.

**3. Consistency between words and actions.** If the letter says "we are disciplined acquirers" and the cash flow statement shows five deals a year at rising multiples, believe the cash flow statement.

**4. Operational competence.** Margins relative to peers, market share trend, employee retention (Glassdoor, turnover in the executive ranks), customer metrics. This matters but is table stakes; a great operator with poor capital allocation still underperforms.

**5. Ownership and skin in the game.** From the proxy: how much stock does the CEO own outright (not options) relative to annual pay? A CEO with $50M in shares and $5M in salary thinks like an owner. Check Form 4s for open-market purchases versus routine option exercises and sales.

## Incentive alignment: reading the proxy

The Compensation Discussion and Analysis section tells you what the board is paying management to do. Look at:

- **Metrics.** Per-share metrics (EPS growth, FCF per share, TSR versus peers, ROIC) align with owners. Absolute revenue or EBITDA targets reward getting bigger regardless of returns. "Adjusted" metrics that exclude stock compensation reward dilution.
- **Time horizon.** Multi-year performance periods (3+ years) beat annual bonuses. Vesting that requires continued performance beats time-based vesting alone.
- **Peer group for benchmarking pay.** Boards often choose larger companies as "peers" to justify higher pay.
- **Quantum.** CEO pay as a percentage of net income and versus median employee pay (disclosed as the pay ratio).
- **Perks and related-party deals.** Personal aircraft use, company loans, family employment, and leases with insiders.
- **Say-on-pay vote results.** Support below 70–80% signals shareholder discontent.

Charlie Munger's line — show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome — is the whole point of this section.

## Red flags

- Frequent large acquisitions, especially outside the core business, and especially with stock when the stock is cheap.
- Buybacks funded with debt while insiders sell.
- Serial "one-time" restructuring charges.
- Changing the headline metric each year (revenue, then adjusted EBITDA, then "community-adjusted EBITDA").
- Guidance that is always beaten by a penny; earnings that never miss suggest managed numbers.
- High executive turnover, especially CFOs and auditors.
- CEO also chairs the board with few independent directors.
- Founder-controlled dual-class structures with no sunset (not always bad, but it removes accountability).
- Pay metrics reset or lowered mid-year to make sure bonuses get paid.
- Promotional language: "disruptive," "transformational," heavy use of adjusted numbers in the earnings release headline.

## How to actually do it

1. Build a ten-year table: revenue, FCF, diluted shares, FCF per share, dividends per share, book value per share, ROIC, goodwill.
2. Compute the growth rate of FCF per share vs. total FCF. The gap is the cost (or benefit) of capital allocation.
3. From the cash flow statement, tally where cash went over ten years: capex, acquisitions, buybacks, dividends, debt paydown. Compare with what management said it would do.
4. For buybacks: from Item 5 of each 10-K, note average repurchase prices and compare with valuation at the time.
5. For acquisitions: list the deals, prices, and any subsequent impairments.
6. Read five years of CEO letters. Note every stated goal and check whether it was met.
7. Open the proxy. Record incentive metrics, CEO ownership vs. pay, related-party transactions, say-on-pay results.
8. Check Form 4 filings for the last two years: open-market buys are strong; routine sales are neutral; large sales before bad news are damning.
9. Score management on four dimensions: track record, candor, incentives, operations. Only invest with confidence when at least three are strong.

## Key takeaways

- Capital allocation — where the cash goes — is the CEO's most important job and the biggest driver of per-share value over time.
- Judge managers by per-share results (FCF, book value, dividends per share), not by company size or revenue.
- Buybacks create value only below intrinsic value; check the prices paid and whether share count actually fell.
- Acquisitions are where the most value is destroyed; read the goodwill line and impairment history.
- Candor in letters and MD&A, tested against what actually happened, is a reliable signal.
- The proxy tells you what management is paid to do; incentives predict behavior.
- Insider ownership relative to salary and open-market purchases show real alignment.
- *The Outsiders* pattern: decentralized operations, centralized capital allocation, opportunistic buybacks, patience punctuated by rare bold moves.
