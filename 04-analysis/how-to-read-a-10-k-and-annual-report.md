# How to Read a 10-K and Annual Report

**In one sentence:** The 10-K is the one document a public company is legally required to tell you the truth in, and reading four of its sections — Business, Risk Factors, MD&A, and the Financial Statements with their notes — plus the proxy statement will teach you more than a year of headlines.

## What the 10-K is (and how it differs from the annual report)

Every U.S. public company must file a **Form 10-K** with the SEC within 60 to 90 days of its fiscal year-end (deadline depends on company size). It is written by lawyers and accountants, signed by the CEO and CFO under penalty of law, and audited. The glossy **annual report** mailed to shareholders is a marketing document that usually wraps the 10-K in photos and a letter from the CEO. Read the letter for tone; read the 10-K for facts.

Quarterly updates come in the **10-Q** (unaudited, shorter). Material events between filings come in an **8-K**. The **DEF 14A proxy statement**, filed before the annual meeting, covers executive pay, board members, and who owns the shares.

All of these are free on [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/). Search the company name, filter by form type.

## Section-by-section walkthrough

The SEC's own investor bulletin lays out the structure. Here is what matters in each part.

### Part I

**Item 1 — Business.** The company describes what it sells, to whom, how it competes, its suppliers, customers, seasonality, regulation, and employees. This is where you learn the business model in the company's own words. Look for: customer concentration ("one customer accounted for 30% of revenue"), segment breakdown, and how management describes its competitive position. Compare this year's description with five years ago — what changed?

**Item 1A — Risk Factors.** A long list of things that could go wrong, written defensively by lawyers. Most are boilerplate ("our stock price may be volatile"). The value is in the *specific* ones and in *changes* year to year. A new risk factor about a regulatory investigation or a key supplier is a signal. Read the first five and any that name a specific customer, product, or lawsuit.

**Item 1B — Unresolved Staff Comments.** Usually "None." If not, the SEC has questioned the company's disclosures — read carefully.

**Item 2 — Properties.** Owned vs. leased facilities. Useful for asset-heavy businesses.

**Item 3 — Legal Proceedings.** Pending lawsuits. Note the size of potential damages relative to earnings.

### Part II

**Item 5 — Market for Common Equity.** Stock data, dividend policy, and a table of share repurchases in the last quarter. Compare buyback prices with current prices.

**Item 7 — Management's Discussion and Analysis (MD&A).** The most important narrative section. Management must explain, in plain language, why results changed year over year, what trends and uncertainties it sees, its liquidity and capital resources, and its **critical accounting estimates** (the judgment calls that most affect reported numbers). The MD&A is where you find why revenue grew, why margins moved, and what management expects. Skeptical reading tip: compare last year's MD&A predictions with what actually happened.

**Item 7A — Market Risk.** Sensitivity to interest rates, currencies, and commodities. Often includes a useful "a 1% rate rise would cost us $X" disclosure.

**Item 8 — Financial Statements and Supplementary Data.** The audited income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement, statement of shareholders' equity, and — crucially — the **notes**. The notes hold the details: revenue recognition policy, debt terms and maturities, lease commitments, pension assumptions, segment data, stock compensation, contingencies, and subsequent events. Also here: the **auditor's report**. Look for an unqualified ("clean") opinion and read the **Critical Audit Matters (CAMs)** — the auditor's own list of the hardest judgment calls.

**Item 9A — Controls and Procedures.** Management and the auditor state whether internal controls over financial reporting are effective. A "material weakness" disclosure is a serious warning.

### Part III

**Items 10–14** — directors, executive compensation, ownership, related-party transactions, and audit fees. Most companies say "incorporated by reference to the proxy statement," which is why you must also read the proxy.

### Part IV

**Item 15 — Exhibits.** Material contracts, debt agreements, and employment agreements are attached or linked here. If a customer contract or credit agreement matters, it is often exhibited in full.

## How Buffett reads them

Warren Buffett has said he reads hundreds of annual reports a year and has described them as the best source of information about a business. From his letters and interviews, his approach comes down to a few habits:

- **Read the report to understand the business, not the stock.** He asks whether he understands how the company makes money and whether it will still make money in ten years.
- **Judge the writing.** Buffett has praised reports that speak candidly about mistakes and criticized ones full of jargon and adjusted metrics. He has said that if management's writing is designed to obscure, that itself is information.
- **Look for owner earnings.** In his 1986 letter he defined owner earnings as reported earnings plus depreciation and other non-cash charges, minus the capital expenditures needed to maintain the business's competitive position — essentially a conservative free cash flow.
- **Read competitors' reports too.** He has said he reads the reports of every company in an industry to understand who is winning and why.
- **Skip the photos.** The value is in the MD&A, the notes, and the numbers over many years.

## The proxy statement (DEF 14A)

The proxy is where you evaluate the people. Read these sections:

- **Compensation Discussion and Analysis (CD&A).** What metrics drive bonuses and stock grants? Metrics tied to per-share value, return on capital, or free cash flow align with shareholders. Metrics tied to raw revenue growth or adjusted EBITDA can reward empire-building.
- **Summary Compensation Table.** Total pay for the top five officers. Compare it with net income; if the CEO earns 5% of profits, ask why.
- **Security Ownership of Beneficial Owners and Management.** How much of the company do insiders actually own (not just options)? Founders and executives with meaningful stakes relative to their salary have skin in the game. Also lists holders above 5% — often index funds and a few large active managers.
- **Related-party transactions.** Leases from the CEO's family, consulting deals with directors, or loans to insiders are red flags.
- **Board composition.** Independent directors, tenure, and whether the CEO is also chair.
- **Shareholder proposals** and how the board recommends voting.

For live insider trading (buys and sells), look at **Form 4** filings on EDGAR. Open-market purchases by executives with their own money are among the more reliable positive signals; sales are noisier because of diversification and tax needs.

## How to actually do it

1. On EDGAR, open the latest 10-K and the two prior ones. Also open the latest DEF 14A.
2. Read Item 1 (Business) slowly. Write down the business model in one paragraph and list the top three competitors it names.
3. Skim Item 1A for risks that are specific to this company, and compare the list with the previous year's. New entries are news.
4. Read Item 7 (MD&A) in full. Note every reason management gives for changes in revenue and margins. Check whether last year's outlook came true.
5. Go to Item 8. Read the auditor's report and Critical Audit Matters first. Then read the notes on revenue recognition, debt, leases, and segments.
6. Check Item 9A for any material weakness in controls.
7. Open the proxy. Record insider ownership percentage, the CEO's total pay, the metrics that drive incentive pay, and any related-party transactions.
8. Look at recent Form 4 filings for open-market insider buys or sells.
9. Write down three questions the filing did not answer — these become your research agenda.

## Key takeaways

- The 10-K is legally accountable disclosure; the glossy annual report is marketing.
- The four sections worth most of your time: Item 1 (Business), Item 1A (Risk Factors), Item 7 (MD&A), Item 8 (Financials and notes).
- Year-over-year *changes* in risk factors and MD&A language are more informative than any single year's text.
- Read the auditor's opinion, Critical Audit Matters, and Item 9A on internal controls — problems there are serious.
- The proxy statement tells you what management is paid to do and how much they own; incentives predict behavior.
- Form 4 filings show real insider buying and selling.
- Buffett's approach: understand the business, judge management's candor, compute owner earnings, and read the competitors' reports too.
