# Investment Account Types

**In one sentence:** The account you hold an investment in can matter as much as the investment itself, because tax-advantaged accounts — 401(k)s, IRAs, HSAs, 529s — let money compound without the annual tax drag that a plain brokerage account imposes, in exchange for rules about who can contribute, how much, and when you can take it out.

> **Year flag:** All contribution limits below are the **2026** figures announced by the IRS in November 2025 (IR-2025-111) and the May 2025 HSA notice. They are indexed to inflation and usually change every year — check the year before relying on any number.

## The three tax treatments

Every account falls into one of three buckets, defined by *when* you pay tax:

| Treatment | Contributions | Growth | Withdrawals | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Taxable** | After-tax | Taxed yearly on dividends/interest; capital gains taxed when sold | No tax on withdrawal itself | Brokerage account |
| **Tax-deferred ("traditional")** | Pre-tax (deductible now) | Untaxed | Taxed as ordinary income | Traditional 401(k), traditional IRA, SEP, SIMPLE |
| **Tax-free ("Roth")** | After-tax | Untaxed | Tax-free if rules are met | Roth 401(k), Roth IRA, 529 (for education), HSA (for medical) |

The HSA is the only account that is deductible going in, untaxed while growing, *and* tax-free coming out (for medical expenses) — "triple tax-advantaged."

## 2026 contribution limits at a glance

| Account | Under 50 | Age 50+ catch-up | Age 60–63 "super" catch-up | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 401(k) / 403(b) / most 457(b) — employee deferral | **$24,500** | +$8,000 (= $32,500) | +$11,250 instead (= $35,750) | Applies across all 401(k)/403(b) plans combined |
| 401(k) total (employee + employer), §415(c) | **$72,000** | $80,000 | $83,250 | Employer match/profit sharing counts here, not against the $24,500 |
| Traditional + Roth IRA (combined) | **$7,500** | +$1,100 (= $8,600) | — | Must have earned income at least equal to contribution |
| SEP-IRA | Lesser of 25% of compensation or **$72,000** | No catch-up | — | ~20% of net self-employment income after the SE-tax adjustment |
| Solo 401(k) | $24,500 employee + up to 25% of compensation employer, total **$72,000** | $80,000 | $83,250 | Same math as SEP but the employee deferral lets you hit the max at lower income |
| SIMPLE IRA | **$17,000** ($18,100 for certain small plans) | +$4,000 | +$5,250 | For employers with ≤100 employees |
| HSA (self-only / family) | **$4,400 / $8,750** | +$1,000 at age 55+ | — | Requires a qualifying high-deductible health plan |
| 529 plan | No federal annual limit; **$19,000** per donor per beneficiary stays under the gift-tax exclusion ($38,000 per couple); **$95,000** via 5-year "superfunding" | — | — | State lifetime caps range from about $269,000 to $621,000 |

**Compensation cap** for plan calculations: $360,000. **Mandatory Roth catch-up:** starting in 2026, employees whose prior-year wages from that employer exceeded $150,000 must make 401(k) catch-up contributions as Roth (after-tax), not pre-tax.

## Taxable brokerage account

The default account. No contribution limits, no withdrawal rules, no age restrictions; you can hold anything, and you can pull money out tomorrow. The cost is tax drag:

- **Dividends and interest** are taxed the year received. "Qualified" dividends and long-term capital gains get preferential rates — **0%, 15%, or 20%** depending on income — plus a 3.8% net investment income tax above ~$200,000 (single) / $250,000 (joint). Interest and non-qualified dividends are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Capital gains** are taxed only when you sell. Hold more than one year for long-term rates; a year or less is short-term, taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%).
- **Losses** offset gains, and up to $3,000 a year of net losses offset ordinary income; the rest carries forward. (Beware the **wash-sale rule**: selling at a loss and rebuying a "substantially identical" security within 30 days disallows the loss.)
- **Step-up in basis:** heirs inherit at market value, wiping out the embedded gain — a powerful reason to hold appreciated assets until death rather than sell.

Best uses: money you may need before 59½, anything beyond your tax-advantaged limits, and tax-efficient holdings (broad index ETFs, municipal bonds).

## 401(k) and cousins (403(b), 457(b), TSP)

An employer-sponsored plan funded by payroll deduction. Key mechanics:

- **Employer match** — commonly 50–100% of your contributions up to 3–6% of salary. This is an instant 50–100% return; contribute at least enough to capture all of it before funding anything else.
- **Vesting** — the match may become fully yours only after 2–6 years of service; your own contributions are always yours.
- **Traditional vs. Roth** — most plans now offer both. Traditional lowers this year's tax bill; Roth means tax-free withdrawals. Rule of thumb: Roth when your tax rate is low now (early career), traditional when it's high; when unsure, split.
- **Withdrawals** before age 59½ generally incur a 10% penalty plus tax (exception: leaving the employer at 55+, the "Rule of 55"). **Required minimum distributions (RMDs)** begin at 73 (75 for those born in 1960 or later); Roth 401(k)s are exempt from RMDs since 2024.
- **Investment menu** is limited to what the plan offers — often a dozen or two funds. Check expense ratios; some plans are excellent (TSP, large employers), some are dreadful.
- **Leaving a job:** you can leave the money, roll it to the new employer's plan, or roll it to an IRA (usually more choice, lower cost). Never take it as cash.
- **403(b)** is the nonprofit/school version; **457(b)** the state/local government version (with the perk of no 10% early-withdrawal penalty after separation); **TSP** the federal version. 457(b) and 401(k) limits are separate, so a government employee with both can defer $49,000.

## Traditional and Roth IRA

An Individual Retirement Arrangement is opened by you at any brokerage, with a nearly unlimited investment menu. The $7,500 limit is shared between traditional and Roth.

**Traditional IRA:** contributions may be deductible. If you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out at modified adjusted gross income of $81,000–$91,000 (single) or $129,000–$149,000 (joint) in 2026; if only your spouse is covered, $242,000–$252,000. Withdrawals are taxed as income; 10% penalty before 59½ with exceptions (first home up to $10,000, higher education, certain emergencies); RMDs at 73.

**Roth IRA:** after-tax contributions, tax-free growth and withdrawals. Direct contributions phase out at $153,000–$168,000 (single) or $242,000–$252,000 (joint) in 2026. Two features make it the most flexible account in the tax code:

1. **Contributions** (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time, any age, tax- and penalty-free.
2. **No RMDs** during the owner's life, and heirs get 10 years of tax-free growth.

Earnings are tax-free after age 59½ *and* five years from your first Roth contribution. Above the income limits, the **backdoor Roth** — contribute to a non-deductible traditional IRA, then convert — remains legal and widely used (watch the pro-rata rule if you hold other pre-tax IRA money).

## Health Savings Account (HSA)

Available only if you're enrolled in a **high-deductible health plan** (2026: deductible of at least $1,700 self / $3,400 family, out-of-pocket max no more than $8,500 / $17,000). Contributions are deductible (and exempt from payroll tax if made through an employer), growth is untaxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free at any age. After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA with no penalty.

The power move: pay current medical bills out of pocket, keep the receipts, let the HSA compound invested in index funds for decades, and reimburse yourself tax-free whenever you like — there is no deadline. Many employers' default HSA custodians have poor investment options; you can transfer to a better one.

## 529 education savings plan

A state-sponsored account for education. Contributions are after-tax federally (about 30+ states give a state deduction or credit — some for *any* state's plan, most only their own), growth is untaxed, and withdrawals are tax-free for qualified expenses: college tuition, fees, books, room and board, registered apprenticeships, up to $10,000 lifetime of student loan repayment, and — from 2026 — up to **$20,000 per year** of K-12 tuition and related expenses. Non-qualified withdrawals owe income tax plus a 10% penalty on the *earnings only*.

Since 2024, up to **$35,000 lifetime** can be rolled from a 529 that has been open 15+ years into the beneficiary's Roth IRA (subject to the annual IRA limit), which removes the old "what if they don't go to college" objection. You can also change the beneficiary to another family member at any time. Anyone can contribute — grandparents included — and 529 assets count lightly in financial-aid formulas.

## Accounts for the self-employed

| | SEP-IRA | Solo 401(k) | SIMPLE IRA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Any self-employed person or small employer | Self-employed with no employees (spouse OK) | Employers with ≤100 employees |
| 2026 max | 25% of comp, up to $72,000 | $24,500 employee + 25% employer, up to $72,000 ($80,000 / $83,250 with catch-ups) | $17,000 + 3% employer match (catch-ups $4,000 / $5,250) |
| Roth option | Yes (since 2023, custodian permitting) | Yes | Yes (since 2023, limited availability) |
| Setup | Minutes, no annual filing | A bit more paperwork; Form 5500-EZ once assets exceed $250,000 | Employer plan documents |
| Best for | Simplicity, or if you also have employees you'd rather not cover generously | Maximizing contributions at moderate income; Roth and loan features | Small businesses wanting a cheap employee plan |

Example: a freelancer netting $80,000 can put about $15,000 into a SEP (20% of net after the SE-tax adjustment) but roughly $39,500 into a Solo 401(k) ($24,500 deferral + $15,000 employer). The Solo 401(k) wins at all but the highest incomes.

## A sensible funding order

A widely used priority list (adjust for your situation):

1. 401(k) up to the full employer match — free money.
2. Pay off high-interest debt (anything above ~7%).
3. Emergency fund of 3–6 months' expenses in cash.
4. HSA to the max, if eligible — the best tax treatment available.
5. Roth or traditional IRA to the max.
6. Back to the 401(k) up to the $24,500 limit.
7. 529 if you have education goals; mega-backdoor Roth if your plan allows after-tax contributions.
8. Taxable brokerage for everything else.

**Asset location** — the follow-on question of *which* investments go in *which* account — is covered in the tax section of this library. The short version: bonds and REITs in tax-deferred accounts, broad stock index funds in taxable and Roth.

## Key takeaways

- The 2026 headline limits: **$24,500** 401(k), **$7,500** IRA, **$4,400 / $8,750** HSA, **$72,000** total 401(k)/SEP/Solo, **$19,000** per-donor 529 gift; catch-ups of $8,000 (401k, 50+), $11,250 (60–63), $1,100 (IRA), $1,000 (HSA, 55+).
- Always capture the full employer match first — it is a guaranteed 50–100% return.
- Roth accounts trade a deduction today for tax-free withdrawals and no RMDs; favor them when your current tax rate is low. Roth IRA contributions can be withdrawn any time.
- The HSA is the only triple-tax-free account; treat it as a stealth retirement account and pay medical bills out of pocket if you can.
- Self-employed: a Solo 401(k) allows far larger contributions than a SEP at moderate incomes.
- 529 plans now allow $20,000/yr for K-12 and a $35,000 lifetime rollover to a Roth IRA, greatly reducing the risk of over-saving.
- Taxable accounts have no limits and no rules; hold tax-efficient assets there and remember long-term gains are taxed at 0/15/20%.
- Starting in 2026, high earners (> $150,000 prior-year wages) must make 401(k) catch-up contributions as Roth.
