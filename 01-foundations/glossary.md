# Glossary of Essential Investing Terms

**In one sentence:** These are the roughly one hundred words that appear constantly in investing writing, each defined in plain language so that the rest of this library — and any prospectus, earnings call, or financial article — reads clearly.

## A

- **Accredited investor** — An individual meeting SEC wealth or income tests (roughly $1M net worth excluding home, or $200K income) who is allowed to buy private, unregistered investments.
- **Active management** — Trying to beat a market index by picking securities or timing trades, as opposed to simply tracking the index.
- **Alpha** — Return above what a benchmark would predict for the risk taken; the measure of manager skill (or luck).
- **Annuity** — An insurance contract that converts a lump sum into a stream of payments, often for life.
- **Ask (offer)** — The lowest price a seller currently accepts; you buy at the ask.
- **Asset allocation** — The percentage split of a portfolio among stock, bonds, cash, and other asset classes; the main driver of risk and return.
- **Asset class** — A group of investments with similar characteristics and behavior: stocks, bonds, cash, real estate, commodities.
- **Asset location** — Deciding which investments go in taxable vs. tax-advantaged accounts to minimize taxes.

## B

- **Backdoor Roth** — Contributing to a non-deductible traditional IRA and converting to Roth, used by people above the Roth income limits.
- **Basis (cost basis)** — What you paid for an investment, including commissions; gain or loss is measured from here.
- **Basis point (bp)** — One hundredth of a percent. 50 bps = 0.50%.
- **Bear market** — A decline of 20% or more from a recent peak.
- **Benchmark** — The index a fund or portfolio is measured against, e.g., the S&P 500.
- **Beta** — How much a security moves relative to the market; 1.0 moves in step, 1.5 amplifies, 0.5 dampens.
- **Bid** — The highest price a buyer currently offers; you sell at the bid.
- **Bid-ask spread** — The gap between bid and ask; a hidden trading cost.
- **Bond** — A loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns principal at maturity.
- **Book value** — A company's assets minus liabilities per its balance sheet; "price-to-book" compares it to the stock price.
- **Broker (brokerage)** — A firm that executes trades and holds your securities.
- **Bull market** — A sustained rise in prices, conventionally 20%+ from a low.
- **Buyback (share repurchase)** — A company buying its own shares, which raises each remaining share's slice of the business.

## C

- **Capital gain / loss** — Profit or loss from selling an investment for more or less than its basis.
- **Capital gains tax** — Tax on realized gains; lower "long-term" rates (0/15/20%) apply after holding more than one year.
- **Cash equivalents** — Very safe, very liquid holdings: money-market funds, Treasury bills, short CDs.
- **Commodity** — A raw material traded in bulk: oil, gold, wheat, copper.
- **Compound interest / compounding** — Earning returns on prior returns; the engine of long-term wealth.
- **Correlation** — How closely two assets move together, from +1 (identical) to −1 (opposite); diversification needs low correlation.
- **Coupon** — The fixed interest payment on a bond, quoted as a percent of face value.
- **Credit risk (default risk)** — The chance a borrower fails to pay interest or principal.
- **Custodian** — The institution that legally holds your assets (often the broker itself).

## D

- **Diversification** — Owning many different assets so no single failure ruins you.
- **Dividend** — Cash a company pays shareholders out of profits, usually quarterly.
- **Dividend yield** — Annual dividends divided by share price.
- **Dollar-cost averaging** — Investing a fixed amount on a schedule regardless of price.
- **Drawdown** — The decline from a peak to a subsequent low, in percent.
- **Duration** — A bond's sensitivity to interest rates; a duration of 6 means roughly a 6% price drop if rates rise 1 percentage point.

## E

- **Earnings per share (EPS)** — Net profit divided by shares outstanding.
- **Efficient market hypothesis** — The theory that prices already reflect all available information, making consistent outperformance very hard.
- **Emergency fund** — Cash covering 3–6 months of expenses, held outside the investment portfolio.
- **Equity** — Ownership; in markets, a synonym for stock.
- **ETF (exchange-traded fund)** — A pooled fund that trades on an exchange all day like a stock.
- **Expense ratio** — The annual percentage of assets a fund charges; deducted invisibly from returns.

## F

- **Fiduciary** — A person legally required to act in your best interest (not all financial advisors are).
- **Fixed income** — Investments that pay set interest: bonds, CDs, preferred stock.
- **Free cash flow** — Cash a business generates after capital spending; what's truly available to owners.
- **Fundamental analysis** — Valuing a security by studying the underlying business, financials, and economy.
- **Futures** — Contracts to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date.

## G–H

- **Growth stock** — A company expected to grow earnings faster than average, typically priced at a premium.
- **Hedge** — An investment made to offset potential losses in another.
- **Hedge fund** — A lightly regulated private fund for accredited investors, often using leverage and complex strategies.
- **High-yield (junk) bond** — A bond rated below investment grade; pays more to compensate for higher default risk.

## I

- **Index** — A rules-based list of securities used as a market yardstick (S&P 500, Nasdaq, Russell 2000).
- **Index fund** — A mutual fund or ETF that simply holds an index's constituents at minimal cost.
- **Inflation** — The general rise in prices over time, which erodes money's purchasing power.
- **Interest-rate risk** — The chance that rising rates reduce the value of existing bonds.
- **IPO (initial public offering)** — A company's first sale of shares to the public.
- **IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement)** — A tax-advantaged personal retirement account, traditional (pre-tax) or Roth (after-tax).

## L

- **Large-cap / mid-cap / small-cap** — Company size by market capitalization, roughly >$10B / $2–10B / <$2B.
- **Leverage** — Using borrowed money to invest, which magnifies both gains and losses.
- **Limit order** — An order to buy or sell only at a specified price or better.
- **Liquidity** — How quickly and cheaply an asset can be converted to cash.
- **Load** — A sales commission on a mutual fund, charged on purchase (front-end) or sale (back-end).

## M

- **Margin** — Borrowing from your broker to buy securities, using your holdings as collateral.
- **Margin call** — A broker's demand for more cash when leveraged positions fall too far.
- **Market capitalization (market cap)** — Share price × shares outstanding; the market's price for the whole company.
- **Market maker** — A firm that continuously quotes bids and asks, providing liquidity and earning the spread.
- **Market order** — An order to trade immediately at the best available price.
- **Maturity** — The date a bond repays its principal.
- **Money-market fund** — A mutual fund holding very short-term, high-quality debt; a cash substitute.
- **Mutual fund** — A pooled investment priced once daily at net asset value.

## N–O

- **NAV (net asset value)** — A fund's assets minus liabilities, per share.
- **Nominal return** — Return before adjusting for inflation.
- **Options** — Contracts giving the right (not the obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a set price by a set date.

## P

- **P/E ratio (price-to-earnings)** — Share price divided by earnings per share; a basic valuation gauge.
- **Passive investing** — Buying and holding index funds rather than trying to beat the market.
- **Portfolio** — The complete collection of your investments.
- **Preferred stock** — A hybrid security with fixed dividends and priority over common stock but usually no voting rights.
- **Principal** — The original amount invested or borrowed, before gains or interest.
- **Prospectus** — The legal document describing a fund's or offering's objectives, risks, and fees.

## Q–R

- **Qualified dividend** — A dividend taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rate.
- **Real return** — Return after subtracting inflation; what actually buys more.
- **Rebalancing** — Periodically buying and selling to restore your target asset allocation.
- **Recession** — A significant, broad decline in economic activity, usually lasting more than a few months.
- **REIT (real estate investment trust)** — A company owning income-producing property that must pay out at least 90% of taxable income as dividends.
- **Required minimum distribution (RMD)** — The mandatory annual withdrawal from tax-deferred accounts starting at age 73 (75 for those born in 1960 or later).
- **Risk capacity** — How much loss your financial situation can absorb.
- **Risk tolerance** — How much loss you can endure emotionally without abandoning your plan.
- **Roth** — An account type funded with after-tax money whose qualified withdrawals are tax-free.

## S

- **Sector** — A broad slice of the economy (technology, healthcare, energy, financials, etc.).
- **Securities** — Tradable financial assets: stocks, bonds, funds, options.
- **SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)** — The U.S. federal regulator of securities markets.
- **Sequence-of-returns risk** — The danger that poor returns early in retirement, combined with withdrawals, permanently impair a portfolio.
- **Settlement** — The transfer of cash and securities after a trade; now T+1 (one business day) for U.S. stocks.
- **Sharpe ratio** — Excess return per unit of volatility; a measure of risk-adjusted return.
- **Short selling** — Borrowing shares to sell now, hoping to buy them back cheaper later.
- **Standard deviation** — The statistical measure of how widely returns scatter; the standard proxy for volatility.
- **Stock (share)** — A unit of ownership in a corporation.
- **Stop order (stop-loss)** — An order that becomes a market order once a trigger price is hit.

## T

- **Target-date fund** — A single fund that holds a diversified mix and automatically grows more conservative as a chosen year approaches.
- **Tax-loss harvesting** — Selling losers to realize losses that offset taxable gains.
- **Technical analysis** — Forecasting prices from charts, patterns, and trading volume.
- **Ticker symbol** — The short code identifying a security (AAPL, VTI).
- **TIPS** — Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities; government bonds whose principal rises with inflation.
- **Total return** — Price change plus dividends or interest, reinvested.
- **Treasuries** — U.S. government debt: bills (≤1 year), notes (2–10 years), bonds (20–30 years).

## V–Y

- **Valuation** — The process or result of estimating what a security is worth.
- **Value stock** — A company priced low relative to earnings, book value, or cash flow.
- **Vesting** — The schedule by which employer contributions become irrevocably yours.
- **Volatility** — How much and how fast prices move; usually measured by standard deviation.
- **Wash-sale rule** — A tax rule disallowing a loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.
- **Yield** — The income an investment pays as a percent of its price.
- **Yield curve** — A plot of Treasury yields by maturity; when short rates exceed long rates ("inverted"), recessions have often followed.
- **Yield to maturity (YTM)** — A bond's total annualized return if held to maturity, assuming coupons are reinvested.

## Key takeaways

- Most investing jargon reduces to a few families: what you own (stock, bond, fund), how it's priced (bid, ask, NAV, P/E), how risky it is (volatility, beta, drawdown), and how it's taxed (basis, capital gain, Roth, RMD).
- **Real vs. nominal** and **total return vs. price return** are the two distinctions most often glossed over in headlines.
- **Expense ratio**, **load**, and **spread** are the three costs to look for before buying any fund.
- **Risk tolerance** (emotional) and **risk capacity** (financial) are different questions; plan around the lower one.
- **Duration** for bonds and **beta** for stocks are the quickest single numbers for "how much will this move?"
- If a term isn't here, Investopedia's dictionary and the Bogleheads wiki are the two best free references.
