# Asset Classes Explained

**In one sentence:** An asset class is a group of investments that behave similarly and are governed by the same economic forces — stocks, bonds, cash, real estate, commodities, gold, crypto, and private markets each play a different role, and the job of a portfolio is to combine them so that the whole is steadier than any part.

## Why asset classes matter

Two investments in the same asset class (Coca-Cola and Pepsi) will usually rise and fall together. Two in different classes (Coca-Cola and a 10-year Treasury bond) often won't. The mix of asset classes — your **asset allocation** — explains the large majority of a diversified portfolio's return variability over time, far more than which specific fund or stock you picked within each class. Get the mix right first; everything else is detail.

## Stocks (equities)

A share of stock is a slice of ownership in a business. You make money two ways: dividends (cash the company pays out) and price appreciation (the business becomes worth more). Over the long run stock returns track corporate earnings growth plus dividends — about 10% nominal a year in the U.S. since 1928, or ~7% after inflation.

The price of that return is volatility. Stocks fell more than 20% in six calendar years since 1928, and peak-to-trough declines of 50% or more happened in 1929–32, 1973–74, 2000–02, and 2007–09. Stocks belong to money you won't need for at least 5–10 years.

Subcategories worth knowing: **large-cap vs. small-cap** (company size), **growth vs. value** (expensive fast-growers vs. cheap slow-growers), **domestic vs. developed international vs. emerging markets**. U.S. stocks are ~60% of global market value; most planners suggest holding some of the other 40% because leadership rotates over decades — international beat the U.S. in the 1980s and 2000s, and the U.S. dominated the 1990s and 2010s.

## Bonds (fixed income)

A bond is a loan. You lend money to a government or company; they pay interest (the **coupon**) and return the principal at **maturity**. Bonds have two main risk dials:

- **Interest-rate risk (duration):** when market rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons lose value. Longer maturities swing more. In 2022, the fastest rate hikes in decades produced the worst year for 10-year Treasuries in the dataset (−17.8%).
- **Credit risk:** the borrower may not pay. Treasuries have essentially none; investment-grade corporates a little; "high-yield" or "junk" bonds a lot (and they behave more like stocks in a crisis).

Bonds return less than stocks (Treasuries ~4.5% nominal, ~1.5% real historically) but usually hold their value — or rise — when stocks crash, because investors flee to safety and central banks cut rates. That negative-or-low correlation is why a 60/40 stock/bond portfolio has a much smoother path than 100% stocks. The exception is inflation shocks (1970s, 2022), when stocks and bonds fall together. **TIPS** (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) and **I Bonds** are designed specifically for that scenario.

## Cash and cash equivalents

Savings accounts, money-market funds, Treasury bills, and CDs. Return roughly tracks the Federal Reserve's policy rate — near zero in 2010–2021, 4–5% in 2023–2025. Cash never has a losing nominal year, but its real return since 1928 is roughly zero, and negative in inflationary decades. Its job is liquidity: emergency funds, money needed within about two years, and optionality to buy when others are forced to sell. It is not a long-term investment.

## Real estate and REITs

You can own property directly (a home, rentals) or through **REITs** — Real Estate Investment Trusts, companies that own portfolios of buildings and must pay out at least 90% of taxable income as dividends. U.S. house *prices* have grown about 4.2% a year since 1928 — roughly inflation plus 1% — and most of the return from direct real estate comes from rental income and leverage (a mortgage), not price appreciation. Publicly traded REITs have delivered roughly 9–10% total returns since the early 1970s, similar to stocks, with similar volatility and a heavy sensitivity to interest rates. REITs are correlated with stocks (roughly 0.6–0.7) but not perfectly, and are a reasonable 5–10% satellite for income and inflation exposure. Note that a broad stock index fund already contains REITs (about 2–3% of the S&P 500).

## Commodities

Oil, natural gas, industrial and precious metals, grains. Commodities produce no cash flow, so their long-run real return is low and hard to pin down; investors mostly hold them because they tend to rise when inflation surprises to the upside — exactly when stocks and bonds struggle. Most individuals get exposure through futures-based ETFs, which carry an extra "roll" cost when futures prices are above spot. A small allocation (0–5%) is defensible as insurance; large allocations have historically been a drag.

## Gold

Gold is the oldest store of value and behaves unlike anything else: it pays nothing, its long-run return (~5.6% nominal since 1928, ~2.5% real) sits between bonds and stocks, and its volatility (~21%) is as high as stocks. Its value comes from low correlation — gold rose in 1973–74, 2000–02, and 2008–11 while stocks fell, and it soared during the 2020s' inflation and geopolitical scares (+66% in 2025 alone). It also went nowhere for 25 years after 1980. Treat it as insurance, sized at 0–10%.

## Crypto

Bitcoin and other digital assets are the newest and most speculative class. Bitcoin's returns since 2010 are astronomical but started from zero and include multiple 70–85% collapses (2014, 2018, 2022). It has no cash flows, so it cannot be valued the way stocks or bonds can; its price is a function of adoption and sentiment. Correlation with tech stocks rose sharply after 2020, undermining the "digital gold" diversification thesis. Spot Bitcoin and Ether ETFs (approved 2024) make holding easy, but the sensible sizing is "money you can watch fall 80% without it changing your life" — for most people 0–5%.

## Private markets

Private equity (buying whole companies), venture capital (funding startups), private credit (direct lending), and private real estate. Historically available only to institutions and wealthy "accredited" investors, now creeping into 401(k)s and interval funds. Reported returns look high and smooth, but the smoothness is partly an artifact of infrequent, self-reported valuations, and after fees (typically 2% of assets plus 20% of profits) the median fund has not clearly beaten a leveraged small-cap index. Lockups of 5–10 years are normal. For most individual investors these are optional at best.

## How correlations behave

Correlation ranges from +1 (move together perfectly) to −1 (move in perfect opposition); 0 means unrelated. Typical long-run values against U.S. stocks:

| Asset | Correlation with U.S. stocks (approx.) | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| International stocks | +0.8 to +0.9 | Rises in crises |
| Treasury bonds | ~0 (ranged −0.4 to +0.5 by decade) | Positive during inflation shocks |
| Cash | ~0 | — |
| REITs | +0.6 to +0.7 | Rose after 2008 |
| Commodities | +0.2 to +0.4 | Higher since 2008 |
| Gold | ~0 | Its main selling point |
| Bitcoin | +0.3 to +0.6 since 2020 | Was near zero before |

The uncomfortable truth: correlations among risky assets rise toward +1 in a panic, exactly when you want diversification most. High-quality government bonds and cash are the only assets that reliably held up in 1987, 2000–02, 2008, and 2020 — which is why they remain the core diversifier despite low returns.

## Putting it together

A classic starting framework, before any personalization:

- **Growth core:** total U.S. stock market + total international stock market (60–100% depending on horizon and stomach).
- **Ballast:** high-quality bonds, adding roughly one percentage point per year of age is a common rule of thumb ("age in bonds," often modified to "age minus 10 or 20").
- **Liquidity:** 3–6 months of expenses in cash, held outside the portfolio.
- **Optional satellites:** REITs, gold, commodities, crypto, each small enough that a bad decade doesn't derail the plan.

Later sections of this library cover the specific strategies for tilting away from this baseline.

## Key takeaways

- Asset allocation — the mix of classes — drives most of a diversified portfolio's risk and return; fund selection is secondary.
- Stocks: ~10% nominal / ~7% real historically, with periodic 50% declines. The growth engine for long horizons.
- Bonds: ~4.5% nominal for Treasuries, lower volatility, and usually (not always) rise when stocks crash. The shock absorber.
- Cash: safe in nominal terms, roughly zero real return. For liquidity, not growth.
- REITs behave like rate-sensitive stocks with high income; a broad index fund already includes them.
- Gold and commodities earn their place through low correlation and inflation protection, not high returns.
- Crypto and private markets are speculative or illiquid satellites; size them so an 80% loss is survivable.
- Correlations converge toward 1 in crises — only Treasuries and cash have consistently held up.
