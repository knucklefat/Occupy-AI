# Core Principles of Investing

**In one sentence:** Almost everything that works in investing reduces to a handful of ideas — let compounding run for a long time, stay invested rather than trying to time the market, spread your bets, keep costs and taxes low, accept that higher expected return always comes bundled with higher risk, and measure your progress after inflation, not before.

## 1. Compounding: return on your returns

Compounding means your gains start earning gains of their own. It is slow at first and absurd at the end. At 7% a year (roughly the long-run *real* return of U.S. stocks — see below), money doubles about every 10 years. The "Rule of 72" is the shortcut: divide 72 by the annual return to get the doubling time (72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10 years; 72 ÷ 10 ≈ 7 years).

| Years invested | $10,000 at 7%/yr | $10,000 at 10%/yr |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | $19,700 | $25,900 |
| 20 | $38,700 | $67,300 |
| 30 | $76,100 | $174,500 |
| 40 | $149,700 | $452,600 |

The lesson hiding in the table: the last decade contributes more dollars than the first three combined. That is why *starting early* matters more than *starting big*, and why interrupting compounding — by selling in a panic, or paying fees that skim 1% a year — is so expensive.

The same math runs in reverse for debt. Credit-card interest at 20%+ compounds against you faster than any diversified portfolio can compound for you, which is why paying off high-rate debt is usually the best "investment" available.

## 2. Time in the market beats timing the market

Market timing means trying to sell before declines and buy before rallies. Almost nobody does this reliably, including professionals, because the best days cluster right next to the worst days — typically inside bear markets, when a timer is most likely to be sitting in cash.

The historical record (S&P 500 including dividends, 1928–2025, computed from Damodaran's dataset):

| Holding period | Worst annualized return | Best annualized return | % of periods that lost money |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 year | −43.8% | +52.6% | 27% |
| 5 years | −12.7% | +28.3% | 12% |
| 10 years | −1.7% | +20.1% | 6% |
| 15 years | −0.2% | +18.8% | 1% |
| 20 years | +2.4% | +17.7% | 0% |

Roughly one year in four is a losing year, but no 20-year window since 1928 has lost money in nominal terms. Time is the one input to investing that reliably reduces the odds of loss — it is not a guarantee, but it is the closest thing to one.

The practical version of this principle is **dollar-cost averaging**: investing a fixed amount on a schedule (every paycheck) regardless of headlines. It removes the timing decision entirely. (Research from Vanguard and others shows that if you already have a lump sum, investing it immediately beats drip-feeding it about two-thirds of the time — but the schedule approach is what keeps most people invested at all.)

## 3. Diversification: the only free lunch

Diversification means owning many things whose prices don't move in lockstep. Any single company can go to zero; a broad index of thousands of companies essentially cannot. Spreading across companies removes *idiosyncratic risk* (bad luck specific to one stock) and leaves only *market risk* (the whole economy having a bad year) — and market risk is the only risk you are actually paid to bear.

Diversification works on several levels:

- **Across companies** — an index fund holds hundreds or thousands.
- **Across sectors** — so a tech bust or an oil crash is a dent, not a wipeout.
- **Across countries** — the U.S. is roughly 60% of world stock market value; the other 40% has had long stretches of outperforming.
- **Across asset classes** — stocks and high-quality bonds have usually (not always; 2022 was the exception) moved differently, so a mix smooths the ride.

Economist Harry Markowitz, who won a Nobel for the math behind this, is usually credited with the line that diversification is "the only free lunch in finance": it lowers risk without lowering expected return.

## 4. Cost control: the one return you can guarantee

Fees are the only part of your return you control completely. A fund charging 1.0% a year versus one charging 0.05% sounds trivial; over 30 years at 7% gross returns it is the difference between ending with about $7.60 and about $6.10 per dollar invested — a 20% haircut on your final wealth.

Morningstar's annual fee study puts the asset-weighted average expense ratio of U.S. funds at **0.32% in 2025**, down from roughly 0.9% in the 1990s — investors have voted with their feet toward index funds. But the *equal*-weighted average of active U.S. stock funds is still about 1%, so plenty of expensive products remain.

Costs to watch, in rough order of damage:

1. **Expense ratios** on funds (the annual % skimmed off assets).
2. **Advisory fees** (commonly 1% of assets per year for a human advisor — sometimes worth it, often not).
3. **Taxes** from unnecessary trading (short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income).
4. **Trading costs** — commissions are mostly zero now, but the bid/ask spread and frequent trading still cost money.
5. **Loads, surrender charges, and 12b-1 fees** — mostly a signal to walk away.

## 5. Risk and return are the same coin

There is no asset that reliably pays more without exposing you to more — more volatility, more chance of a permanent loss, less liquidity, or longer lockups. Cash is safe and pays little; stocks pay a lot on average and periodically fall by half. Anything that advertises high returns with low risk is either mispriced (rare and temporary), illiquid, or a fraud.

The long-run U.S. record, 1928–2025 (annualized, i.e., geometric average; Damodaran data):

| Asset | Nominal return | Annual volatility (std. dev.) | Worst single year | Growth of $100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. stocks (S&P 500, with dividends) | **10.0%** | 19.4% | −43.8% (1931) | ~$1,160,000 |
| Baa corporate bonds | 6.6% | 7.7% | −15.7% (1931) | ~$54,000 |
| 10-year U.S. Treasury bonds | 4.5% | 7.9% | −17.8% (2022) | ~$7,700 |
| 3-month Treasury bills (cash) | 3.4% | 3.0% | +0.03% (2014) | ~$2,600 |
| Gold | 5.6% | 21.5% | −32.6% (1981) | ~$21,000 |
| U.S. housing (price only) | 4.2% | 6.2% | −12.0% (2008) | ~$5,600 |

(Arithmetic averages — the simple mean of yearly returns — are higher: 11.9% for stocks. Always ask which average someone is quoting; the geometric figure is what you actually compound at.)

Stocks won by a mile, but look at the volatility column: they earned that premium by being roughly 2.5× as jumpy as bonds and by delivering six calendar years of −20% or worse. The extra return is compensation for living through those years without selling.

## 6. Inflation and the difference between real and nominal

A **nominal** return is the number on your statement. A **real** return is what is left after inflation has eaten its share. Only real returns buy anything.

U.S. consumer prices have risen about **3.0% per year on average since 1928** (with wild swings: deflation of −9% to −10% in 1931–32, +14% in 1947, +13.5% in 1980, +8% in 2022). Subtracting that from the nominal table:

| Asset | Nominal (1928–2025) | Approx. real |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. stocks | 10.0% | **~7%** |
| Corporate bonds | 6.6% | ~3.5% |
| Treasury bonds | 4.5% | ~1.5% |
| Treasury bills (cash) | 3.4% | ~0.4% |

Two things jump out. First, "cash is safe" is only true in nominal terms: a dollar under the mattress in 1928 buys about five cents' worth today. Second, stocks are the only mainstream asset that has grown purchasing power at a serious rate — which is the fundamental reason long-horizon money belongs mostly in stocks.

Be careful with forward-looking numbers. The 10% nominal figure is a *historical average*, not a promise. Starting valuations matter: when stocks are expensive (as they were by most measures in 2026), forecasters such as Vanguard project only ~4–6% nominal for U.S. stocks over the next decade. Plan with conservative numbers and let upside be a pleasant surprise.

## 7. Behavior is the bottleneck

The principles above are simple; following them is not. Investors reliably buy after prices rise and sell after they fall, chase last year's winners, and trade too much. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" studies consistently find that the average fund investor earns roughly 1 percentage point per year *less* than the funds they own, purely from poorly timed buying and selling. An investment plan you can stick with through a 40% decline beats an optimal plan you will abandon.

## Key takeaways

- Compounding rewards time more than skill; the last decade of a 40-year horizon produces more dollars than the first three combined.
- Since 1928, U.S. stocks have lost money in about 1 of every 4 calendar years but in **zero** 20-year holding periods.
- Diversification removes the risks you are not paid for (single companies, single sectors) and keeps the one you are (the market).
- Long-run U.S. stock returns: **~10% nominal, ~7% real**. Treasury bonds ~4.5% nominal / ~1.5% real. Cash ~3.4% nominal / roughly zero real.
- Inflation has averaged ~3% a year; measure everything in real terms.
- Fees compound too: 1% a year for 30 years costs roughly 20% of your final wealth.
- Higher return never comes without higher risk — volatility, illiquidity, or lockups. If it seems otherwise, look harder.
- The investor's own behavior — panic selling, performance chasing — is usually the biggest drag on returns.
