# Benjamin Graham

**In one sentence:** Graham invented value investing as a discipline, teaching that a stock is a piece of a business, that the market is a moody counterparty rather than an oracle, and that the only real protection an investor has is a margin of safety between price and value.

## Who he is

Benjamin Graham (1894–1976) was an investor, Columbia Business School professor, and author. He lost heavily in the 1929 crash, rebuilt, and turned the lessons into two books that remain the foundation of security analysis: *Security Analysis* (1934, with David Dodd) and *The Intelligent Investor* (1949). His students and employees included Warren Buffett, Walter Schloss, Irving Kahn, and Bill Ruane, several of whom compiled outstanding records of their own. Buffett has said Graham's book was the most important thing he ever read about investing and calls chapters 8 (Mr. Market) and 20 (margin of safety) the two most important chapters ever written on the subject.

## Track record (verified, with caveats)

- **Graham-Newman Corporation, 1936–1956:** by Jason Zweig's calculation the fund earned **at least 14.7% per year** versus **12.2%** for the broad market, and that figure *excludes* its single greatest investment. Source: [Wikipedia, citing Zweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham-Newman_Corporation).
- **GEICO (1948):** Graham-Newman bought roughly half of GEICO for about $712,500 and distributed the shares to its holders. Graham wrote that the profits from GEICO ultimately exceeded those from everything else the firm did in 20 years combined. Including GEICO, some writers put the firm's return near 20% a year; that higher number is widely repeated but not verifiable from primary data, so treat 14.7%+ as the floor.
- The record is pre-SEC-style disclosure and pre-standardised reporting, so all Graham-Newman numbers are reconstructions.

## Core philosophy

**Investment versus speculation.** Graham's opening distinction: an investment operation is one that, after thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Everything else is speculation. He didn't forbid speculation; he demanded you know which one you're doing and size it accordingly.

**Mr. Market.** Imagine you own a share in a private business with a partner named Mr. Market who, every day, offers to buy your stake or sell you his at a price that swings with his mood. Some days he's euphoric and quotes absurdly high; some days he's despairing and quotes absurdly low. You are free to ignore him. The lesson: price quotations are a service to be used, not a verdict to be obeyed.

**Margin of safety.** Never pay full value. If you estimate a business is worth $100 a share, buy at $60, so that an error in your estimate or bad luck in the business still leaves you whole. Graham considered this the central concept of investment.

**Quantitative cheapness.** Graham's favourite hunting ground was "net-nets": stocks trading below their net current asset value (current assets minus all liabilities), meaning you paid less than the liquidation value of the working capital and got the factories and the business for free. He also bought diversified baskets of low price-to-earnings, low price-to-book stocks with strong balance sheets, relying on the statistics of the group rather than deep insight into any single company.

**The defensive versus the enterprising investor.** Graham split his readers in two. The defensive investor wants adequate results with minimal effort: a 50/50 stock/bond split (never less than 25% in either), a diversified list of large, conservatively financed, dividend-paying companies bought at reasonable multiples, and dollar-cost averaging. The enterprising investor, willing to do the work, can hunt bargains, special situations, and net-nets. Most people, he said honestly, should be defensive.

## Rules you can steal

- **Decide whether you're investing or speculating, and keep the two in separate accounts.** Graham suggested a small, capped "speculative" fund so it can't contaminate the real money.
- **Only buy with a margin of safety.** Write down your value estimate and your required discount before you look at the price.
- **Use Mr. Market, don't obey him.** A falling price is an invitation to check the value, not a signal that the value fell.
- **Defensive-investor screen:** large company, conservative financing (current assets at least twice current liabilities for industrials), uninterrupted dividends for 20 years, earnings growth over ten years, price under 15 times average three-year earnings and 1.5 times book value.
- **Rebalance between stocks and bonds.** Keep stocks between 25% and 75% of the portfolio, leaning toward the low end when markets are expensive.
- **Diversify when you're relying on statistics.** A basket of 20–30 cheap stocks lets the odds work; concentration requires knowledge Graham didn't think most people had.
- **Dollar-cost average.** Fixed sums at fixed intervals remove the temptation to time.
- **Beware of the new and popular.** Graham warned that new issues, hot industries, and "growth at any price" are where amateurs lose most.

## Best things he's written or said

- *The Intelligent Investor* (1949; the 1973 fourth edition is the one Buffett recommends, ideally with Jason Zweig's commentary in the 2003 edition). Read chapters 8 and 20 first.
- *Security Analysis* (1934, with David Dodd). The professional's textbook; dense but foundational.
- "A Conversation with Benjamin Graham" (*Financial Analysts Journal*, 1976): late in life he conceded that elaborate analysis had become less rewarding and recommended simple, mechanical, diversified approaches.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Net-nets are nearly extinct.** The bargains Graham lived on existed because information was scarce and screens didn't exist; in modern markets, stocks below liquidation value are rare and usually rare for a reason.
- **His method undervalues great businesses.** Graham's balance-sheet focus systematically passed on companies whose value lies in brands, networks, or software. Buffett and Munger had to add Fisher to Graham to solve this.
- **His own best investment broke his rules.** GEICO was a concentrated bet on a growth company, held for decades: the opposite of the diversified cigar-butt approach, and it made more than everything else combined.
- **Return record is modest by modern-legend standards.** Beating the market by 2.5 points a year (ex-GEICO) is excellent but not miraculous, and the data is reconstructed.
- **Formulas date.** His specific thresholds (P/E under 15, etc.) were calibrated to mid-century interest rates and accounting.

## Key takeaways

- Graham-Newman beat the market by at least 2.5 percentage points a year over two decades, before its single best idea (GEICO) is counted.
- Three ideas carry the whole system: stocks are businesses, Mr. Market serves you, and margin of safety protects you.
- Separate investing from speculation explicitly; size the speculative part so it can't hurt.
- Most investors should be "defensive": diversified, low-cost, rebalanced, and unbothered by daily prices.
- His quantitative screens have decayed; his psychological framework has not aged a day.
- Even Graham, late in life, leaned toward simple mechanical rules over deep analysis for ordinary investors.
