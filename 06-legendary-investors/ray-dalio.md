# Ray Dalio

**In one sentence:** Dalio built Bridgewater, the largest hedge fund in the world, on the idea that economies and markets run like machines whose cause-and-effect relationships can be written down, tested against history, and turned into systematic rules, and that diversification across uncorrelated bets is the closest thing investing has to a free lunch.

## Who he is

Ray Dalio (born 1949) founded Bridgewater Associates in 1975 from his two-bedroom apartment in New York. It grew into the world's largest hedge fund, managing around $150 billion at its peak, largely for pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and endowments. Its two flagship strategies are **Pure Alpha** (launched 1991, an actively managed global macro fund) and **All Weather** (launched 1996, a passive "risk parity" portfolio designed to perform acceptably in any economic environment). Dalio stepped back from day-to-day management in 2017 and gave up control of the firm in 2022. He is also known for *Principles* (2017), a manual of the "radical transparency" management culture he built, and for his historical studies of debt crises and the rise and fall of empires.

## Track record (verified, with caveats)

- **Pure Alpha, December 1991–2019:** average annual return of about **11.5%** over 28 years, net of fees, with positive results in all but three years to 2018. Notable years: **+9.4% in 2008** while the S&P 500 fell 37%, **+14.6% in 2018** when most assets fell, then a rare loss in 2020. Source: [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsxa6cu6tic60cizc8ao/portfolio/in-disappointing-year-bridgewaters-flagship-fund-returns-0-5). Earlier reporting (2015) put the figure at [over 13% annualised](https://www.pionline.com/article/20150219/INTERACTIVE/150219857/bridgewater-s-pure-alpha-strategy-up-more-than-13-annually-since-its-inception/); the difference reflects weak years in the late 2010s.
- **Caveat:** Bridgewater's funds are private and results reach the public through press reports and investor letters, not audited public filings. Pure Alpha runs at different volatility targets ("12%" and "18%" versions), and the two can diverge in a given year. Performance in 2020–2024 was mixed, with a strong 2022 and a weak 2020; the long-run number has drifted down accordingly.
- Bridgewater is reported to have earned more total dollars for clients than any hedge fund in history, a claim based on LCH Investments' annual rankings.

## Core philosophy

**The economic machine.** Dalio's model: the economy is driven by productivity growth (slow, steady), the short-term debt cycle (5–8 years, driven by central banks), and the long-term debt cycle (50–75 years, ending in deleveraging). Every transaction is someone's spending and someone else's income; credit lets spending exceed income temporarily, which creates cycles. His free 30-minute video "How the Economic Machine Works" is the clearest statement.

**Systematise everything.** Bridgewater writes down its decision rules, back-tests them across countries and centuries, and lets the rules trade. Dalio's stated goal was to make his own judgment reproducible so it didn't depend on him being right in the moment.

**Timeless and universal.** A rule is only trusted if it would have worked in the 1930s United States, 1990s Japan, and 2010s Europe alike. This is why Dalio studies the Weimar hyperinflation and the Dutch empire: to find what recurs.

**Risk parity and the "Holy Grail."** Dalio's most transferable idea: if you can find 15 or 20 good return streams that are genuinely uncorrelated, combining them cuts risk by roughly 80% without cutting expected return. All Weather applies this to asset classes: instead of a 60/40 portfolio where stocks contribute 90% of the risk, it balances *risk* (not dollars) across stocks, long bonds, inflation-linked bonds, and commodities so that growth, recession, inflation, and deflation each have assets that do well.

**Pain + reflection = progress.** From *Principles*: mistakes are data. Dalio traces his own approach to a nearly firm-ending wrong call on a 1982 depression, after which he became, in his words, humble enough to ask "how do I know I'm right?"

**Believability-weighted decision making.** Weigh opinions by the track record of the person offering them, not by seniority or volume.

## Rules you can steal

- **Balance risk, not dollars.** A 60/40 portfolio is really a stock portfolio. Consider how much of your risk each holding contributes, not just its weight.
- **Prepare for four environments.** Rising growth, falling growth, rising inflation, falling inflation. Own something that works in each; you will not know which one is coming.
- **Diversify across uncorrelated things, not just many things.** Twenty stocks are one bet on stocks. Stocks, bonds, gold, and inflation-linked bonds are four bets.
- **Write down your rules before you need them.** What will you do when stocks fall 30%? Decide now, in writing.
- **Study history you didn't live through.** Most investors' sample size is their own career. Read about 1929, 1970s inflation, and Japan after 1989.
- **Ask "how do I know I'm right?"** Seek the smartest person who disagrees with you and understand their reasoning before you act.
- **Treat pain as a signal to learn, not to quit.** Log every mistake with its cause and the principle you'll apply next time.
- **Don't bet the firm on a forecast.** Dalio's own worst year came from being certain about a macro call; his best rule is to never be certain.

## Best things he's written or said

- [How the Economic Machine Works (video, 2013)](https://www.economicprinciples.org/): 30 minutes; the best free introduction to credit cycles anywhere.
- *Principles: Life and Work* (2017): the management and decision-making rules.
- *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises* (2018): a template for deleveragings with 48 historical cases; free PDF on his site.
- *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* (2021): the rise-and-fall-of-empires framework.
- [Bridgewater Research and Insights](https://www.bridgewater.com/research-and-insights): the firm's public papers, including the original "All Weather" story and "Engineering Targeted Returns and Risks."

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Returns have faded.** Pure Alpha's recent decade is far below its early record, and All Weather struggled badly in 2022 when stocks and bonds fell together, the exact scenario risk parity is meant to handle. Correlations are not stable.
- **Risk parity needs leverage.** To get equity-like returns from a bond-heavy portfolio you must borrow; retail versions without leverage are just conservative balanced funds.
- **The culture is divisive.** *Principles* and Bridgewater's recorded-meeting, rate-your-colleague-in-real-time culture have been described by former employees as oppressive; several books and articles (notably Rob Copeland's *The Fund*, 2023) dispute how systematic Bridgewater's process really was.
- **Macro forecasting is hard even for him.** Dalio's public calls (e.g., "cash is trash" in early 2020 just before a crash, or repeated warnings of imminent debt crises) have a mixed record.
- **Opaque.** As a private fund, Bridgewater's numbers are not independently verifiable in the way Berkshire's are.

## Key takeaways

- Pure Alpha compounded at roughly 11.5% net for 28 years with only a few down years, including a positive 2008; the record has softened since.
- The economy is a machine of cycles driven by credit; learn the short and long debt cycles.
- Diversify across genuinely uncorrelated return streams; balance risk, not dollars.
- Prepare a portfolio for all four growth/inflation environments rather than predicting one.
- Write down your rules and test them against history you didn't live through.
- Treat mistakes as the raw material of principles; ask constantly how you know you're right.
