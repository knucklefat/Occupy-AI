# Nick Sleep and the Nomad Investment Partnership

**In one sentence:** Sleep and his partner Qais Zakaria ran Nomad for 13 years, returned 921% versus 117% for the world index, and then closed the fund at the top of their game, leaving behind a set of letters that explain better than anyone else's why a business that gives its scale advantages back to customers (Costco, Amazon) becomes almost impossible to compete with.

## Who they are

Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria were analysts at Marathon Asset Management in London when, in September 2001, they launched the **Nomad Investment Partnership** inside Marathon, taking it independent in 2006 as Sleep, Zakaria and Company. They ran it until the end of 2013, then wound it up, returned capital to partners, and retired from managing money to focus on philanthropy; Sleep runs the IGY Foundation, through which the full set of Nomad letters was made public in 2021. Neither has written a book or sought publicity, which is part of why the letters have acquired a cult following among investors.

## Track record (verified)

- **Nomad Investment Partnership, September 2001 – December 2013:** cumulative return of about **921% net of fees**, versus about **117%** for the MSCI World Index, or roughly **20.8% a year versus 6.5%**. Sources: [Quartr](https://quartr.com/insights/investment-strategy/nick-sleep-from-stellar-returns-to-legendary-letters) and [Steady Compounding](https://steadycompounding.com/investing/sleep/), both citing the final Nomad letter. The letters themselves are the primary source and are [freely available from the IGY Foundation (PDF)](https://igyfoundation.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Full_Collection_Nomad_Letters_.pdf).
- **Fee structure:** Nomad charged a low fixed fee to cover costs (not a percentage-of-assets management fee) plus a performance fee only on returns above a 6% annual hurdle, with the performance fee held back and subject to clawback if later results fell short. The exact terms are described in the letters; the principle, that the managers should be paid only for results the partners could not have got in an index fund, is the point. (Specific percentages here are from secondary summaries of the letters; check the primary document before quoting them.)
- **The end state:** by 2013 the fund held about three stocks, Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway, and Sleep and Zakaria continued holding them personally after closing. Amazon's rise from around $400 at the fund's closure to over $3,000 (pre-split) by 2020 is not in the record but is part of the legend.
- **2008:** Nomad fell more than the index that year (a drawdown around 45% is cited in secondary sources), then recovered strongly; the letters from that period are among the best writing on holding through a crisis.

## Core philosophy

**Scale economics shared.** Most businesses that get bigger keep the resulting cost savings as higher margins. A few, Costco above all, give the savings back to customers as lower prices. The result is a loop: lower prices bring more customers, more customers bring more scale, more scale brings lower costs, which fund lower prices. Competitors can't match the prices without matching the scale, and can't get the scale without the prices. Sleep noticed that this model looked *bad* on the usual metrics (thin margins, low reported returns) and that this is exactly why the market misprices it. Amazon, he argued in 2004–2006 when it was widely considered a money-losing bookseller, was Costco on the internet.

**Destination analysis.** Instead of asking what the next quarter looks like, Sleep asked what the business will look like in ten or twenty years, what it must do to get there, and whether management is doing those things. Price reductions and heavy reinvestment that depress today's earnings are, from this angle, the most important investments a company makes, and they never appear on the balance sheet.

**Information has a half-life.** Most of what investors consume (quarterly numbers, macro commentary, price targets) is worthless within weeks. Sleep tried to spend his time on information whose shelf life was measured in years: the business model, the culture, the founder's incentives, the customers' habits.

**Founders and psychology.** Nomad concentrated on companies run by founders (Bezos, Sinegal at Costco, Buffett) who could ignore Wall Street's demands for margin expansion. Sleep argued that the only two durable investing edges are analytical (thinking better) and psychological (behaving better), and that informational edges vanish quickly.

**Inactivity as a decision.** Nomad's turnover was tiny. Sleep wrote that the decision *not* to sell is as active as any purchase, and that the best investments are ones you don't need to sell. The partnership's final years were spent, in effect, owning three companies and doing very little.

**Alignment.** From the fee structure to the decision to close when the opportunity set shrank, Nomad was built so that the managers won only when the partners did. Sleep's letters repeatedly criticise the fund industry for the opposite.

## Rules you can steal

- **Ask who gets the benefit of scale.** When a company grows, do the savings go to shareholders as margin, to management as pay, or to customers as price? The last is rarest and most durable.
- **Do destination analysis.** Write down what the business looks like in 10 years and what has to happen. If you can't, you are trading, not investing.
- **Treat reinvestment as an asset.** Low margins caused by deliberate price cuts or R&D are not the same as low margins caused by weak positioning.
- **Filter information by shelf life.** Before reading anything, ask whether it will matter in three years.
- **Look for founders who ignore Wall Street.** Short-term pressure from analysts is a tax on long-term value; managers who refuse to pay it are worth a premium.
- **Do less.** Set a maximum number of trades per year and treat exceeding it as a failure of process.
- **Concentrate when the analysis is done.** Nomad's edge came from owning a few businesses in size; diversification would have diluted it to nothing.
- **Align your incentives.** If you manage money for others, get paid for outperforming what they could buy cheaply elsewhere. If you manage your own, stop measuring against the neighbours.
- **Know when to stop.** Sleep and Zakaria closed a fund with a 20% record because they thought they'd found their destination. Enough is a number.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Thirteen years, and three stocks.** The record is superb but short, and a large share of the final result came from Amazon and Costco. That is the method working as designed, but the sample size is small.
- **A 45% drawdown.** Concentration in 2008 was painful; partners who redeemed at the bottom did not get the 921%.
- **Survivorship.** Sleep was right about Amazon when many quality-growth investors were right about nothing; the letters are persuasive partly because we know how the story ended.
- **The approach is slow.** Destination analysis and near-zero turnover mean years of looking wrong; Nomad's partners were patient institutions, not individuals watching daily prices.
- **Little guidance on valuation.** The letters are rich on business quality and thin on what price to pay; Sleep's answer, that a long enough runway makes the entry price less important, is true only if the runway is really there.

## Key takeaways

- Nomad returned about 921% net versus 117% for the MSCI World over 2001–2013, roughly 20.8% a year, then closed voluntarily.
- Scale economics shared: businesses that pass scale savings to customers build loops that competitors can't break, and the usual metrics understate them.
- Ask where the business is going in ten years (destination analysis), not what it earns next quarter.
- Reinvestment that depresses today's earnings is often the most valuable thing a company does.
- Filter information by shelf life; focus on the business model, the founder, and the customer habit.
- Inactivity is a decision; concentrate, hold, and know when enough is enough.
