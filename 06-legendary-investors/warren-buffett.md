# Warren Buffett

**In one sentence:** Buffett turned a failing New England textile mill into a $1 trillion conglomerate by buying understandable businesses with durable advantages at sensible prices, holding them for decades, and treating every market panic as a shopping trip rather than a fire alarm.

## Who he is

Warren Buffett (born 1930, Omaha, Nebraska) is the chairman and, until the end of 2025, the CEO of Berkshire Hathaway. He studied under Benjamin Graham at Columbia, worked briefly at Graham's fund, ran a private investment partnership from 1957 to 1969, then used Berkshire, a struggling textile company he took control of in 1965, as the vehicle for everything that followed. Berkshire today owns dozens of operating companies outright (GEICO, BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, See's Candies) plus a large portfolio of listed stocks (Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Bank of America, five Japanese trading houses). In 2025 Buffett announced that Greg Abel would take over as CEO on January 1, 2026, and would write the annual letters from then on. Buffett remains chairman.

## Track record (verified)

- **Berkshire Hathaway, 1965–2024:** per-share market value compounded at **19.9% per year** versus **10.4%** for the S&P 500 with dividends reinvested. Overall gain 1964–2024: **5,502,284%** versus **39,054%** for the index. Source: the performance table on page 2 of the [2024 shareholder letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2024ltr.pdf).
- **Buffett Partnership, 1957–1969:** the partnership compounded at roughly **31.6% per year gross** and **25.3% net to limited partners**, against **9.1%** for the Dow, with no losing year. These numbers come from Buffett's own partnership letters as summarised by [Novel Investor](https://novelinvestor.com/notes/buffett-partnership-letters-by-warren-buffett/); they are self-reported but were never disputed.

Note the second number is the more remarkable one: the early Buffett was running a small, concentrated, Graham-style portfolio. The Berkshire-era return is lower partly because the capital base became enormous (Buffett has said repeatedly that size is an anchor on performance).

## Core philosophy

Buffett's approach evolved in three stages, and it helps to know which one you are copying.

1. **Cigar butts (1950s–60s).** Pure Graham: buy statistically cheap stocks trading below the value of their net assets, take one last "puff," move on.
2. **Wonderful businesses at fair prices (1970s onward).** Under Charlie Munger's and Philip Fisher's influence, Buffett shifted to paying up for companies with a *moat*, a durable competitive advantage (a brand, a low-cost position, a network, a regulatory franchise) that lets them earn high returns on capital for a long time. See's Candies (1972) was the turning point.
3. **Capital allocator (1980s onward).** Berkshire's real machine is *float*: insurance premiums collected now and paid out later, which Buffett invests in the meantime. Combined with retained earnings from wholly-owned subsidiaries, this gave him permanent, cheap capital that no fund manager facing redemptions can match.

Underneath all three stages sit a few constants: think of a stock as a fractional ownership of a business, not a ticker; stay inside your *circle of competence* (the set of businesses you can actually understand); insist on a *margin of safety* (pay meaningfully less than your estimate of value so that being somewhat wrong still works out); and treat "Mr. Market" as a manic-depressive business partner whose quotes you may accept or ignore.

## Rules you can steal

- **Buy businesses, not tickers.** Before buying, write one paragraph on how the company makes money and why it will still make money in ten years. If you can't, pass.
- **Define your circle of competence and stay in it.** Its size matters less than knowing where the edge is.
- **Demand a margin of safety.** Estimate value conservatively, then only buy well below that number.
- **Be fearful when others are greedy, greedy when others are fearful.** Buffett's biggest purchases (American Express 1964, Washington Post 1973, Goldman Sachs and GE 2008, Japanese trading houses 2020) were made when prices were depressed by panic, not when the outlook was rosy.
- **Concentrate when you know, diversify when you don't.** Buffett's advice to the non-professional is an S&P 500 index fund; his own portfolio is a handful of big positions.
- **Let winners run.** Coca-Cola has been held since 1988. The tax code rewards not selling; so does compounding.
- **Avoid leverage.** Berkshire always keeps a large cash cushion so it can never be forced to sell at the wrong moment.
- **Correct mistakes quickly.** In the 2024 letter he calls delaying the fix of an error "the cardinal sin."
- **Ignore forecasts.** Buffett has said for 60 years that he makes no attempt to predict markets or the economy.
- **Keep costs and activity low.** Fees, turnover, and taxes are the silent killers of compounding.

## Best things he's written or said

- [Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters (1977–present)](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html): the single best free education in business and investing. Start with 1983 (goodwill), 1987 (Mr. Market), 1992 (growth vs. value), 2008 (crisis), 2014 (50th anniversary).
- [Buffett Partnership letters, 1957–1969](https://novelinvestor.com/notes/buffett-partnership-letters-by-warren-buffett/) (summary and excerpts): the young, hungry Buffett explaining "generals, workouts, and controls."
- *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville* (1984 speech): his rebuttal to the efficient-market theory.
- Berkshire annual meeting Q&A sessions, freely available on CNBC's Buffett archive.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **You cannot clone Berkshire's structure.** Float, permanent capital, and the ability to buy whole companies are advantages no individual has.
- **Underperformance in recent decades.** Berkshire has roughly matched, not crushed, the S&P 500 over the last 15–20 years. Buffett himself warns that future results will be only "slightly better" than average because of size.
- **Missed technology for decades.** He avoided Microsoft, Google, and Amazon at their cheapest and only bought Apple in 2016. His discipline cost real money as well as saving it.
- **Governance and succession.** Berkshire's structure is unusually dependent on one person's judgment; the post-Buffett era is untested.
- **The advice-versus-practice gap.** He tells ordinary investors to index, then runs one of the most concentrated large portfolios in the world. Both are honest; just know which one applies to you.

## Key takeaways

- Berkshire's 19.9% annual compounding for 60 years is the best long-run public record of any investor operating at that scale.
- The partnership years (25% net) show the method works even better with small capital and concentrated bets.
- His edge is temperament, not IQ: patience, indifference to crowds, and the willingness to do nothing for years.
- Moats and management quality matter more than statistical cheapness once your capital grows.
- Cash is not idle; it is an option on the next panic.
- For most people, Buffett's honest advice is a low-cost index fund plus the discipline not to sell.
