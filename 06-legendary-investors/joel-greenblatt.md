# Joel Greenblatt

**In one sentence:** Greenblatt compounded at roughly 50% a year for a decade hunting spin-offs, restructurings, and other "special situations" that Wall Street ignores, then distilled the essence of value investing into a two-factor "Magic Formula" (cheap plus good) that anyone can run with a screen and a calendar.

## Who he is

Joel Greenblatt (born 1957) founded Gotham Capital in 1985 with $7 million, reportedly seeded in part by junk-bond financier Michael Milken. After a decade of extraordinary returns he returned outside capital in 1995 and ran the firm with partners' money. He has taught the value investing course at Columbia Business School since 1996, co-founded the Value Investors Club (an invitation-only idea-sharing site) in 1999, and later launched Gotham Asset Management, which runs long-short and long-only mutual funds based on systematic value ranking. He also helped fund the Success Charter Network of schools in New York.

## Track record (verified, with caveats)

- **Gotham Capital, 1985–1994:** about **50% annualised before fees**, per Greenblatt's own account in *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* and repeated in profiles such as [Quartr](https://quartr.com/insights/investment-strategy/joel-greenblatt-the-magic-of-value-investing). A dollar became roughly forty dollars in ten years. **Caveat:** self-reported, private partnership, small capital base (the fund was capped and returned money), and gross rather than net — the commonly cited breakdown is about 50% after expenses but before the general partner's incentive allocation, and roughly 30% net of all fees. Nobody has disputed the figure, but it is not an audited public number.
- **Magic Formula backtest, 1988–2004:** in *The Little Book That Beats the Market* (2005) Greenblatt reported that a portfolio of the top 30 Magic Formula stocks, rebalanced annually, returned about **30.8% a year** versus **12.4%** for the S&P 500. **Caveat:** this is a backtest run by the author, subject to the usual concerns (survivorship in the database, look-ahead bias, transaction costs, small-cap illiquidity). Independent replications have generally found meaningful outperformance but smaller than the book's figure, and with multi-year stretches of underperformance.
- **Gotham Asset Management funds (2012–present):** the public mutual funds have had mixed, often index-lagging results, particularly during the 2010s growth rally. This is the fairest test of the systematic approach at scale and it's sobering.

## Core philosophy

**Special situations.** Greenblatt's first book is a guide to corporate events that create mispriced securities: **spin-offs** (a parent distributes a subsidiary's shares to holders who didn't ask for them and often sell indiscriminately), **restructurings**, **rights offerings**, **merger securities** (odd instruments issued in deals that nobody wants), **bankruptcies**, and **recapitalisations**. The common thread: structural reasons for selling that have nothing to do with value, plus little analyst coverage.

**The Magic Formula.** Rank all stocks (above a minimum size, excluding financials and utilities) on two measures:
- **Earnings yield** = operating earnings (EBIT) divided by enterprise value (market cap plus debt minus cash). Higher is cheaper.
- **Return on capital** = EBIT divided by tangible capital employed (net working capital plus net fixed assets). Higher means a better business.

Add the two ranks, buy the top 20–30, hold each for a year, repeat. It is Buffett ("wonderful businesses at fair prices") reduced to arithmetic.

**Why it keeps working.** Greenblatt's answer is that the formula has bad years, sometimes two or three in a row, and most people can't stand that. The strategy's durability comes from its psychological difficulty, not its secrecy.

**Mr. Market, taught to MBAs.** His Columbia course and *The Big Secret for the Small Investor* (2011) return to Graham's parable: prices are offers from an emotional counterparty, value is what a business will earn, and the gap is your opportunity.

**Valuation is about the future.** In his teaching he emphasises that all valuation methods (discounted cash flow, relative value, acquisition value, liquidation value) are just ways to estimate what a business will earn, and that the honest analyst uses several and expects to be roughly right.

## Rules you can steal

- **Screen for cheap and good together.** Cheap alone buys junk; good alone overpays. The intersection is where value lives.
- **Look where selling is structural.** Spin-offs, index deletions, and post-bankruptcy equities are sold by people who don't care what they're worth.
- **Read the filings for special situations.** The Form 10 for a spin-off tells you who's getting stock options and at what price; insiders' incentives are the tell.
- **Buy a basket if you're running a formula.** 20–30 names, equal weight, replaced annually. A formula with three stocks is just three bets.
- **Commit for at least three years.** Any value strategy will underperform for stretches; decide now that you'll hold through them, or don't start.
- **Use enterprise value, not market cap.** Debt is part of the price you're paying.
- **Take care with taxes on annual rebalancing.** Greenblatt suggests selling losers just before the one-year mark and winners just after, to favour long-term capital gains.
- **Don't fall in love with the screen.** It is a starting list; a quick check for accounting oddities, one-time gains, and disappearing businesses is still required.

## Best things he's written or said

- *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* (1997): despite the title, a serious, funny, and specific guide to special situations. Many professional investors cite it as the most useful investing book they've read.
- *The Little Book That Beats the Market* (2005; updated as *The Little Book That Still Beats the Market*, 2010): the Magic Formula, explained for a twelve-year-old.
- *The Big Secret for the Small Investor* (2011): valuation and why index funds beat most people.
- [magicformulainvesting.com](https://www.magicformulainvesting.com/): the free official screen.
- His Columbia lectures, several of which circulate as notes and transcripts, and appearances on the *Invest Like the Best* and *Masters in Business* podcasts.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Gotham's record isn't checkable, and it was earned small.** 50% a year on a capped $7–50 million fund in a golden era for spin-offs is not evidence for what any strategy can do at scale.
- **The Magic Formula's live results are much weaker than the backtest.** Gotham's public funds, and independent tests since 2005, show smaller and less consistent outperformance, with long droughts (2014–2020 in particular).
- **Data-mining risk.** Two factors chosen after seeing the data, tested on that data. Greenblatt is honest about this but it remains a concern.
- **Crowding.** Once a screen is published and free, the cheapest, best names on it get bought; the residual list is small, illiquid, and often full of businesses in structural decline.
- **Special situations require time and skill.** Reading spin-off filings is a real edge; it is also a lot of work for an individual with a job.

## Key takeaways

- Gotham Capital's ~50% a year for ten years (self-reported, gross) is one of the great small-fund records; it came from special situations, not the Magic Formula.
- The Magic Formula is Buffett in arithmetic form: rank on earnings yield and return on capital, buy the top of the list, hold a year, repeat.
- The formula's backtest (30.8% a year) is far better than its live results; expect modest outperformance with painful multi-year droughts.
- Structural selling (spin-offs, index changes, bankruptcies) is the most reliable source of mispricing for a small investor.
- Any value strategy's edge is psychological: it survives because people quit during the bad years.
- Use enterprise value, run baskets not bets, and commit for years before you start.
