# Jim Simons

**In one sentence:** Simons, a code-breaker and prize-winning mathematician, built Renaissance Technologies' Medallion Fund into the best track record in investing history (about 66% a year gross, 39% net, for three decades) by ignoring everything the other investors in this section believe and letting statistical patterns, not stories, decide every trade.

## Who he is

James Simons (1938–2024) earned a PhD in mathematics at 23, broke Soviet codes for the Institute for Defense Analyses, chaired the math department at Stony Brook, and won the Oswald Veblen Prize for geometry. In 1978, at 40, he left academia to trade. After a decade of mixed results with mostly discretionary methods, he founded Renaissance Technologies (1982) and launched the Medallion Fund (1988), staffed with mathematicians, physicists, signal-processing experts, and astronomers rather than finance people. Medallion closed to outside investors in 1993 and, since 2005, has been owned entirely by Renaissance employees. Simons retired as CEO in 2010 and gave away billions to mathematics and science research.

## Track record (verified)

The figures come from the appendix to Gregory Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market* (2019), compiled from investor documents, and have been analysed by academics.

- **Medallion Fund, 1988–2018:** average annual return of about **66% before fees** and about **39% after fees**. Compound (geometric) gross return roughly **63%** a year. Source: [Cornell Capital Group's analysis of Zuckerman's data](https://www.cornell-capital.com/blog/2020/02/medallion-fund-the-ultimate-counterexample.html).
- **No losing year** in the 31-year sample. After the first two years, the worst year was **+31.5%**. It made **+56.6%** in the dot-com crash year and **+74.6%** in 2008.
- **Fees:** Medallion charged a **5% management fee and a 44% performance fee** by the end of the period, the highest in the industry, which is why the gross-net gap is so large. (Fee figures are from Zuckerman's reporting rather than the Cornell summary.)
- **The catch:** Medallion is capped at roughly $10 billion and pays out profits every year, because its strategies stop working at larger size. The fund's dollar profits are enormous but its *strategy* cannot absorb more capital.
- **Renaissance's public funds** (RIEF, RIDA), which are open to outsiders and hold positions for longer, have performed far worse, sometimes trailing the S&P 500 for years. That gap is the clearest evidence that Medallion's edge is specific to high-frequency, capacity-limited signals.

## Core philosophy

**Patterns, not narratives.** Renaissance does not ask why a signal works. It asks whether the pattern is statistically robust across huge amounts of data, whether it persists out of sample, and whether it can be traded at a cost lower than the edge. Many of Medallion's signals are, by the firm's own admission, inexplicable. Discretion is forbidden: humans build and monitor the models but do not override them.

**Small edges, enormous frequency.** Simons said the firm is right only about 50.75% of the time, but on millions of trades that is enough. This is the casino model: a house edge of a fraction of a percent, applied constantly, with the law of large numbers doing the work. Medallion's holding periods are measured in hours or days, not years.

**Data as the raw material.** Renaissance collected price histories back to the 19th century, cleaned them obsessively, and expanded to weather, shipping, earnings, and anything else that might carry a signal, long before "alternative data" became a buzzword.

**One model, one firm.** Unlike most quant shops where teams run separate strategies, Renaissance funnelled everything into a single integrated model so that signals could be netted, risks managed together, and trading costs minimised.

**Hire scientists, not financiers.** Simons believed people trained in finance carried too many priors about how markets should behave. He wanted people who would treat markets as an unknown physical system.

**Leverage and execution.** Medallion's raw signals are modest; the returns come from applying leverage (reportedly 10–20x at times, via basket options arranged with banks) and from world-class execution that keeps trading costs below the edge.

## Rules you can steal

Simons' strategy is not replicable by individuals; his *principles* partly are.

- **Trust the data over the story.** If your thesis for a stock is a narrative, ask what evidence would make you abandon it, and check whether that evidence already exists.
- **Know your edge and its capacity.** Any edge has a size limit; Medallion caps itself at $10 billion. Ask what would happen to your strategy if everyone did it.
- **Test out of sample.** A rule that worked on the data you used to find it proves nothing. Hold back data, or wait for new data, before you trust it.
- **Remove discretion where you can.** Write the rule, follow the rule, review the rule on a schedule, and never override it in the moment.
- **Costs decide marginal strategies.** A signal that earns 0.5% a trade and costs 0.6% to execute is a losing signal. Fees, spreads, and taxes kill most retail "systems."
- **Beware of fitting noise.** Simons' team threw out most patterns they found. With enough data you will always find something; the skill is in rejecting.
- **Hire (or read) outside your field.** Renaissance's edge came from people who had never worked in finance.
- **Judge yourself on process and probabilities.** A 50.75% hit rate looks like a coin flip on any given week.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Unreplicable at every level.** Hundreds of PhDs, decades of cleaned data, custom execution, bank-arranged leverage, and a capacity cap. Nothing an individual does resembles it.
- **Opacity.** Medallion's methods are secret; the record is verified only through leaked investor documents and Zuckerman's reporting. Its returns are not audited public numbers.
- **Tax controversy.** Renaissance settled with the IRS in 2021 for around $7 billion over its use of basket options to convert short-term gains into long-term ones, the largest tax settlement in US history.
- **The public funds disappoint.** RIEF's mediocre results show the Medallion machine does not scale or slow down.
- **Zero-sum.** Medallion's profits come from other market participants' trading costs and mistakes; it adds liquidity but not, arguably, much else.

## Key takeaways

- Medallion's roughly 66% gross and 39% net annual return from 1988 to 2018, with no losing year, is the best documented investment record that exists.
- It was built by mathematicians treating markets as data, with discretion removed and narratives ignored.
- The edge is tiny per trade and enormous in aggregate; frequency and execution, not insight, generate the return.
- Every edge has a capacity limit; Medallion caps itself at $10 billion and its scalable sibling funds underperform.
- For individuals, the transferable lessons are epistemic: test out of sample, distrust stories, count costs, and know when your "system" is fitting noise.
- Its existence is the strongest counterexample to the idea that markets are perfectly efficient, and also a reminder of how much infrastructure it takes to exploit that.
