# Philip Fisher and Terry Smith

**In one sentence:** Fisher (1907–2004) invented quality-growth investing, the practice of finding a small number of superbly managed companies with long runways and holding them for decades, and Terry Smith's Fundsmith is the clearest modern proof that "buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing" still works at scale.

## Who they are

**Philip Fisher** started Fisher & Company in San Francisco in 1931, in the depths of the Depression, and ran it for nearly 70 years. He never managed a large fund and left almost no public record of returns; his influence comes from *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (1958), the first investing book to make Harvard's required reading, and from Warren Buffett, who described himself as "85% Graham and 15% Fisher" and later moved much closer to Fisher. Fisher's son Ken founded Fisher Investments.

**Terry Smith** (born 1953) was a top-rated UK bank analyst who was fired by UBS in 1992 for publishing *Accounting for Growth*, a book exposing the accounting tricks of his employer's corporate clients. He ran the brokerage Collins Stewart and the interdealer broker Tullett Prebon, then in November 2010 launched **Fundsmith Equity Fund** with a concentrated, low-turnover portfolio of global quality companies. It became the largest actively managed equity fund in the UK. His annual letters, modelled on Buffett's, are unusually blunt and data-heavy.

## Track record (verified, with caveats)

**Fisher:**
- No audited record exists; he ran private accounts. The famous datapoint is his purchase of **Motorola in 1955**, which he held until his death in 2004, roughly half a century, through which the stock rose more than a hundredfold. He also held Texas Instruments from the 1950s. Source: [Investing Caffeine](https://investingcaffeine.com/2009/11/17/standing-on-the-shoulders-of-a-growth-giant-phil-fisher/) and Ken Fisher's writing. **Treat any annualised return figure for Fisher as unverified.**

**Smith:**
- **Fundsmith Equity Fund, 1 Nov 2010 – 31 Dec 2024:** cumulative **+607.3%**, or **14.8% annualised**, versus **+403.4% (12.1% annualised)** for the MSCI World Index in sterling, with lower downside volatility. Source: [Fundsmith 2024 annual letter (PDF)](https://www.fundsmith.co.uk/media/pirmvyly/annual-letter-to-shareholders-2024.pdf). These are audited, public, net-of-fees figures.
- **Recent underperformance:** the fund returned **8.9% in 2024** versus **20.8%** for the index, and has lagged for several years as a handful of mega-cap technology stocks drove the market. Smith addresses this directly in the letters.
- **Portfolio quality (2024):** the fund's holdings averaged **32% return on capital employed** and **64% gross margin**, versus 16% and 45% for the S&P 500.
- **Turnover:** about **3% a year**, meaning dealing costs added only 0.01% to the ongoing charge.

## Core philosophy

**Fisher's fifteen points.** Fisher's checklist for a company worth owning for decades asks, in summary: Does it have products with a large, multi-year growth runway? Is management determined to develop new products when today's growth slows? Is R&D effective? Is the sales organisation above average? Are profit margins worthwhile, and is the company working to improve them? Are labour relations, executive relations, and management depth good? Is cost accounting sound? Does the company have something special in its industry? Does it think long-term at the expense of short-term profits? Will growth require dilutive equity issues? Does management talk freely when things go well and clam up when they don't? Is management of unquestionable integrity?

**Scuttlebutt.** Fisher's research method: go around the company. Talk to customers, suppliers, competitors, former employees, and trade associations. Five competitors asked about each other's strengths will tell you more than any annual report. Buffett adopted this wholesale.

**When to sell.** Almost never. Fisher's three reasons: you made a mistake in the original analysis; the company no longer meets the fifteen points; or you found something dramatically better. Selling because the stock "looks high" or the market "looks toppy" was, in his view, the most common and costly error.

**Concentration.** Fisher typically held fewer than ten stocks and thought most diversification was a hedge against ignorance.

**Smith's three rules.** "Buy good companies. Don't overpay. Do nothing." A *good company* for Fundsmith means one that consistently earns a high return on capital, converts profits into cash, has a durable advantage (usually intangible: brands, installed bases, network effects, regulatory moats), grows from reinvestment rather than acquisition, and carries little debt. *Don't overpay* means comparing the free-cash-flow yield to what the business can be expected to compound at. *Do nothing* means turnover in the low single digits, so that compounding and the absence of trading costs and taxes do the work.

**Smith's key metrics.** Return on capital employed, gross margin, operating margin, cash conversion (free cash flow divided by net profit), interest cover, and free-cash-flow yield. He publishes the portfolio's aggregates every year against the index so investors can see what they own.

**The lineage.** Fisher → Buffett/Munger (See's Candies, Coca-Cola) → Nick Sleep (Costco, Amazon) → Smith (Microsoft, Novo Nordisk, L'Oréal, Stryker). Each generation adds tools; the core is unchanged: a few businesses that can reinvest at high returns for a very long time, bought at a fair price, held for as long as that remains true.

## Rules you can steal

- **Run the fifteen points as a checklist.** Any company you plan to hold for a decade should pass most of them; write your answers down.
- **Do scuttlebutt.** Read customer reviews, competitor earnings calls, and Glassdoor; call a supplier if you can. The annual report is the company's version of events.
- **Screen for high, consistent return on capital.** A business that earns 25% on its capital and reinvests most of it will compound at nearly that rate for as long as the returns last.
- **Check cash conversion.** Profits that don't turn into cash are a warning.
- **Don't sell because the price rose.** Sell because the business changed or you were wrong.
- **Accept that quality looks expensive.** Smith's argument: what matters is the return on capital and the runway; a 25x multiple on a business compounding at 20% is cheaper than 10x on one shrinking.
- **Turnover is a cost you control.** Every trade is a tax event and a chance to be wrong twice.
- **Concentrate.** 20–30 holdings for Smith, under 10 for Fisher; enough to diversify away the idiosyncratic disaster, few enough that research depth is possible.
- **Read the letters, not the headlines.** Fundsmith's annual letters explain each year's result against the metrics, including bad years.

## Best things they've written or said

- Philip Fisher, *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (1958; the 2003 edition includes *Conservative Investors Sleep Well* and *Developing an Investment Philosophy*). Chapters 3 (the fifteen points) and 6 (when to sell) are the core.
- [Fundsmith annual letters to shareholders (2010–present)](https://www.fundsmith.co.uk/): the 2024 letter is a good place to start; the 2015 and 2019 letters are famous for their attack on "value" as commonly practised.
- Terry Smith, *Investing for Growth* (2020): collected essays and letters.
- Terry Smith, *Accounting for Growth* (1992): the book that got him fired; still the best plain-English guide to spotting creative accounting.
- [Standing on the Shoulders of a Growth Giant: Phil Fisher — Investing Caffeine](https://investingcaffeine.com/2009/11/17/standing-on-the-shoulders-of-a-growth-giant-phil-fisher/): a compact summary of Fisher's method.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Fisher has no verifiable record.** His reputation rests on a book, a few famous holdings, and Buffett's endorsement.
- **Quality has had a rough half-decade.** Fundsmith has trailed the index since around 2021, partly because it avoided the largest AI winners and partly because high-quality consumer staples de-rated. Smith's response, that the metrics remain superior and the multiple has compressed, is reasonable but unproven.
- **Fees.** Fundsmith charges around 1% a year, high for a low-turnover global equity fund of its size.
- **"Never sell" can become "never admit."** Fisher's rule against selling on price assumes you can recognise when the business has changed; many quality investors held Nokia, Kodak, or Tesco far too long.
- **Quality is now a crowded factor.** The premium for high-ROCE businesses was small in 1958 and large by 2020; Smith's own success helped compress it.

## Key takeaways

- Fisher invented the quality-growth checklist and the scuttlebutt method; Buffett's post-1972 approach is largely Fisher applied at scale.
- Fundsmith compounded at 14.8% a year net from November 2010 to end-2024 versus 12.1% for the MSCI World, with turnover near 3%, though it has lagged since 2021.
- Look for high, consistent return on capital, strong cash conversion, low debt, and a durable intangible advantage.
- Sell only when the business changes, you were wrong, or something clearly better appears; never because the price rose.
- Low turnover is a return enhancer in itself: fewer costs, fewer taxes, fewer decisions to get wrong.
- Quality looks expensive by the usual ratios; judge it by return on capital and the length of the runway instead.
