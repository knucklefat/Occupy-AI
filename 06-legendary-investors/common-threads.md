# Common Threads: What the Greats Agree On, Where They Disagree, and 20 Principles

**In one sentence:** Fifteen investors with wildly different methods, from Buffett's decades-long holds to Simons' hours-long trades to Bogle's "own everything," turn out to agree on almost everything that matters for an individual (costs, temperament, survival, and honesty about your edge) and disagree mostly about the one thing that matters least for most people: how to pick.

## What they agree on

**1. Survival comes first.** Every investor here has a version of "don't lose money." Buffett's cash cushion, Marks' defensive investing, Klarman's risk-first ordering, Druckenmiller's no-losing-year rule, Dalio's "how do I know I'm right?", Munger's post-1974 aversion to forced sales. The arithmetic is universal: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover, and compounding only works if you're still in the game.

**2. Costs and activity are the enemy.** Bogle built a firm on it, but Buffett (Coca-Cola since 1988), Fisher (Motorola for 50 years), Smith (3% turnover), Sleep (three stocks), and Pabrai (don't sell for two years) all practise it. Even Simons, the exception, obsesses over execution costs because his edge is smaller than his costs would be if he were careless.

**3. Temperament beats intelligence.** Graham's Mr. Market, Munger's psychology of misjudgment, Lynch's "more money lost preparing for corrections than in them," Marks' cycle temperature, Spier's environment design, Greenblatt's observation that value works because people can't stand the bad years. Every one of them says the hard part is behavioural.

**4. Price matters, and so does the crowd's position.** Graham's margin of safety, Marks' second-level thinking ("is it better than the price implies?"), Klarman's forced sellers, Greenblatt's structural selling, Buffett's fear-and-greed rule, Druckenmiller's 18-month horizon. Nobody here thinks a good company is automatically a good investment, and nobody thinks the crowd is usually wrong; they think it is wrong at extremes and structurally in certain corners.

**5. Know your edge and its limits.** Buffett's circle of competence, Munger's "too hard" pile, Simons' capacity cap, Dalio's believability weighting, Spier's decision to close when his edge eroded, Bogle's whole career. The honest ones are precise about what they don't know.

**6. Concentrate when you know; diversify when you don't.** Buffett, Munger, Sleep, Pabrai, Druckenmiller, and Fisher run concentrated books. Graham, Lynch, Greenblatt's formula, Dalio, and Bogle diversify. The apparent disagreement resolves once you notice that each concentrates exactly where they have real knowledge and diversifies where they don't. Buffett tells everyone else to index for the same reason.

**7. Think in years, not quarters.** Buffett's three-to-five-year minimum for judging results, Sleep's destination analysis, Smith's do-nothing, Klarman's willingness to look wrong for years, Greenblatt's three-year commitment. Even Druckenmiller looks 18 months out. The only short-term thinker is Simons, and his time frame is hours, not quarters; nobody here lives in the quarterly-earnings zone that dominates financial media.

**8. Write things down.** Letters (Buffett, Sleep, Smith, Pabrai, Marks), checklists (Munger, Pabrai, Fisher's fifteen points), principles (Dalio), rules (Druckenmiller's "what would prove me wrong"). All of them externalise their reasoning so they can check it against what actually happened.

**9. Study failure.** Munger's inversion, Pabrai's checklist built from others' mistakes, Dalio's pain-plus-reflection, Buffett's "cardinal sin" of delaying corrections, Druckenmiller's 2000 lesson. They learn more from what went wrong than from what went right.

**10. Fees should follow results.** The Buffett Partnership, Nomad, Pabrai, and Spier all charged nothing unless they beat a hurdle. Bogle's Vanguard returns profits to fund holders. Medallion charges the most of anyone, and only its employees pay it. The common view: a manager paid regardless of outcome is a manager with the wrong incentives.

## Where they disagree

**Can you beat the market at all?** Bogle says the average investor can't after costs, and the SPIVA data backs him. Everyone else on the list did, but most of them agree that most people shouldn't try. The useful synthesis: indexing is the default, and the burden of proof is on any departure from it.

**Cheap or good?** Graham, Klarman, and Greenblatt's special situations start from price. Fisher, Smith, Sleep, and late Buffett start from business quality. Greenblatt's Magic Formula and Buffett's "wonderful at a fair price" are attempts to hold both; Marks' 2021 memo "Something of Value" argues the distinction is mostly a matter of time horizon.

**Does the macro matter?** Lynch says 13 minutes a year is 10 too many; Buffett ignores forecasts; Sleep and Smith don't mention it. Dalio, Soros, and Druckenmiller built careers on it. Marks sits in between: don't forecast, but know where you are in the cycle. The reconciliation is that bottom-up investors are implicitly betting that owning good businesses at fair prices survives any macro environment, and the macro traders are explicitly betting they can time the environments. Both have worked; the second is far rarer.

**Stories or statistics?** Simons trades only patterns and forbids narratives. Lynch wants a two-minute story. Buffett wants to understand the business. Greenblatt's formula is statistical, his special situations are narrative. The lesson is that both work, but they are different games with different infrastructure, and mixing them (a statistical screen justified by a story, or a story validated by a backtest) is where amateurs get hurt.

**How much cash?** Klarman holds 30–50% for years; Buffett holds a lot at Berkshire but tells individuals to stay invested; Lynch and Bogle say stay fully invested always; Druckenmiller goes to cash after losses. There's no consensus because the answer depends on whether you're an absolute-return manager, a business owner, or a saver, and on whether you have the temperament to redeploy cash in a panic.

**When to sell?** Fisher: almost never. Lynch: when the story changes. Druckenmiller: the moment you're wrong, and fast. Greenblatt's formula: after one year regardless. Sleep: ideally not at all. The disagreement is real, but each rule is consistent with its owner's holding period and edge.

## Twenty principles, combined

1. **Never lose money you can't recover from.** Size every position so that its total loss wouldn't change your life. (Buffett, Klarman, Druckenmiller)
2. **Costs are the only certainty.** Every 1% a year of fees, turnover, and taxes is a large fraction of your ending wealth. (Bogle)
3. **The default is the index.** Any active strategy must beat a low-cost index fund after costs and taxes over many years, or it is a hobby. (Bogle, Buffett)
4. **A stock is a business.** Before you buy, explain in a paragraph how it makes money and why that will continue. (Graham, Buffett, Lynch)
5. **Demand a margin of safety.** Pay meaningfully less than your estimate of value so that being wrong still works out. (Graham, Klarman)
6. **Ask what the price implies.** The question is never "is it good?" but "is it better than everyone already thinks?" (Marks)
7. **Stay inside your circle of competence, and keep a "too hard" pile.** Most things belong in it. (Buffett, Munger)
8. **Invert.** List the ways an investment destroys capital before the ways it wins. (Munger)
9. **Look where selling is structural.** Spin-offs, index deletions, forced liquidations, panics: sellers who don't care about value are your best counterparties. (Greenblatt, Klarman)
10. **Prefer businesses that reinvest at high returns for a long time.** Return on capital and runway matter more than today's multiple. (Fisher, Smith, Sleep, Munger)
11. **Find the loop.** Where scale lowers prices which raises scale (Costco, Amazon), or where rising prices improve fundamentals (Soros), you've found a trend that resists mean reversion. (Sleep, Soros)
12. **Concentrate where you know, diversify where you don't.** Both are correct; know which situation you're in. (Everyone)
13. **Diversify across things that actually differ.** Twenty stocks are one bet; stocks, bonds, gold, and inflation-linked bonds are four. Balance risk, not dollars. (Dalio)
14. **Do less.** Set a maximum number of trades a year. Inactivity is a decision. (Sleep, Smith, Buffett)
15. **Hold winners; sell when the story changes or you were wrong, never because the price rose.** (Lynch, Fisher)
16. **Cut real losses fast and never trade to make them back.** A thesis the market keeps rejecting is a thesis to re-examine. (Druckenmiller, Soros)
17. **Take the market's temperature, don't take its forecast.** Know whether credit is easy or tight and whether investors are greedy or fearful, and adjust how aggressive you are. (Marks, Dalio)
18. **Test rules out of sample and count the costs.** A pattern found in the data you searched proves nothing until it works on data you didn't. (Simons, Greenblatt)
19. **Design your environment.** Remove live prices, don't trade during market hours, don't talk your book, and build a checklist from other people's mistakes. (Spier, Pabrai, Munger)
20. **Write an annual letter to yourself.** Explain each year's decisions and what you'd do differently; the record is the only teacher that doesn't flatter you. (Buffett, Sleep, Dalio, Pabrai)

## Rules you can steal

- Start with an index fund and a written allocation; treat every departure from it as a hypothesis with a burden of proof.
- Before any active purchase, write: the business in one paragraph, the value estimate, the required discount, what the market is missing, and what would prove you wrong.
- Run a mistake-derived checklist at the end of every analysis.
- Cap position size by what you can afford to lose entirely.
- Set a trade budget for the year and a two-year minimum holding period for anything you didn't buy as a special situation.
- Once a quarter, take the market's temperature in writing and adjust aggressiveness, not predictions.
- Once a year, write the letter.

## Key takeaways

- The greats agree on survival, costs, temperament, price discipline, knowing your edge, long horizons, and writing things down; they disagree mainly on *how* to pick.
- The disagreements (cheap vs. good, macro vs. bottom-up, stories vs. statistics, when to sell) each resolve into "it depends on your edge and horizon," which is itself the lesson.
- Concentration and diversification are not opposites; everyone concentrates where they know and diversifies where they don't.
- Indexing is the baseline; every active record here is a claim to have beaten it, and most of the investors say most people shouldn't try.
- The most transferable skills are behavioural and procedural: checklists, letters, environment design, and honest post-mortems.
- The least transferable are structural: float, permanent capital, PhDs, bankruptcy negotiations, and 30 years of no losing years.
