# Peter Lynch

**In one sentence:** Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan Fund for 13 years at 29% a year by working harder than anyone else, owning hundreds of stocks across every category, and insisting that ordinary people who pay attention to what they buy, use, and see at work can spot great companies before Wall Street does.

## Who he is

Peter Lynch (born 1944) joined Fidelity as an analyst in 1969 and took over the small Magellan Fund in May 1977. He ran it until retiring in 1990 at age 46, by which point it was the largest mutual fund in the world. He then wrote three popular books, *One Up on Wall Street* (1989), *Beating the Street* (1993), and *Learn to Earn* (1995), that made him the most quoted stock-picker for retail investors. He has remained at Fidelity as a vice chairman and mentor and is a major philanthropist.

## Track record (verified)

- **Fidelity Magellan Fund, 1977–1990:** average annual return of **29.2%**, roughly double the S&P 500 over the same period. He beat the index in **11 of 13 years**. Assets grew from about **$18–20 million to over $14 billion**. Sources: [The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/08/22/peter-lynch-beat-the-sp-500-in-11-of-his-13-years/) and Fidelity's own materials; the 29.2% figure appears consistently across Fidelity, Yahoo Finance, and the books.
- Caveats: Magellan was closed to new investors for several early years, so the record's first stretch was earned on a small, nimble base. Also widely repeated (including by Fidelity) is the claim that the *average investor* in Magellan made far less than the fund because they bought high and sold low; the specific statistic is not verifiable from a primary source, so treat the direction as true and the number as folklore.

## Core philosophy

**Invest in what you know, then do the work.** Lynch's most famous line is also his most misunderstood. Noticing that a store is always full or that your kids love a product is a *lead*, not a thesis. The next step is reading the annual report, checking the balance sheet, understanding what drives earnings, and confirming the price is reasonable. His actual claim was that amateurs have an edge in *sourcing* ideas, not that they can skip the analysis.

**Turn over the most rocks.** Lynch visited hundreds of companies a year and held as many as 1,400 stocks. He believed the person who looks at the most ideas finds the most winners, and that a few "tenbaggers" (stocks that rise tenfold) pay for a great many mediocre picks.

**Know what kind of stock you own.** He sorted companies into six types, each with its own playbook:
- *Slow growers* (utilities): own for dividends, don't expect much.
- *Stalwarts* (large, steady growers): buy on dips, sell after a 30–50% gain.
- *Fast growers* (20%+ earnings growth): the tenbagger hunting ground; watch for the growth to slow.
- *Cyclicals* (autos, airlines, steel): timing matters; buy when things look worst.
- *Turnarounds*: high risk, high reward, uncorrelated with the market.
- *Asset plays*: hidden value on the balance sheet that the market ignores.

**Growth at a reasonable price.** Lynch popularised the PEG ratio: the price-to-earnings multiple divided by the earnings growth rate. A PEG around 1 or below suggested you weren't overpaying for growth; well above 2 was a warning.

**The story.** Before buying, Lynch wanted to state the case for a stock in two minutes, in plain language a child could follow. If the story needed jargon, it was probably wrong.

**Ignore the macro.** He said that if you spend 13 minutes a year on economics, you've wasted 10 of them. He didn't try to predict recessions or rates; he tried to find companies that would be bigger in five years.

## Rules you can steal

- **Write the two-minute story.** What does the company do, why will earnings grow, what could go wrong, and why is the price reasonable?
- **Categorise before you buy.** A cyclical, a fast grower, and a stalwart require different holding periods and sell rules.
- **Use PEG as a sanity check.** Growth is worth paying for, but not at any price.
- **Prefer boring.** Dull names, dull industries, no analyst coverage, no institutional ownership: that's where mispricing lives.
- **Check the balance sheet.** Lynch avoided companies with heavy debt; a good story with a bad balance sheet is a turnaround, not a growth stock.
- **Don't sell just because a stock went up.** Selling winners and keeping losers is "pulling the flowers and watering the weeds."
- **Sell when the story changes.** Not when the price moves, not when the market wobbles.
- **Stay invested.** Far more money has been lost preparing for corrections than in corrections themselves.
- **Own enough to matter.** A few positions that could be tenbaggers, sized so a winner changes your result.

## Best things he's written or said

- *One Up on Wall Street* (1989): the best single introduction to stock-picking for non-professionals; the six categories, the PEG ratio, and the "story" method are all here. [Full summary in the library](../07-publications-and-resources/book-summaries/one-up-on-wall-street.md).
- *Beating the Street* (1993): more mechanics, including his 21 "Peter's Principles" and detailed case studies.
- [Fidelity legend Peter Lynch: "I never said to invest in the stock market" — Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/fidelity-legend-peter-lynch-i-never-said-to-invest-in-the-stock-market-120755736.html): Lynch correcting the "buy what you know" misreading.
- His 1994 National Press Club speech (freely available on video) is the most concise statement of his approach.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **Unreplicable effort.** Lynch worked seven days a week, met hundreds of managements, and had Fidelity's analyst army behind him. "Buy what you know" understates how much work he actually did.
- **Era effects.** 1977–1990 was a strong bull market for small and mid-cap growth; his edge in an age of no internet and thin institutional coverage of small caps may not exist today.
- **Enormous diversification.** With over 1,000 holdings, his fund's outperformance came from a relatively small number of huge winners; many of his picks were forgettable.
- **He quit while ahead.** A 13-year record is long but not decades long; there is no evidence of how the method would have fared through the 2000 or 2008 crashes with a $14 billion fund.
- **Survivorship in the anecdotes.** The Dunkin' Donuts and Hanes stories are memorable; the picks that didn't work don't make the books.

## Key takeaways

- 29.2% a year for 13 years, beating the market in 11 of them, is one of the best mutual-fund records ever.
- Personal observation is a source of ideas, never a substitute for analysis.
- Classify every stock (slow grower, stalwart, fast grower, cyclical, turnaround, asset play) and apply that type's sell rules.
- Growth at a reasonable price: use the PEG ratio to avoid overpaying.
- Hold winners; sell only when the story changes; ignore macro forecasts.
- His workload, era, and team are not replicable; his framework is.
