# Charlie Munger

**In one sentence:** Munger, Buffett's partner for nearly 60 years, contributed the idea that a great business at a fair price beats a fair business at a great price, and built a "latticework of mental models" from psychology, math, and biology that lets an investor avoid stupidity rather than chase brilliance.

## Who he is

Charles T. Munger (January 1, 1924 – November 28, 2023) was a lawyer by training (Harvard Law), a real-estate developer by accident, and an investor by choice. He met Warren Buffett in 1959 in Omaha, ran his own investment partnership from 1962 to 1975, became vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway in 1978, and served in that role until his death at 99. He also chaired Wesco Financial and the Daily Journal Corporation and sat on Costco's board for decades. Buffett credits Munger with the "blueprint" that moved Berkshire from buying cheap junk to buying great businesses.

## Track record (verified)

- **Wheeler, Munger & Co., 1962–1975:** compounded at about **19.8% per year before fees** versus roughly **5%** for the Dow over the same stretch. The record includes a brutal drawdown in 1973–74 (down roughly 31% and 31% in consecutive years), after which he closed the partnership. Source: [Nasdaq/AP obituary](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/charlie-munger-half-of-a-legendary-partnership-dies-at-99); the figures originally appear in Buffett's 1984 "Superinvestors" essay. They are self-reported partnership numbers, not audited public fund data.
- **Berkshire Hathaway, 1978–2023 (as vice chairman):** shares in Berkshire's 19.9% annual record (see [warren-buffett.md](warren-buffett.md)). His specific contributions, See's Candies (1972) and the shift to quality, are documented in Buffett's letters.
- **Daily Journal Corporation:** in 2009 he put the company's cash into bank stocks near the bottom of the crisis, a small but well-documented example of his "bet big when the odds are overwhelming" rule.

## Core philosophy

**1. Multidisciplinary thinking (the latticework).** Munger argued that you cannot understand the world, or a business, with the tools of one discipline. He advocated learning the "big ideas" from every major field (compound interest and probability from math, incentives from economics, evolution and ecosystems from biology, feedback loops from engineering, cognitive biases from psychology) and hanging every new fact on that framework. Mental models are simply well-tested ways of thinking, and the point of having many is that "to a man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail."

**2. Inversion.** Borrowed from the mathematician Jacobi: "invert, always invert." Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how you would guarantee failure, then avoid those things. In investing that means listing the ways an investment can permanently lose money (too much debt, a fading product, a dishonest manager, a price that assumes perfection) before you list the ways it can win.

**3. The psychology of human misjudgment.** In a 1995 Harvard talk, later expanded in *Poor Charlie's Almanack*, he catalogued about 25 tendencies that lead smart people astray: incentive-caused bias, social proof, commitment and consistency, deprival super-reaction (loss aversion), authority bias, and the "lollapalooza" effect when several combine. His claim was that most investing errors are psychological, not analytical.

**4. Sit on your hands.** Munger believed the money is made in waiting, not trading. Opportunities that are both understandable and obviously mispriced are rare; when one appears, bet heavily, and otherwise do nothing. He kept a "too hard" pile and was proud of how much went into it.

**5. Quality over cheapness.** His decisive influence on Buffett: a business that can reinvest at high returns for a long time is worth paying a fair price for, because the compounding does the work.

## Rules you can steal

- **Build a checklist from the failures of others.** Write down how investments go wrong and run every idea against the list.
- **Invert.** For each holding, write the three most likely ways it destroys capital. If you can't, you don't understand it.
- **Three baskets: yes, no, too hard.** Most things belong in the third. There is no penalty for passing.
- **Know your incentives and everyone else's.** Ask who is paid to tell you this, and how.
- **Avoid the lollapalooza.** Be most suspicious when several biases push the same way: a hot sector, a charismatic CEO, friends getting rich, and a story that flatters your self-image.
- **Concentrate on your best ideas.** Munger held a handful of positions and thought wide diversification was "for people who don't know anything."
- **Read constantly.** He described himself as "a book with a couple of legs sticking out."
- **Never use debt that can force a sale.** His 1973–74 drawdown taught him that survival is the precondition for compounding.
- **Be a learning machine.** Go to bed a little wiser than you woke up; the compounding of knowledge mirrors the compounding of money.

## Best things he's written or said

- *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (2005; expanded editions since). Collected speeches, including "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom" (1994 USC talk) and "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment."
- [Wise words from Charlie Munger — Novel Investor](https://novelinvestor.com/wise-words-from-charlie-munger/): a curated quote collection with sources.
- Daily Journal annual meeting Q&As (2014–2023), widely transcribed online, are the most candid late-career Munger.
- Berkshire annual meeting remarks, especially his one-line verdicts ("I have nothing to add").

## Criticisms and limitations

- **The record is thin and old.** His only standalone track record is 13 years of a small partnership half a century ago, with two years of roughly 30% losses. Most of his reputation rides on Berkshire and on his ideas.
- **Concentration cuts both ways.** Daily Journal's late-career bets on Alibaba and Wells Fargo went poorly; concentration at 97 looks the same as concentration at 47 when it fails.
- **Survivorship in the advice.** "Bet big when the odds are overwhelming" is only wisdom if you can tell overwhelming odds from overconfidence. Munger admitted that skill is rare.
- **Some models are folksy, not rigorous.** The latticework is a useful metaphor, but he never formalised it, and imitators sometimes collect model names without learning the underlying disciplines.

## Key takeaways

- Munger's partnership compounded at nearly 20% a year for 13 years, but the Berkshire "quality" pivot is his real legacy.
- Avoiding stupidity is more reliable than seeking brilliance; invert the question.
- Most investing mistakes are psychological; know the 25 tendencies and assume they apply to you.
- Patience is a position: doing nothing for years, then betting heavily, is a legitimate strategy.
- Learn the big ideas from many fields; a single lens distorts.
- Survival first: no leverage that can force you out of a good position at a bad time.
