# Mohnish Pabrai and Guy Spier

**In one sentence:** Pabrai and Spier are the most candid practitioners of "cloning," the deliberate copying of great investors' ideas, portfolios, and habits, and between them they've turned Buffett's principles into a set of practical tools (checklists, environment design, "heads I win, tails I don't lose much" bets) that an individual can actually use.

## Who they are

**Mohnish Pabrai** (born 1964) sold his IT consulting firm in 2000 and launched Pabrai Investment Funds in 1999 with about $1 million from friends, explicitly modelled on the Buffett Partnership: no management fee, a performance fee only above a 6% hurdle, concentrated positions, and annual letters. He wrote *The Dhandho Investor* (2007), popularising the idea of low-risk, high-uncertainty bets, and later added the Pabrai Wagons Fund, a mutual fund, in 2023. He founded the Dakshana Foundation, which coaches poor Indian students for engineering-school entrance exams, and describes it as his real career.

**Guy Spier** (born 1966) went from Oxford and Harvard Business School to a brief, regretted stint at a boiler-room investment bank, then launched the **Aquamarine Fund** in 1997, also modelled on the Buffett Partnership. His 2014 memoir *The Education of a Value Investor* is about unlearning Wall Street habits and redesigning his life, moving from New York to Zurich, to make good decisions easier. In 2008 he and Pabrai paid $650,100 at charity auction for lunch with Buffett, an encounter both credit with changing how they operate. In March 2026 Spier announced he was winding down Aquamarine after 28 years, citing health and the erosion of his research edge.

## Track record (verified, with caveats)

**Pabrai:**
- **Pabrai Funds, 2000–2013:** **517% net to investors versus 43% for the S&P 500** since inception, per a 2013 [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/phildemuth/2013/09/23/how-mohnish-pabrai-crushed-the-market-by-1100-since-2000/) profile drawing on the fund's letters. That's roughly 14% a year net over a period that included the 2008 crash, in which the fund fell about 60% (widely reported; from his own letters).
- **Since 2013:** results have been mixed and are not consistently public; the funds are private and Pabrai has openly discussed several large losses (Horsehead Holdings in 2016, for example). **Treat the long-run record as unverified after 2013.**

**Spier:**
- **Aquamarine Fund, 1997–2026:** lifetime return of about **1,186%** (roughly 9% a year), with **eight consecutive years of underperformance** against the S&P 500 before its closure and about **$470 million** under management at the end. Source: [Yahoo Finance / Investing.com (March 2026)](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/hedge-fund-veteran-guy-spier-100016627.html). The S&P 500's total return over the same 28 years was similar or somewhat higher; Aquamarine roughly matched the index over its life and beat it comfortably in its first half.

Neither record is spectacular by the standards of this section, which is itself instructive: both men are honest that their value comes from *method*, not from being the next Buffett.

## Core philosophy

**Cloning.** Pabrai's most provocative claim is that originality is overrated. His fund's best ideas were, by his own account, mostly borrowed from the 13-F filings (quarterly disclosures of holdings) of investors he admires. He argues that few people clone well because ego gets in the way, which makes cloning an underexploited edge. Spier calls the same idea "not trying to be original" and extends it to habits: copy Buffett's reading, Munger's checklists, and Pabrai's letter format wholesale.

**Dhandho: heads I win, tails I don't lose much.** Pabrai's term (from the Gujarati word for business) for bets with limited downside and large upside. He looks for situations where the market has priced in a catastrophe that is possible but unlikely: a company trading below liquidation value, a temporarily crushed cyclical, a spin-off nobody wants. Low risk and high uncertainty are not the same thing; the market confuses them, and that is the opportunity.

**Checklists.** After the 2008 crash exposed errors in his process, Pabrai read Atul Gawande's *The Checklist Manifesto* and built an investing checklist by going through the public mistakes of investors he admired (including Buffett's) and asking what question would have caught each one. It grew to about 100 items in categories like leverage, moat, management and ownership, and miscellaneous (union issues, regulatory risk). Spier adopted it. The checklist runs at the *end* of analysis, as a final catch, not as a substitute for thinking.

**Environment design (Spier).** Spier's central insight is that willpower is unreliable, so build a life where the right decision is the default: no Bloomberg terminal, no market-hours news, no talking to sell-side brokers, a quiet office far from Wall Street, a rule to never trade during market hours, and a rule to never discuss a position with anyone while he still holds it. He describes these as guardrails against his own psychology.

**Few bets, big bets, infrequent bets.** Pabrai runs 10 or so positions at 10% each, buys only when the discount to intrinsic value is large, and expects to make a few decisions a year.

**Gratitude and giving.** Both have written about compounding goodwill (thank-you notes, generosity, charity) as a life practice; Pabrai attributes it to Buffett, Spier to Pabrai. It sounds soft; both claim it has produced their best deal flow.

## Rules you can steal

- **Clone shamelessly.** Read the 13-F filings of five investors you respect (they're free on the SEC's EDGAR site) and treat their new positions as a research list.
- **Build a checklist from other people's mistakes.** Every time a well-known investor loses badly, write down the question that would have caught it. Run the list before every purchase.
- **Ask "heads I win, tails I don't lose much?"** If the downside isn't capped by assets, cash, or a cheap price, it isn't a Dhandho bet.
- **Separate risk from uncertainty.** Uncertain outcomes with a capped downside are where the market overpays you for discomfort.
- **Design your environment.** Remove the news feed, don't trade during market hours, and don't tell anyone what you own until you've sold.
- **Don't sell within two or three years of buying** (Pabrai's rule, borrowed from Buffett's partnership), unless you've clearly made a mistake, so that a thesis has time to play out and you're not trading on price noise.
- **Charge yourself only for results.** If you manage family money, judge yourself against an index fund after costs and taxes; if you can't beat it, buy it.
- **Write an annual letter to yourself.** Both men credit the discipline of explaining each year's decisions in writing.

## Best things they've written or said

- Mohnish Pabrai, *The Dhandho Investor* (2007): short, clear, and full of case studies (Patels running motels, Papa Patel, Stewart Enterprises, Frontline).
- Guy Spier, *The Education of a Value Investor* (2014): the best book on the psychology and life design of investing; the chapter on his "Zurich rules" is essential.
- Pabrai's lectures (Google, Boston College, Peking University) and his [YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@MohnishPabrai) are freely available and unusually specific.
- [Guy Spier's site and annual reports](https://www.guyspier.com/) including the [Aquamarine 2015 annual report (PDF)](https://www.guyspier.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/AF_AR_Annual-Report_2015_OH.pdf).

## Criticisms and limitations

- **The records are ordinary.** Pabrai's post-2013 results are opaque and include some large, self-admitted losses; Spier's fund merely matched the index over 28 years and closed after eight straight years of underperformance. Their value is as teachers, not as track records.
- **Cloning has a timing problem.** By the time a 13-F is public, the position is 45 days old and often already re-rated. Cloning works for slow, long-duration holdings and poorly for anything else.
- **Checklists don't fix judgment.** A 100-item list can become theatre; both men admit the checklist did not stop later mistakes.
- **Spier's closure argument cuts against the method.** He said his research edge was being commoditised by AI and that "everybody is looking everywhere." If true, the Dhandho hunting grounds are shrinking.
- **Concentration risk with a real cost.** Pabrai's roughly 60% 2008 drawdown is the price of 10-stock portfolios.

## Key takeaways

- Pabrai returned 517% net versus 43% for the S&P 500 over 2000–2013; Spier's Aquamarine returned about 1,186% over 1997–2026, roughly matching the index over its life and closing in 2026.
- Cloning great investors' ideas and habits is a legitimate, underused edge; ego is the main obstacle.
- Dhandho: hunt for capped-downside, open-upside bets where the market confuses uncertainty with risk.
- Build a checklist from other investors' documented mistakes and run it before every purchase.
- Design your environment so the good decision is the default: no live prices, no market-hours trading, no talking your book.
- Both men are proof that the method can be learned, and that learning it does not guarantee a Buffett-sized result.
