# Howard Marks

**In one sentence:** Marks co-founded Oaktree, the world's largest distressed-debt investor, and has spent 35 years writing memos arguing that superior results come not from predicting the future but from understanding where you are in the cycle, thinking one level deeper than the crowd, and controlling risk so you survive to collect when prices revert.

## Who he is

Howard Marks (born 1946) began his career at Citicorp in 1969, moved to TCW in 1985 where he built high-yield and distressed-debt businesses, and in 1995 co-founded Oaktree Capital Management with Bruce Karsh and others. Oaktree specialises in credit: high-yield bonds, distressed debt (the bonds and loans of companies in or near bankruptcy), real estate, and private lending. It manages roughly $200 billion and has been majority-owned by Brookfield since 2019. Marks is best known for his client memos, which he has written since 1990 and which Warren Buffett has said he reads the moment they arrive.

## Track record (verified, with caveats)

- **Memo record:** Marks' calls are unusually documented because they're timestamped. "bubble.com" (January 2, 2000) warned about technology stocks two months before the peak. "Nobody Knows" (September 19, 2008) argued for buying into the panic; Oaktree deployed billions into distressed debt in late 2008 and the resulting funds were among its best ever. Source: the [Oaktree memo archive](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos).
- **Oaktree distressed funds:** the firm's distressed-debt funds have historically reported high-teens to low-20s percent net internal rates of return over the 1988–2010s period, and its 2008-vintage fund is widely regarded as one of the best-timed credit funds ever raised. These figures come from Oaktree's public filings and press coverage rather than a single consolidated public statistic, and Oaktree runs many funds with different results, so **no single "Howard Marks annual return" exists and any such number you see online should be treated with suspicion.**
- **Oaktree as a business:** grew from a start-up in 1995 to roughly $200 billion under management, and was sold to Brookfield at a valuation of about $4.7 billion for the 62% stake in 2019.

## Core philosophy

**Second-level thinking.** First-level thinking says "good company, buy the stock." Second-level thinking says "good company, but everyone knows it, so the price already reflects it, so sell." The question is never whether something is good but whether it is *better than the price implies*. Investing is a game against other people's expectations.

**You can't predict, but you can prepare.** Marks does not forecast interest rates, recessions, or markets. Instead he takes the market's temperature: are investors euphoric or fearful? Is capital easy or tight? Are deals being done on lenders' terms or borrowers' terms? Knowing you're in the late innings of a cycle doesn't tell you when it ends, but it tells you to be cautious.

**Risk is not volatility.** Risk is the probability of permanent loss, and it is highest precisely when it feels lowest, because rising prices and low perceived risk cause people to pay more and demand less protection. Marks' formulation: risk is created by high prices, and the biggest risk is the belief that there is no risk.

**Cycles.** Everything oscillates around a trend. Credit cycles especially: easy money leads to bad loans, bad loans lead to losses, losses lead to tight money, tight money starves good borrowers, distressed prices emerge, and the cycle restarts. Oaktree's business is to have dry powder when the cycle turns.

**Defensive investing.** Marks' goal is not to have the best years but to avoid the worst ones. Oaktree's stated aim is to be in the top quartile in bad years and merely respectable in good ones, which compounds to top-decile results over time. In his tennis analogy (borrowed from Charles Ellis), amateurs win by not making errors, not by hitting winners.

**Luck and the range of outcomes.** Any investment result is one draw from a distribution of things that could have happened. Judge decisions by process, not by a single outcome. Be humble about what you don't know.

## Rules you can steal

- **Ask "and who doesn't know that?"** Every thesis should identify what the market is missing, not just what's true.
- **Take the temperature, don't take a forecast.** Once a quarter, write down whether credit is easy or tight, investors greedy or fearful, and adjust aggressiveness accordingly.
- **Aim for asymmetry.** Investments with limited downside and open upside; avoid the reverse.
- **Play defence.** Losing less in downturns matters more than winning in booms, because losses compound against you.
- **Price is what matters.** Nothing is a good investment at any price, and nearly anything is at some price.
- **Be a contrarian carefully.** The crowd is right most of the time; the money is made at extremes, which are rare. Marks' line: being too early is indistinguishable from being wrong.
- **Buy when others are forced to sell.** The best bargains appear when holders must liquidate regardless of value.
- **Judge yourself by process.** A good outcome from a bad decision is luck; record which one you had.
- **Read the memos with a highlighter.** They are free and cover 35 years of cycles.

## Best things he's written or said

- [Oaktree memos archive (1990–present)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos): start with "The Most Important Thing" (2003), "Risk Revisited" (2014), "You Bet!" (2020), "Something of Value" (2021, on value vs. growth), and "Sea Change" (2022, on the end of the low-rate era).
- *The Most Important Thing: Uncommon Sense for the Thoughtful Investor* (2011; illuminated edition 2013 with commentary). Twenty short chapters, each "the most important thing."
- *Mastering the Market Cycle* (2018): the fullest treatment of where-we-are thinking.
- "bubble.com" (January 2000) and "Nobody Knows" (September 2008): read them knowing what came next.

## Criticisms and limitations

- **No clean public return.** Unlike Buffett or Lynch, there is no single audited number to hold him to; his reputation rests on memos, business success, and the reported performance of specific credit funds.
- **His edge is institutional.** Distressed debt requires legal expertise, scale, and the ability to control bankruptcies. A retail investor can borrow the mindset but not the strategy.
- **Cautious by temperament.** Marks has been early or wrong-footed in bull markets, notably remaining wary through much of the 2010s rally. Defensive investing has a cost in long booms, which he acknowledges.
- **The memos repeat.** After 35 years the themes are familiar; readers sometimes mistake familiarity for depth. Marks himself warns that "everyone knows" his ideas now, which by his own logic reduces their edge.

## Key takeaways

- Marks' timestamped memos in January 2000 and September 2008 are among the best-documented cycle calls in modern investing.
- Second-level thinking: the question is not "is it good?" but "is it better than the price implies?"
- Risk is permanent loss, not volatility, and it is highest when it feels lowest.
- You cannot predict the cycle's turn, but you can know roughly where you stand and adjust aggressiveness.
- Defence wins: avoid the big losses and the compounding takes care of the rest.
- Judge decisions by process and by the range of outcomes that could have occurred, not by the one that did.
