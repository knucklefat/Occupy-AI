# Seasonality and Calendar Effects

**In one sentence:** Calendar effects ("Sell in May," the January effect, the presidential cycle, the Santa Claus rally, turn-of-the-month strength) are real patterns in the historical averages, but most are small, unstable, partly explained by a few outlier years, and too weak to overcome taxes and transaction costs, so they belong in the "interesting context" bin rather than the "trading rule" bin.

## Why calendar patterns exist at all (and why they fade)

Markets are supposed to be hard to predict; if stocks reliably rose in December, people would buy in November, and the pattern would vanish. Yet several seasonal regularities have persisted for decades. The usual explanations:

- **Institutional cash flows.** Year-end bonuses, pension contributions, 401(k) deposits, and mutual-fund distributions cluster at certain dates, especially the turn of the month and the turn of the year.
- **Tax behavior.** Investors sell losers in December for tax purposes and buy back in January, historically lifting small, beaten-down stocks.
- **Behavior and attention.** Summer vacations reduce trading volume and risk appetite; September brings fund fiscal year-ends and post-summer repositioning.
- **Policy cycles.** Administrations tend to stimulate the economy ahead of elections.

The pattern-fading argument is only partly right. Effects that require capital and carry risk (like holding small caps in January) have mostly been arbitraged away. Effects that are structural (cash flows at month-end) or too small to arbitrage profitably after costs have persisted. And some "effects" were never more than noise that survived because they made good stories.

## The effects, and what the evidence says

| Effect | The claim | The historical numbers (S&P 500, roughly 1950–2025) | What rigorous studies find | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **"Sell in May and go away" / Halloween indicator** | Stocks do better from November through April than from May through October | Nov–Apr averaged about +6.8%; May–Oct about +1.7%. The winter half accounts for nearly all of the long-run gain. | Bouman and Jacobsen (American Economic Review, 2002) found the effect in 36 of 37 countries; Jacobsen and Zhang (2020) extended it to 300+ years of UK data and 100+ markets, still present out of sample. Critics (Maberly and Pierce, 2004) showed the US result weakens sharply if you remove October 1987 and August 1998. | The *most robust* calendar effect statistically. But May–Oct returns are still *positive* on average, so "sell" is the wrong word; the summer half simply has a lower return with similar risk. After capital-gains taxes and the cost of being wrong in years like 2020 (May–Oct +12%), a buy-and-hold investor loses nothing by ignoring it. |
| **January effect** | Small-cap stocks, especially recent losers, outperform in early January | Small caps beat large caps in January in most years from 1926 to the 1980s, with most of the excess return in the first five trading days | Rozeff and Kinney (1976) first documented it; Keim (1983) tied it to small caps. Studies since the 1990s (e.g., Haug and Hirschey, 2006, in the *Financial Analysts Journal*) find it survived among the very smallest stocks but has largely disappeared for anything investable. | Mostly gone. Once widely known, it was arbitraged away in the 1990s; what remains is concentrated in illiquid micro-caps where trading costs eat it. |
| **"As goes January, so goes the year" (January barometer)** | A positive January predicts a positive year | Since 1950, the barometer has been "right" about 70–75% of the time | The base rate for any year being positive is about 73%, so the barometer adds almost nothing. The January return is itself part of the annual return, inflating the hit rate. | Statistical artifact. Ignore. |
| **Presidential election cycle** | Year 3 (pre-election) is strongest; year 2 (midterm) is weakest | Since 1950: pre-election years averaged ~+16%; midterm years ~+4.5% with average intra-year drawdowns near 17%; election years ~+7%; post-election years ~+6–7%. The S&P was higher 12 months after every midterm election low since 1950. | The year-3 effect is striking (no negative pre-election year from 1939 through 2023) but rests on only ~19 observations, and the theory (fiscal stimulus timed to elections) fits some cycles and not others. Midterm-year weakness is consistent with policy uncertainty being resolved in November. | Suggestive but statistically thin. Useful as context (2026 is a midterm year, so a bumpy year with a Q4 recovery would be "on script"), not as a trading rule. |
| **Santa Claus rally** | The last five trading days of December plus the first two of January are positive | Average gain ~1.3%, positive ~78% of the time since 1950 (Yale Hirsch, Stock Trader's Almanac). The rally "failed" in 2024, 2025, and 2026, the first three-year streak on record. | Any random 7-day window has ~57% odds of being positive; 78% is meaningfully higher, and the effect ties to year-end bonuses, light volume, and window-dressing. Hirsch's corollary that a failed rally predicts a weak year has a mixed record: full-year returns average ~6% after failures vs ~10% after successes, but 2024 and 2025 both delivered double-digit gains after failed rallies. | Real but tiny (1.3% is not tradeable after costs) and its "predictive" corollary is weak. |
| **Turn-of-the-month effect** | Returns cluster in the last trading day of the month and the first three days of the next | Lakonishok and Smidt (1988) found nearly all of the market's gains in that four-day window; McConnell and Xu (2008, *Financial Analysts Journal*) confirmed it in US data 1926–2005 and in 30 of 34 other countries, with returns on the *other* days averaging roughly zero. | The most credibly *structural* effect: it maps onto payroll and pension cash flows. Still present in recent data, though smaller and shifted earlier as more investors know about it. | Real and persistent, but capturing it means trading 24 times a year; costs and taxes make it viable only for tax-advantaged accounts with zero commissions, and the edge is a fraction of a percent per month. |
| **September effect** | September is the worst month | Average −0.7% and positive only ~44% of the time since 1950; the only month with both a negative average and a sub-50% hit rate | Persistent across decades and countries, but the average is dragged down by 1974, 2001, 2002, 2008, and 2022. Exclude those and September is unremarkable. | A few bad years, not a curse. Not tradeable. |
| **Day-of-week (Monday) effect** | Mondays are weak | Mondays were negative on average through the 1980s | French (1980) documented it; it reversed or vanished after 1990. | Gone. |
| **Options expiration / quarter-end / index rebalancing** | Predictable flow-driven volatility around the third Friday and quarter-ends | Volume spikes are real; directional edge is not | Structural but not directional. | Awareness helps you avoid trading into noise; nothing more. |

## Why most of these are weaker than they look

1. **Small samples.** Seventy-five years of data is only 75 Januaries, 19 presidential cycles, and one COVID crash. A single outlier year (October 1987 was −22%) moves the averages.
2. **Data mining.** With twelve months, five weekdays, and dozens of holidays, you will find "significant" patterns by chance. Most academic calendar anomalies were discovered by searching, then justified after the fact.
3. **Overlapping explanations.** "Sell in May" and the September effect and the turn-of-the-month effect are partly the same few weeks counted three ways.
4. **Costs.** A 1–2% average edge, before taxes on realized gains and before the risk of sitting out a 10% summer rally, is not an edge for a taxable investor.
5. **Adaptation.** The January effect shows what happens when a pattern becomes well known: it moves earlier (to late December), shrinks, and finally disappears.

## What still has some use

- **Expectation-setting.** Knowing midterm years are choppy and that September is often rough can keep you from panicking over normal seasonal weakness.
- **Timing of planned actions.** If you intend to rebalance or deploy new cash anyway, doing it in early October rather than early September, or at month-end rather than mid-month, costs nothing and lines up with the flow-driven patterns.
- **Tax-loss harvesting.** The tax mechanics behind the old January effect still apply to *you*: harvest losers in December, but be aware that beaten-down small caps often bounce in early January.
- **Combining with real indicators.** Seasonal weakness plus a breadth divergence plus stretched sentiment is a stronger reason for caution than any one alone. Seasonality should be the tie-breaker, never the driver.

## How to use it

1. **Don't build a strategy around the calendar.** No seasonal pattern has a large enough, stable enough edge to justify trading in and out of a diversified portfolio, especially in a taxable account.
2. **Use "Sell in May" as a risk lens, not a sell signal.** If you are overexposed and were planning to trim anyway, spring is historically a slightly better time than autumn. That is the full extent of the actionable content.
3. **Schedule around flows.** Deploy new money and rebalance at month-end/month-start; avoid making big decisions during options-expiration weeks and quarter-ends when flows dominate prices.
4. **Treat the presidential cycle as a narrative check.** In a midterm year (like 2026), expect volatility and don't over-interpret a summer selloff; the post-midterm rally has a strong, if small-sample, record.
5. **Ignore the January barometer and the Santa Claus corollary.** Their "predictive" power is a base-rate illusion.

## Key takeaways

- Calendar effects are real in the averages but small, unstable, and heavily influenced by a few outlier years.
- "Sell in May" is the most statistically robust (documented in 36 of 37 countries and over 300 years of UK data), but summer returns are still positive, so the right response is "don't panic in summer," not "sell."
- The January effect for investable small caps is essentially gone; it lives on only in micro-caps.
- The turn-of-the-month effect is structural (cash flows) and persistent, but worth only a fraction of a percent per month before costs.
- The presidential cycle and Santa Claus rally rest on tiny samples; midterm years are choppy, pre-election years strong, but 19 observations is not a law.
- September's bad reputation comes from five catastrophic years; exclude them and it is ordinary.
- Use seasonality to time actions you were already going to take and to calibrate expectations, never as the reason for a trade.
