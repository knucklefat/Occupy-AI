# Order of Operations for Investing Dollars

**In one sentence:** Every dollar you save should go to the account that gives the highest guaranteed, after-tax return first — employer match, then debt that costs more than the market returns, then the accounts with the best tax breaks — and only then to a plain taxable brokerage.

*Limits below are for tax year 2026, per IRS Notice 2025-67 and Rev. Proc. 2025-19. Last checked September 2026.*

## Why order matters more than fund choice

Two people each invest $15,000 a year in the same index fund. One dumps it all into a taxable brokerage; the other routes it through a 401(k) match, an HSA, and a Roth IRA. Over 30 years the second person ends up with meaningfully more money — not because of better picks, but because the employer added free money, the government never taxed the growth, and nothing leaked out in annual dividend taxes. The "waterfall" below is the community-standard answer to *where does the next dollar go*, popularized by the [Bogleheads wiki](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Prioritizing_investments) and the [r/personalfinance flowchart](https://www.reddit.com/r/personalfinance/wiki/commontopics/). Both say essentially the same thing, so you can trust it as a default.

## The waterfall

### Step 1 — Starter emergency fund

Park 1–3 months of essential expenses (rent, food, insurance, minimum debt payments) in a high-yield savings account or a money-market fund. This isn't an investment; it's the thing that stops you from selling investments at the worst time or swiping a credit card at 25% when the car dies. Grow it to 3–6 months once the higher-return steps are covered.

### Step 2 — 401(k) / 403(b) / TSP up to the full employer match

If your employer matches 50% of the first 6% of pay, contributing 6% earns an instant 50% return before the market does anything. Nothing else in finance is this good. **Worked example:** on a $80,000 salary, a 6% contribution ($4,800) attracts a $2,400 match. Skip it and you've turned down $2,400 of salary. Check the vesting schedule (how long you must stay before the match is fully yours), but even partially vested money is worth capturing.

### Step 3 — High-interest debt (roughly 7% and up)

Paying off a 24% credit card is a *guaranteed*, tax-free 24% return. The stock market's long-run average is about 7% after inflation with no guarantee at all. Anything above roughly 7–8% should be killed before you invest beyond the match. Debt between about 4% and 7% (many car loans, some student loans) is a judgment call — see step 8.

### Step 4 — Health Savings Account (HSA) to the max

If you're on an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan, the HSA is the single best account in the tax code: contributions are deductible (and skip FICA payroll tax if made through payroll), growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for medical costs are tax-free. **2026 limits: $4,400 self-only, $8,750 family, plus $1,000 catch-up at age 55+.** The trick is to invest it and *not* spend it — see [hsa-as-a-stealth-retirement-account.md](hsa-as-a-stealth-retirement-account.md).

### Step 5 — IRA to the max

**2026 limit: $7,500, plus $1,100 catch-up at age 50+.** Why the IRA before maxing the 401(k)? Because you choose the provider and therefore the funds and fees, whereas a 401(k) menu can be mediocre and expensive. Roth vs traditional depends on your marginal tax rate now versus in retirement — see [roth-vs-traditional-decision.md](roth-vs-traditional-decision.md). If your income is above the Roth phase-out (2026: $153,000–$168,000 single, $242,000–$252,000 married filing jointly), use the [backdoor Roth](backdoor-roth-and-mega-backdoor-roth.md). If your 401(k) is genuinely excellent (low-cost index funds), swapping steps 5 and 6 is fine.

### Step 6 — 401(k) to the employee maximum

**2026 limit: $24,500, plus $8,000 catch-up at 50+, or $11,250 "super catch-up" at ages 60–63** (if the plan offers it). New for 2026: if you earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages from that employer in 2025, your catch-up must go in as Roth (after-tax); if your plan has no Roth option, you can't make catch-up contributions at all. Ask HR.

### Step 7 — Mega backdoor Roth (if available)

Some plans allow *after-tax* (not Roth) contributions above the $24,500 line, up to the total plan limit of **$72,000 in 2026** (all sources: your deferrals + employer money + after-tax). Those after-tax dollars can then be converted to Roth. This is the largest Roth-funding channel that exists for W-2 employees, but only about a third of plans allow it. Details in [backdoor-roth-and-mega-backdoor-roth.md](backdoor-roth-and-mega-backdoor-roth.md).

### Step 8 — Medium-interest debt and 529 plans

Debt around 4–7% is roughly a coin flip against expected market returns, so pay it down if it bothers you, or invest if you're comfortable with risk. If you have kids, a 529 offers tax-free growth for education (and up to $35,000 lifetime can later roll into the child's Roth IRA under SECURE 2.0). Fund retirement before college: you can borrow for school, not for retirement.

### Step 9 — Taxable brokerage

No contribution limit, no withdrawal restrictions, and still pretty tax-efficient if you hold broad index ETFs and avoid selling. Long-term gains are taxed at 0/15/20% — often lower than your salary rate — and there's a step-up in basis at death. See [tax-efficient-fund-selection.md](tax-efficient-fund-selection.md).

### Step 10 — Low-interest debt (mortgage)

A 3% mortgage is cheap money; paying it early is a guaranteed 3% return, which loses to expected market returns but wins on peace of mind. Purely personal.

## A full-year worked example

Sam, 35, single, earns $95,000 and can save $25,000 this year. Employer matches 100% of the first 4%.

| Step | Amount | Running total |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k) to match (4%) | $3,800 | $3,800 (plus $3,800 free) |
| HSA (self-only) | $4,400 | $8,200 |
| Roth IRA | $7,500 | $15,700 |
| More 401(k) | $9,300 | $25,000 |

Sam never touches a taxable account, captures $3,800 of free money, and shelters every dollar from annual taxes. Next year, if raises allow, the extra goes to the 401(k) until it hits $24,500.

## How to actually do it

1. Open a high-yield savings account; automate a transfer until it holds one month of expenses.
2. Log in to your 401(k) portal, find the match formula in the plan summary, and set your contribution to at least that percentage.
3. List every debt with its interest rate. Attack anything above ~7% with every spare dollar.
4. Check whether your health plan is HSA-eligible (2026: deductible at least $1,700 self / $3,400 family). If so, elect payroll HSA contributions and open an investable HSA (Fidelity or Lively) to transfer into.
5. Open an IRA at a low-cost brokerage; set up automatic monthly contributions ($625/month hits $7,500).
6. Raise the 401(k) percentage each time you get a raise until you reach $24,500.
7. Ask HR: "Does our plan allow after-tax contributions and in-plan Roth conversions?" If yes, read the mega backdoor file.
8. Anything left goes to a taxable account in a total-market index ETF.

## Key takeaways

- The employer match is the highest-return investment you will ever make; never leave it on the table.
- Guaranteed returns from paying high-interest debt beat uncertain market returns.
- HSA beats IRA beats 401(k) on tax treatment, which is why the waterfall orders them that way after the match.
- IRA before 401(k) because you control the fees; swap them if your 401(k) is excellent.
- 2026 numbers: 401(k) $24,500; IRA $7,500; HSA $4,400/$8,750; total 401(k) additions $72,000.
- High earners (>$150k prior-year wages) must make 401(k) catch-ups as Roth starting 2026.
- Retirement before college; you can't borrow for retirement.
- Automate every step so the order happens without willpower.
