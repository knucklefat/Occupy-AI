# Tax-Efficient Fund Selection

**In one sentence:** In a taxable account, the fund's *structure* (ETF vs mutual fund) and *behavior* (index vs active, turnover, distributions) can cost or save you more than its expense ratio, so pick broad index ETFs for taxable, and park the tax-hungry stuff — bonds, REITs, active funds — inside IRAs and 401(k)s.

*Last checked September 2026.*

## Two kinds of tax drag

Your taxable account gets taxed two ways: when *you* sell (which you control), and when the *fund* pays out dividends or capital-gain distributions (which you don't). The second kind is the silent killer. A fund that distributes 3% of its value in capital gains every year forces you to pay tax on gains you never asked for — and, worse, to pay it on growth that happened before you even bought in.

Morningstar's **tax-cost ratio** measures this drag directly: it's the percentage of a fund's annual return lost to taxes on distributions for an investor in the top bracket. A broad index ETF might show 0.4–0.5% (all from dividends). An active mutual fund can show 1.5–2.5%. That's a hidden expense ratio bigger than the visible one. You can find it on any fund's Morningstar page under the "Price" or "Tax" tab.

## Why ETFs beat mutual funds in taxable accounts

When a mutual fund investor redeems, the fund sells holdings to raise cash, realizing gains that are distributed to *all remaining shareholders* at year-end. When an ETF investor sells, they sell shares on the exchange to another investor; the fund does nothing. When large institutions ("authorized participants") do redeem directly, the ETF hands them a basket of securities **in kind** rather than cash — and the fund manager chooses the *lowest-basis* lots to hand over, flushing embedded gains out of the fund tax-free. Result: the major broad-market ETFs (VTI, ITOT, SCHB, VOO, IVV, VXUS, IXUS) have paid essentially zero capital-gain distributions for a decade or more.

**Vanguard's patent.** Since 2001 Vanguard had a patent on running an ETF as a *share class* of its index mutual fund, letting the ETF's in-kind redemptions scrub gains from the mutual fund too. That's why Vanguard's index mutual funds (VTSAX, VFIAX) were as tax-efficient as ETFs while Fidelity's and Schwab's equivalents occasionally distributed gains. The patent **expired in May 2023**, and in late 2025 the SEC granted Dimensional Fund Advisors the first exemptive order allowing ETF share classes of existing mutual funds; dozens of other firms have applications in. Expect more mutual funds to gain this feature over 2026–2027, but until your specific fund has it, prefer the ETF.

**Heartbeat trades.** ETFs also use a well-documented maneuver: a bank deposits a large block of cash for new shares, and days later redeems in kind, letting the fund shed low-basis stock around index rebalances. It's legal, routine, and one reason even ETFs tracking frequently changing indexes rarely distribute gains.

## Index vs active turnover

Turnover is the share of the portfolio traded in a year. A total-market index fund turns over roughly 2–4%; a typical active fund 30–100%. Every trade of a winner in a mutual fund creates a gain that gets distributed. A high-turnover active *ETF* mostly avoids distributions via in-kind redemptions, but its short-term gains from derivatives or its inability to redeem certain holdings in kind can still leak. For taxable accounts: broad index ETF first, index mutual fund second, active anything last.

## Avoid buying distributions

Mutual funds declare capital-gain distributions in November–December. If you buy a fund the week before its record date, you receive the distribution — which is really a return of your own money — and owe tax on it. Fund companies publish estimated distributions in October/November. In a taxable account, check the estimate before buying late in the year; wait until the day after the ex-date. Irrelevant in an IRA.

## Asset location: what goes where

The goal is to hold the least tax-efficient assets where taxes don't apply:

| Asset | Annual tax drag | Best home |
|---|---|---|
| Total US stock index ETF | Low (qualified dividends ~1.3%) | Taxable is fine |
| International stock index ETF | Low-moderate (foreign tax credit recoverable only in taxable) | Taxable |
| Taxable bonds / bond funds | High (interest at ordinary rates) | 401(k) / traditional IRA |
| REITs | High (mostly ordinary income) | IRA / Roth |
| Active or high-turnover funds | High | IRA / 401(k) |
| Small-cap value, high-dividend funds | Moderate | Tax-advantaged if room |
| Municipal bonds | ~Zero federal | Taxable only (pointless in an IRA) |

Put your highest-expected-return assets in Roth (tax-free growth) and your bonds in traditional accounts (where the government "owns" part of the balance anyway). Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts so you never realize gains in taxable.

## Municipal bonds for high earners

Interest from municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, and from state tax if the bond is from your state. To compare with a taxable bond, compute the **tax-equivalent yield**: muni yield ÷ (1 − your marginal rate).

**Worked example:** A 35%-bracket investor (plus 3.8% NIIT) compares a 3.4% national muni fund (VTEB, MUB) with a 4.8% taxable bond fund. Tax-equivalent yield of the muni: 3.4% ÷ (1 − 0.388) = **5.56%**, beating 4.8%. For a 22%-bracket investor: 3.4% ÷ 0.78 = 4.36%, losing to 4.8%. Munis generally win only at the 32% bracket and above, and only in taxable accounts. Avoid the pitfalls: private-activity bond interest can trigger AMT, and muni interest still counts toward Social Security taxability and IRMAA.

## Other tax-efficiency details

- **Foreign tax credit.** International funds withhold foreign taxes; in a taxable account you can claim a credit (often 0.2–0.3% of the position's value per year). In an IRA that credit is lost, which is a small argument for holding international in taxable.
- **Treasury interest** is exempt from *state* tax; hold Treasury or TIPS funds in taxable if you live in a high-tax state and need bonds there.
- **Series I bonds** defer federal tax until redemption and skip state tax entirely.
- **Target-date funds** are fine in a 401(k) but poor in taxable: they hold bonds, rebalance internally, and some have distributed large gains (Vanguard's 2021 episode cost taxable investors dearly).
- **Zero-fee funds** like Fidelity's FZROX are mutual funds with no ETF share class; they're excellent in an IRA but can distribute gains in taxable and can't be transferred to another broker in kind.

## How to actually do it

1. For each fund in your taxable account, look up its Morningstar tax-cost ratio and its capital-gain distribution history (the fund's "Distributions" page). Anything above ~0.7% tax-cost or with regular gain distributions is a candidate to move.
2. Replace mutual funds with the equivalent broad index ETF (VTSAX → VTI; FSKAX → ITOT or VTI; SWTSX → SCHB) for *new* money. Don't sell existing shares if they carry big gains; just stop adding.
3. Move bonds and REITs into your 401(k)/IRA and hold more stock in taxable to keep your overall allocation the same.
4. If you're in the 32%+ bracket and need bonds in taxable, use a national muni ETF (or your state's fund if it's large and low-cost).
5. Never buy a mutual fund in taxable in November or December without checking the estimated distribution.
6. Rebalance only inside tax-advantaged accounts or with new contributions.
7. Turn on specific-lot identification so any sale you do make is as cheap as possible.

## Key takeaways

- Fund distributions are a hidden tax you don't control; the tax-cost ratio on Morningstar measures them.
- ETFs' in-kind redemptions let them flush gains; broad index ETFs have distributed nearly zero for years.
- Vanguard's ETF-share-class patent expired May 2023 and the SEC began approving other firms in late 2025, but the ETF is still the safe default for taxable money.
- Bonds, REITs, and active funds belong in IRAs and 401(k)s; stock index ETFs and munis belong in taxable.
- Munis only make sense at the 32% bracket and above; check tax-equivalent yield.
- Don't buy mutual funds in taxable just before a year-end distribution.
- International in taxable recovers the foreign tax credit; Treasuries in taxable skip state tax.
- Rebalance in tax-advantaged accounts to avoid realizing gains.
