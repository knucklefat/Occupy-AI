# Charitable Giving and Estate Tricks

**In one sentence:** Never give cash to charity if you own appreciated shares, never take a required minimum distribution you don't need if you're charitable, and never let low-basis assets get sold before death when they could pass to heirs with the gain erased.

*2026 figures from IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, July 2025). Last checked September 2026.*

## Donate appreciated shares, not cash

If you give a charity $10,000 of stock you bought for $2,000 and have held more than a year, you deduct the full **$10,000** fair market value *and* nobody ever pays capital-gains tax on the $8,000 gain. If you'd sold first and given cash, you'd have paid 15–23.8% on the gain (up to $1,900) and had less to give.

**Worked example:** Nina, 35% bracket plus NIIT, wants to give $20,000. She holds VTI shares worth $20,000 with a $6,000 basis.
- Sell and donate cash: gain $14,000 × 23.8% = $3,332 tax; $16,668 to charity, or $20,000 to charity with $3,332 out of her pocket.
- Donate the shares: $20,000 to charity, $0 gains tax, full $20,000 deduction (if itemizing), and she can immediately rebuy VTI with her cash to reset the basis to $20,000. No wash-sale issue, since there's no loss.

Rules: shares must be held more than one year (otherwise the deduction is limited to basis); deduction for appreciated securities to public charities is capped at 30% of adjusted gross income per year (cash is 60%), with a five-year carryforward. Most large charities and every donor-advised fund accept stock; the transfer is a simple form at your brokerage.

## Donor-advised funds (DAFs)

A DAF is a charitable account you fund now (getting the deduction immediately), invest, and grant out to charities over years. Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, and Vanguard Charitable are the big ones; minimums range from $0 to $25,000 and fees run ~0.6% plus fund costs. Why bother:

- **Bunching.** The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 single / $32,200 joint. If your itemized deductions (mortgage interest, SALT up to $40,400, charity) hover near that line, you get almost no tax benefit from giving. Instead, put three years of giving into a DAF in one year, itemize big that year, take the standard deduction the other two.
- **Timing.** A big bonus, stock sale, or Roth conversion year is the year to front-load charitable deductions.
- **Simplicity.** One receipt; the DAF handles the stock liquidation; grants can be anonymous.

**Worked example of bunching:** A married couple gives $12,000 a year and has $22,000 of other itemized deductions. Each year: $34,000 itemized vs $32,200 standard — only $1,800 of the gift produces any tax benefit. Instead they put $36,000 (three years) into a DAF in 2026: itemized $58,000, a $25,800 excess over the standard deduction, worth about $6,200 at 24%. In 2027 and 2028 they take the standard deduction. Same giving, roughly $5,000 more tax saved over three years.

**New OBBBA rules for 2026:** itemizers can only deduct charitable gifts above a floor of **0.5% of AGI**; top-bracket (37%) taxpayers get deduction value capped at **35%**; and non-itemizers get a new above-the-line deduction of **$1,000 single / $2,000 joint** for *cash* gifts (not to DAFs). The 0.5% floor makes bunching more valuable, since the floor applies once per bunched year rather than every year.

## Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs)

At **age 70½** or older, you can transfer up to **$111,000 (2026)** per person directly from an IRA to charity. The amount is excluded from income entirely — better than a deduction, because it also lowers AGI (helping with Medicare IRMAA surcharges, Social Security taxability, and the new 0.5% floor) and works even if you take the standard deduction. From age 73 (or 75), QCDs count toward your RMD.

**Worked example:** Walt, 74, has a $40,000 RMD and gives $15,000 a year to his church. Taking the RMD and writing a check: $40,000 of income, and (using the standard deduction) no deduction for the gift — tax at 22% ≈ $8,800. Sending $15,000 as a QCD and taking the other $25,000: $25,000 of income, tax ≈ $5,500. Saves $3,300 and may keep him below an IRMAA tier.

Mechanics: the check must go from the IRA custodian to the charity (or be payable to the charity; some custodians issue IRA checkbooks). DAFs and private foundations are *not* eligible recipients. A one-time QCD of up to about $55,000 (indexed) can fund a charitable gift annuity or remainder trust. The 1099-R won't flag it — you report it yourself on Form 1040 by writing "QCD" next to the line.

## Gifting to family

- **Annual exclusion 2026: $19,000 per recipient per giver**, so a married couple can give $38,000 to each child (or child-in-law, or grandchild) with no paperwork.
- Gifts above that just require **Form 709** and eat into your lifetime exemption; no tax is due until you exceed it.
- **Lifetime estate and gift exemption 2026: $15 million per person ($30 million per couple)**, made permanent and inflation-indexed by OBBBA. Under 1% of estates will owe federal estate tax, but a dozen states (plus DC) have their own estate or inheritance taxes with far lower thresholds — Oregon and Massachusetts start at $1–2 million.
- Direct payment of tuition or medical bills to the institution is unlimited and doesn't count as a gift.
- Gifting *appreciated* stock to a child in the 0% capital-gains bracket lets them sell tax-free (watch the kiddie tax: unearned income above $2,700 for a dependent under 19, or under 24 if a student, is taxed at the parents' rate).

## 529 superfunding

A 529 contribution is a gift, so it's normally limited to $19,000 per year per giver. The **five-year election** lets you front-load **$95,000** (five years' worth) in one year — **$190,000** per couple per child — and spread it over five years on Form 709. The money starts compounding tax-free immediately instead of trickling in.

**Worked example:** Grandparents put $190,000 into a newborn's 529. At 7% for 18 years it's about **$642,000**, tax-free for education — versus about $527,000 if they'd contributed $38,000 a year for five years. If the child doesn't need it all, up to **$35,000 lifetime** can roll into the beneficiary's Roth IRA (account open 15+ years, subject to annual IRA limits), the beneficiary can be changed to a sibling or the parents, or it can fund K-12 tuition ($20,000/year from 2026) and, under OBBBA, tutoring, testing fees, and credentialing programs. If the giver dies within the five years, the un-elapsed portions come back into their estate — usually harmless under a $15M exemption.

## Step-up in basis: sell the right things, keep the right things

Assets held in taxable accounts get a new cost basis equal to their value on the date of death. So:

- **Spend down** cash, bonds, and high-basis holdings first in retirement; leave the old, low-basis stock for last.
- **Never** gift low-basis stock to your heirs while alive (they inherit your basis); gift cash or high-basis shares instead, and let the low-basis shares step up at death.
- **Do** gift low-basis stock to charity, where basis is irrelevant.
- Retirement accounts get no step-up and, for most non-spouse heirs, must be emptied within 10 years — so they're the best thing to spend or convert to Roth during life, and the worst thing to leave to a high-earning child.
- In community-property states, both halves of jointly owned property step up when the first spouse dies; elsewhere only the decedent's half does. Married couples in common-law states can sometimes retitle assets to the spouse more likely to die first (blunt, but it's what estate lawyers do).

## Beneficiary designations beat wills

IRAs, 401(k)s, HSAs, life insurance, and any account with a "transfer on death" (TOD) or "payable on death" (POD) registration pass **by the beneficiary form, not the will**, and skip probate. An outdated form (ex-spouse, deceased parent, no one listed) is one of the most common and most painful estate mistakes. Name primary *and* contingent beneficiaries; use "per stirpes" if you want a deceased child's share to go to their children; and re-check after every marriage, divorce, birth, or death. Add TOD registration to taxable brokerage accounts for the same probate-skipping effect.

## How to actually do it

1. This year, before giving cash to any charity, check your taxable account for a long-term holding with a large gain. Transfer those shares instead (brokerages have a "gift securities" form); rebuy with the cash.
2. If you give more than a few thousand a year and are near the standard-deduction line, open a DAF and fund 2–3 years of giving in one shot, ideally with appreciated stock, in a high-income year.
3. At 70½, route all charitable giving through QCDs from your IRA; tell your custodian to send checks directly.
4. If you have children or grandchildren and money to spare, superfund a 529 with the five-year election; file Form 709 that spring.
5. Once a year, log into every retirement account, HSA, and life-insurance policy and confirm the beneficiaries. Add TOD to taxable accounts.
6. Build a simple "sell order" for retirement: cash and bonds → high-basis stock → traditional IRA (or convert to Roth) → low-basis stock (last, ideally never).
7. If your estate might exceed your state's threshold (check — it may be $1–7 million), see an estate attorney about trusts, gifting, and titling.

## Key takeaways

- Donating long-term appreciated shares skips the gains tax and still gives a full deduction; rebuy the shares with cash.
- DAFs let you bunch several years of giving into one itemizing year; the new 0.5%-of-AGI floor makes this more valuable in 2026.
- QCDs (70½+, $111,000 in 2026) reduce AGI directly and satisfy RMDs; they beat any deduction.
- 2026 gift exclusion $19,000; 529 superfund $95,000 ($190,000 per couple); lifetime exemption $15 million.
- Low-basis assets should be the last thing sold, and the first thing donated or inherited.
- Retirement accounts get no step-up and a 10-year heir payout; spend or convert them first.
- Beneficiary forms override wills; review them annually and add TOD to taxable accounts.
