# HSA as a Stealth Retirement Account

**In one sentence:** A Health Savings Account is the only account where money goes in untaxed, grows untaxed, and comes out untaxed — so the winning move is to invest it, pay medical bills from your checking account, keep the receipts, and reimburse yourself decades later.

*2026 limits from IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19. Last checked September 2026.*

## The triple tax advantage, spelled out

Every other account gives you two of three tax breaks. A traditional 401(k) skips tax going in and while growing, but taxes withdrawals. A Roth skips tax while growing and coming out, but not going in. The HSA skips all three:

1. **Contributions are deductible** — and if made through payroll they also avoid the 7.65% Social Security and Medicare (FICA) tax, which even a 401(k) can't do.
2. **Growth is untaxed** — dividends, interest, capital gains, all of it.
3. **Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are untaxed** — at any age, with no time limit on when the expense occurred (as long as it was after the HSA was opened).

**Worked example:** Jordan, in the 24% federal bracket plus 5% state, contributes $4,400 through payroll. Tax saved: 24% + 5% + 7.65% = 36.65%, or about $1,612. The same $4,400 in a Roth IRA would have cost $6,945 of pre-tax salary. In a taxable account, its dividends would be taxed every year on top.

## Who qualifies (2026)

You must be covered by an **HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan (HDHP)** and have no other disqualifying coverage (a general-purpose FSA, Medicare, or a spouse's non-HDHP plan that covers you). For 2026 an HDHP must have:

| | Self-only | Family |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum deductible | $1,700 | $3,400 |
| Maximum out-of-pocket | $8,500 | $17,000 |

Not every high-deductible plan is "HSA-eligible" — the insurer must say so explicitly.

**Contribution limits 2026:** **$4,400** self-only, **$8,750** family, plus a **$1,000 catch-up** at age 55+ (each spouse 55+ needs their own HSA to get their own catch-up). Employer contributions count toward the limit. If you're only eligible part of the year, the limit is prorated by month (unless you use the "last-month rule" and stay eligible through the following year).

## Invest it, don't spend it

Most people use the HSA as a checking account for co-pays. That wastes its best feature. The strategy:

1. Contribute the maximum every year.
2. Move the balance into low-cost index funds (some providers hold a small cash minimum; Fidelity and Lively allow $0).
3. Pay current medical bills from your regular cash flow.
4. Save every receipt and Explanation of Benefits (scan them to a cloud folder, log them in a spreadsheet).
5. Let the account compound for decades.

**Why this works:** the IRS doesn't require you to reimburse yourself in the same year as the expense. A $300 dental bill you paid out of pocket in 2026 can be reimbursed tax-free from the HSA in 2056. So the HSA becomes a Roth-like bucket whose withdrawals you can trigger at will, in any amount up to your lifetime receipts.

**Worked example:** Casey maxes a family HSA at $8,750 a year for 25 years, invested at 7%. Balance at the end: about **$553,000**, all of it tax-free for medical costs. Fidelity estimates a 65-year-old couple needs roughly $330,000 for healthcare in retirement, so a large chunk of this will be spent on Medicare premiums, dental, vision, hearing aids, and long-term-care insurance premiums — all qualified expenses.

## The retirement account fallback

After **age 65**, HSA withdrawals for *non*-medical purposes are simply taxed as ordinary income — exactly like a traditional IRA, with no 20% penalty. So even if you stay healthy, the worst case is a traditional IRA that let you skip FICA on the way in. Before 65, non-medical withdrawals carry a 20% penalty plus income tax, so don't raid it.

What counts as medical in retirement: Medicare Part B, D, and Advantage premiums (but not Medigap), long-term-care insurance premiums up to age-based limits, and any out-of-pocket care. The list of qualified expenses is in IRS Publication 502.

## Watch-outs

- **Enrolling in Medicare ends HSA eligibility.** Medicare Part A is retroactive up to six months when you enroll after 65, so stop contributions six months before enrolling to avoid an excess-contribution penalty.
- **HSAs are bad inheritance vehicles.** A spouse inherits it as an HSA; anyone else receives the full balance as taxable income in one year. Spend it down late in life, or name your spouse.
- **Some states don't play along.** California and New Jersey tax HSA contributions and earnings as if the account were taxable. The federal benefit still makes it worthwhile, but keep records.
- **Ineligible expenses** trigger income tax plus 20% penalty before 65. Cosmetic surgery, gym memberships (usually), and most over-the-counter vitamins don't qualify.
- **Employer HSAs often have fees and weak fund menus.** You can hold your employer's HSA (to get payroll FICA savings) and periodically transfer the balance to a better HSA of your choosing; transfers between HSAs are unlimited and untaxed.

## How to actually do it

1. During open enrollment, confirm your plan is labeled "HSA-eligible." Compare total premiums + expected out-of-pocket costs against the PPO alternative; the HDHP usually wins for healthy people and often wins even for heavy users once the employer's HSA contribution is counted.
2. Elect payroll contributions that hit the 2026 max ($4,400 / $8,750, plus $1,000 at 55+), net of any employer contribution.
3. Open a Fidelity or Lively HSA. Set up a recurring transfer (or a once-a-year lump) from the employer HSA.
4. Set the investment to a total-market index fund; keep only what the provider requires in cash.
5. Create a "HSA receipts" folder. Every medical payment from your own money goes in with the date, amount, and a note. A running spreadsheet total is your "reimbursable at any time" balance.
6. In retirement, use the account for Medicare premiums and care; after 65, treat any excess as a traditional IRA.
7. Name your spouse as beneficiary; if unmarried, plan to draw it down in your later years.

## Key takeaways

- The HSA is the only triple-tax-free account, and payroll contributions also dodge FICA.
- 2026: $4,400 self / $8,750 family / +$1,000 at 55+; HDHP deductible must be at least $1,700 / $3,400.
- Invest the balance and pay current bills out of pocket; reimburse yourself years later from saved receipts.
- After 65 it works like a traditional IRA for any purpose, with no penalty.
- Stop contributing six months before Medicare enrollment.
- Non-spouse heirs get taxed on the whole balance, so spend it down late in life.
- Fidelity and Lively charge nothing and have no investment minimum; move money there from a fee-charging employer HSA.
