# Common Costly Mistakes

**In one sentence:** Most of the money people lose to taxes and fees isn't lost to bad investments but to a short list of avoidable administrative errors — cashing out a 401(k), skipping the match, buying the wrong thing in the wrong account, or forgetting a form — each of which is cheap to prevent and expensive to fix.

*2026 figures. Last checked September 2026.*

## 1. Cashing out a 401(k) when you change jobs

Roughly 40% of workers cash out at least part of a 401(k) on a job change, mostly small balances. The bill: ordinary income tax on the whole amount, a 10% early-withdrawal penalty if under 55 (or 59½ for an IRA), 20% mandatory withholding up front, and the loss of decades of compounding.

**Worked example:** A 30-year-old cashes out $20,000. Tax at 22% + 10% penalty + 5% state = $7,400 gone; they net $12,600. Left invested at 7% until 65, the $20,000 would have been about **$213,000**. Even a $3,000 balance cashed out at 25 is roughly $45,000 at 65. Do a direct rollover instead (see [brokerage-selection-and-perks.md](brokerage-selection-and-perks.md)). Note that since 2024 employers can automatically roll balances under $7,000 to an IRA, and "auto-portability" networks can move them to your new plan — but don't rely on it.

## 2. Not getting the full employer match

Contributing 3% when the match goes up to 6% forfeits an instant 50–100% return. On a $70,000 salary with a 50%-of-6% match, contributing 3% instead of 6% leaves $1,050 a year on the table — about $100,000 over a career after growth. A related error: front-loading contributions so you hit the $24,500 cap in September and stop getting matched for the rest of the year, if your plan matches per paycheck without a "true-up." Check the plan document or ask HR.

## 3. Holding bonds (or REITs) in taxable when there's room in an IRA

Bond interest and REIT distributions are taxed as ordinary income every year. If you hold $100,000 of bonds yielding 4.5% in a taxable account at a 32% bracket, you pay $1,440 a year in tax; the same bonds in a traditional IRA, with $100,000 of stock index ETF swapped into taxable, pay essentially nothing annually (the stock's 1.3% qualified dividend costs ~$200). Over 20 years the location swap alone is worth tens of thousands. See [tax-efficient-fund-selection.md](tax-efficient-fund-selection.md). The exception: if you genuinely need bonds in taxable for liquidity, use Treasuries (state-tax-free) or munis (32%+ bracket).

## 4. Wash-sale violations across accounts

You sell VTI at a loss in your taxable account for tax-loss harvesting; the same week your 401(k) payroll contribution buys the plan's total-market fund, or your IRA's dividend reinvestment buys more VTI. The loss is disallowed — and if the purchase happened in an IRA, it's disallowed *permanently* (Revenue Ruling 2008-5), not just deferred. Brokers only track wash sales within one account at one firm, so nothing warns you. Fixes: turn off dividend reinvestment in taxable accounts, keep your IRA and 401(k) in *different* funds from your taxable account (e.g., VTI in taxable, FSKAX or a 500 fund in the 401(k)), and harvest into a similar-but-not-identical fund.

## 5. Overpaying an advisor (or paying for nothing)

A 1% AUM fee on a $750,000 portfolio is $7,500 a year. If the advisor's actual work is a quarterly rebalance and an annual call, that's an hourly rate above $1,000. Worse are "fee-based" advisors at insurance companies or wirehouses who layer commissions and proprietary products on top. Ask: "Are you a fiduciary at all times? Are you fee-only? What do I pay in total dollars per year, including fund expenses?" If you can't get a straight answer, leave. A flat-fee planner for $3,000 a year, or a robo-advisor at 0.25%, does the same job. See [minimizing-fees-and-costs.md](minimizing-fees-and-costs.md).

## 6. Buying a mutual fund right before its year-end distribution

In December, an active mutual fund announces a 6% capital-gains distribution. You buy $50,000 of it on December 10; on December 15 you receive a $3,000 distribution — your own money handed back — and owe up to ~$714 in tax on it. The fund's share price drops by the same $3,000. Check estimated distributions (published on fund websites in October–November) before any taxable-account mutual fund purchase in Q4, or just buy the ETF equivalent.

## 7. Not naming (or not updating) beneficiaries

An IRA with no beneficiary goes to your estate, loses the stretch options a named heir would have, sits in probate, and may have to be emptied within five years. An IRA still naming an ex-spouse goes to the ex-spouse — courts have upheld this repeatedly even when the will says otherwise. Fix it in ten minutes: name a primary and contingent beneficiary on every IRA, 401(k), HSA, life-insurance policy, and add transfer-on-death to taxable accounts. Review after every life event.

## 8. Skipping Form 8606 after a backdoor Roth

The nondeductible IRA contribution is your tax basis. If you never file Form 8606, the IRS has no record of it, and years later the conversion (or a withdrawal) gets taxed in full — you pay tax twice on the same $7,500. Every year you make a nondeductible contribution *or* a conversion, the form must be attached. Missing years can be filed late on their own. See [backdoor-roth-and-mega-backdoor-roth.md](backdoor-roth-and-mega-backdoor-roth.md).

## 9. Rolling an old 401(k) into an IRA, then trying a backdoor Roth

The rollover IRA makes the pro-rata rule bite: a $150,000 pre-tax IRA turns a $7,500 backdoor conversion into a 95% taxable event. If backdoor Roths are in your future, roll old 401(k)s into your *current* 401(k) (or a solo 401(k)), not an IRA.

## 10. Letting cash rot at 0.4%

Schwab's default bank sweep paid about 0.4% in mid-2026 while its own money market fund paid ~3.5%. Robinhood's non-Gold cash and many bank "savings" accounts pay similarly little. $25,000 of idle cash at 0.4% instead of 3.5% costs $775 a year. Sweep it into a money-market fund, a Treasury fund, or a high-yield savings account — or better, invest it.

## 11. Missing a required minimum distribution

The penalty is 25% of the amount you should have withdrawn (10% if fixed within two years). On a $60,000 RMD that's $15,000. Set up automatic RMDs with your custodian the year you turn 73 (born 1951–59) or 75 (born 1960+). Inherited IRAs have their own RMD rules (most non-spouse heirs must empty the account within 10 years, with annual RMDs in years 1–9 if the original owner had already started them).

## 12. Contributing to a Roth IRA when your income is too high

Directly contributing $7,500 to a Roth while earning above the 2026 phase-out ($168,000 single / $252,000 joint) creates an excess contribution with a 6% penalty *every year* until removed. If you're anywhere near the limit, use the backdoor from the start; it works at every income level. If you already made the error, recharacterize to traditional before the tax deadline and then convert.

## 13. Selling winners in under a year

Selling a stock or ETF at day 300 instead of day 366 turns a 15% tax into a 24–37% tax. On a $20,000 gain in the 32% bracket, that's $3,400 versus $6,400. Unless the position is collapsing, wait.

## 14. Ignoring the HSA or using it as a checking account

Choosing the PPO by default when the HDHP plus employer HSA contribution would have cost less, or spending the HSA on every co-pay instead of investing it, forfeits the best tax shelter available. See [hsa-as-a-stealth-retirement-account.md](hsa-as-a-stealth-retirement-account.md).

## 15. Taking a 401(k) loan and then leaving the job

A 401(k) loan is fine while employed. Leave (or get laid off) and the outstanding balance is usually due by the tax-filing deadline for that year; anything unpaid becomes a taxable distribution plus a 10% penalty if under 59½. Don't borrow from the 401(k) unless you're confident of your job and can repay quickly.

## 16. Making a taxable Roth conversion in a year you get a big bonus

Conversions add to ordinary income. Converting $100,000 in a year you're already in the 35% bracket instead of a gap year at 12% costs $23,000 extra. Convert in *low* years — sabbaticals, early retirement, layoffs — and never in a year with a bonus, a home sale with taxable gain, or a big stock-option exercise.

## How to actually do it

1. Pull up every account today. For each: is the beneficiary correct? Is cash earning a real yield? Is the fund menu cheap?
2. Confirm your 401(k) contribution captures the full match and that the plan has a true-up.
3. If you've left a job: initiate a direct rollover this week — to the new 401(k) if you plan backdoor Roths, else to an IRA.
4. Turn off dividend reinvestment in taxable accounts; hold different (but equivalent) funds in taxable vs tax-advantaged.
5. Move bonds and REITs into IRAs; move stock index ETFs into taxable.
6. Set a December calendar reminder: check mutual-fund distribution estimates, estimate the year's income, decide on gain/loss harvesting or a Roth conversion.
7. If you did a backdoor Roth in any prior year, confirm Form 8606 exists in that year's return; file missing ones.
8. If you pay an advisor, write down the total annual dollar cost and what you get for it. Compare against a flat-fee quote.

## Key takeaways

- Cashing out a small 401(k) at 30 can cost six figures at 65; always roll it over directly.
- The employer match is free money; contribute at least enough to get all of it, and check for true-up.
- Bonds and REITs go in IRAs; stock index ETFs go in taxable.
- Wash sales span all accounts, including IRAs and 401(k)s, and brokers won't warn you.
- Verify beneficiary forms yearly; they override your will.
- File Form 8606 for every nondeductible contribution and conversion.
- Don't roll old 401(k)s into an IRA if you'll use the backdoor Roth.
- Idle cash, missed RMDs, short-term sales, and badly timed conversions are all cheap to avoid and expensive to ignore.
