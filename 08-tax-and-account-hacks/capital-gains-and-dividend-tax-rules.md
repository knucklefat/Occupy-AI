# Capital Gains and Dividend Tax Rules

**In one sentence:** Hold investments more than a year, know where the 0/15/20% long-term brackets fall, and you can control — sometimes to zero — how much tax your taxable account ever generates.

*2026 thresholds from IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32. Last checked September 2026.*

## Short-term vs long-term

A **capital gain** is the profit when you sell something for more than you paid (your **cost basis**). If you owned it for **one year or less**, it's short-term and taxed as ordinary income at your regular bracket (10–37%). Own it for **more than one year** — a year and a day — and it's long-term, taxed at the preferential rates below. Holding period starts the day after purchase and includes the sale date.

## 2026 long-term capital gains brackets

The rate depends on your *taxable income* (after deductions), with gains stacked on top of ordinary income:

| Rate | Single | Married filing jointly | Head of household |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0%** | up to $49,450 | up to $98,900 | up to $66,200 |
| **15%** | $49,451 – $545,500 | $98,901 – $613,700 | $66,201 – $579,600 |
| **20%** | over $545,500 | over $613,700 | over $579,600 |

Because the standard deduction ($16,100 single / $32,200 joint) comes off first, a married couple with no other income can realize about **$131,100** of long-term gains in 2026 and pay **$0 federal tax**. Two exceptions: collectibles (art, coins, precious-metal ETFs structured as trusts) max out at 28%, and unrecaptured depreciation on real estate is taxed at up to 25%.

## Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

An additional **3.8%** applies to investment income (gains, dividends, interest, rents) when modified adjusted gross income exceeds **$200,000 single / $250,000 married filing jointly**. These thresholds are *not* inflation-indexed, so more people hit them every year. The effective top federal rate on long-term gains is therefore 23.8%, and on short-term gains up to 40.8%.

## Qualified vs ordinary dividends

**Qualified dividends** are taxed at the long-term capital gains rates. **Ordinary (non-qualified) dividends** are taxed as regular income. To be qualified, the dividend must come from a US corporation or qualifying foreign company, *and* you must hold the shares for more than 60 days within the 121-day window around the ex-dividend date. Most dividends from broad stock index funds are qualified (typically 90–100% for US funds, less for international funds). REIT distributions, bond fund interest, and money-market yields are ordinary — one reason those belong in tax-advantaged accounts. Your 1099-DIV shows the split in boxes 1a (total) and 1b (qualified).

## Tax-gain harvesting in the 0% bracket

Most people know tax-*loss* harvesting. Its mirror image is less known and often more valuable: if you're in the 0% long-term bracket, **sell winners, pay nothing, and immediately buy them back** to reset your cost basis higher. No wash-sale rule applies to gains.

**Worked example:** A retired couple has $40,000 of Social Security and pension income and $200,000 of index fund shares with a $120,000 basis. Taxable income before gains: $40,000 − $32,200 = $7,800. Room to the top of the 0% bracket: $98,900 − $7,800 = $91,100. They sell $200,000 of shares (gain $80,000), pay $0 federal, and rebuy the same fund the same day. New basis: $200,000. If they later need to sell at higher income, the $80,000 of gain is gone forever. Watch state taxes (most states tax all gains as income) and the fact that gains still raise AGI, which can affect ACA subsidies, IRMAA, and taxability of Social Security.

## Tax-loss harvesting and the wash-sale rule

Selling a position at a loss lets you offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar, then deduct up to **$3,000** a year against ordinary income, carrying the rest forward indefinitely. The **wash-sale rule** disallows the loss if you buy the same or "substantially identical" security within **30 days before or after** the sale — a 61-day window. Pitfalls:

- It applies across *all* your accounts, including IRAs and your spouse's accounts. Buying the same fund in an IRA within the window permanently destroys the loss (IRS Revenue Ruling 2008-5).
- Automatic dividend reinvestment and 401(k) payroll purchases of the same fund count as purchases.
- Two S&P 500 funds from different sponsors are widely treated as *not* substantially identical (e.g., VOO → IVV), though the IRS has never ruled precisely. Swapping to a different index (S&P 500 → total market) is the conservative move.
- Since 2024, brokers must track basis and wash sales across covered securities; crypto was historically exempt from wash sales, but check current guidance.

## Cost basis methods

When you sell part of a position, which shares did you sell? The choice changes your gain:

- **FIFO** (first in, first out): the default; sells oldest shares, usually the lowest basis and biggest gain.
- **Average cost**: mutual funds only; simple but inflexible.
- **Specific identification** ("SpecID"): you choose the lots. This is the one to use. Sell high-basis lots to minimize gains, or low-basis lots when harvesting gains in the 0% bracket.

Set your brokerage default to specific identification (Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all allow this in account settings) *before* you sell; you can't change it after settlement.

## Step-up in basis

When you die, your heirs' cost basis in inherited assets resets to the fair market value on the date of death (or six months later, if the estate elects). A $500,000 position bought for $50,000 passes with zero embedded gain. This is why old, low-basis holdings are usually the *last* thing to sell and the best thing to leave to heirs, and why it's often better to gift appreciated shares to charity than to sell them. Assets in IRAs and 401(k)s get no step-up. Community-property states (CA, TX, WA, AZ, NV, ID, LA, NM, WI) give a *full* step-up on both halves when the first spouse dies; common-law states step up only the decedent's half of jointly owned property.

## Holding-period tricks worth knowing

- Sell a loser before the one-year mark (short-term losses are more valuable against short-term gains).
- Wait until day 366 to sell a winner; a 22%-bracket investor pays 15% instead of 22% and skips nothing.
- Gifted shares carry the giver's holding period and basis; inherited shares are always long-term.
- Losses first offset gains of the same type (short vs short, long vs long), then cross over.

## How to actually do it

1. In your brokerage settings, change the default cost-basis method to specific identification.
2. Turn off automatic dividend reinvestment in taxable accounts if you plan to harvest losses; reinvest manually into a different fund.
3. Each December, estimate taxable income. If you're under the 0% threshold, harvest gains up to the line. If you're above it and have losers, harvest losses (swap to a similar-but-not-identical fund for 31 days).
4. Never sell a winner in a taxable account within 12 months unless you have a compelling reason.
5. Keep REITs, bond funds, and high-turnover funds in IRAs and 401(k)s where their ordinary income is sheltered.
6. Check Form 1099-DIV box 1b each spring to confirm your funds are throwing off qualified dividends.
7. Track your carryforward losses on Schedule D; they're an asset.

## Key takeaways

- Long-term (held >1 year) gains are taxed at 0/15/20%; short-term at ordinary rates.
- 2026 0% bracket tops out at $49,450 taxable income single / $98,900 joint; add the standard deduction for total income room.
- NIIT adds 3.8% above $200k/$250k MAGI, and those thresholds never rise.
- Qualified dividends get capital-gains rates; REIT, bond, and money-market income does not.
- Tax-gain harvesting in the 0% bracket permanently erases future tax at no cost.
- The wash-sale rule spans all accounts, including IRAs and a spouse's; dividend reinvestment can trip it.
- Use specific-lot identification for every sale.
- Low-basis holdings get a full step-up at death; sell them last or donate them.
