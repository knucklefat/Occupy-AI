# Roth vs Traditional Decision

**In one sentence:** Traditional wins if your marginal tax rate today is higher than the rate you'll pay when you withdraw, Roth wins if it's lower, and the real skill is engineering low-tax years (early retirement, sabbaticals, job gaps) to convert traditional money to Roth cheaply before required minimum distributions force your hand.

*2026 brackets from IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32. Last checked September 2026.*

## The core math

"Traditional" (pre-tax) means you skip tax now and pay it on withdrawal. "Roth" means you pay tax now and never again. People assume Roth is better because "tax-free growth sounds great," but the arithmetic is symmetric: multiplication is commutative.

**Worked example:** You have $10,000 of pre-tax salary, a 24% bracket now, 7% returns, 25 years.
- Traditional: $10,000 grows to $54,274. Withdraw at a 24% rate → **$41,248**.
- Roth: pay $2,400 tax first, invest $7,600, it grows to **$41,248**.

Identical. The only thing that matters is the rate *now* versus the rate *then*. If you'll withdraw at 12% instead of 24%, traditional yields $47,761 — 16% more. If you'll withdraw at 32%, Roth wins.

## Why traditional usually wins for peak earners

Your contribution is deducted at your **marginal** rate (the top slice of your income). Withdrawals in retirement fill the brackets from the **bottom up**: the first $16,100 (2026 single standard deduction) is taxed at 0%, the next $12,400 at 10%, the next $38,000 at 12%, and so on. A retiree pulling $80,000 a year from a traditional IRA with no other income pays an average rate of about 11% — far below the 24–35% a working professional saves at contribution time.

**2026 federal brackets (taxable income, after the standard deduction of $16,100 single / $32,200 joint):**

| Rate | Single | Married filing jointly |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | $0 – $12,400 | $0 – $24,800 |
| 12% | $12,400 – $50,400 | $24,800 – $100,800 |
| 22% | $50,400 – $105,700 | $100,800 – $211,400 |
| 24% | $105,700 – $201,775 | $211,400 – $403,550 |
| 32% | $201,775 – $256,225 | $403,550 – $512,450 |
| 35% | $256,225 – $640,600 | $512,450 – $768,700 |
| 37% | over $640,600 | over $768,700 |

## Why Roth still wins in many situations

- **You're in the 10–12% bracket** (early career, grad school, part-time). Paying 12% now to never pay again is a bargain.
- **You expect much higher income later** (residents, junior lawyers, founders).
- **You'll have big taxable income in retirement anyway** — a pension, rental income, or a large traditional balance that will produce huge RMDs.
- **You live in a no-income-tax state now but may retire in a high-tax one** (the reverse favors traditional: deduct at California's 9.3% today, withdraw in Florida at 0%).
- **Estate planning**: heirs get Roth money tax-free; inherited traditional IRAs must be emptied within 10 years, often during the heir's peak earning years.
- **Hedging**: nobody knows 2050 tax rates. Holding both types lets you choose which bucket to draw from each year.

A common rule of thumb: traditional in the 22% bracket and above, Roth at 12% and below, and either is fine in between. Always take the employer match regardless — it goes in pre-tax no matter what you choose.

## Roth conversions: paying tax on your terms

A conversion moves money from traditional to Roth and adds the amount to your taxable income that year. There's no income limit and no dollar limit. The trick is to convert in years when your income is unusually low:

- The gap years between retiring and claiming Social Security or hitting RMD age.
- A sabbatical, layoff, or the year you go back to school.
- A year with a large deductible loss.
- Early retirement funded from cash or a taxable account.

**Worked example:** Elena retires at 60 with $1.2 million in a traditional 401(k) and $300,000 in taxable savings. She lives on the taxable money and converts $100,800 a year (the top of the 12% bracket for a married couple, plus the $32,200 standard deduction = about $133,000 of room). Each year she pays about $11,600 of tax on $133,000 of conversions — an 8.7% average rate — instead of being pushed into the 22–24% bracket by RMDs at 75.

## The Roth conversion ladder (early retirees)

Roth *contributions* can be withdrawn anytime tax- and penalty-free. Each *conversion* becomes withdrawable penalty-free five tax years after the conversion. So an early retiree can convert one year's living expenses each year; five years later that money is available before 59½ with no penalty. Bridge the first five years with taxable savings, Roth contributions, or a 72(t) SEPP plan. The Mad Fientist popularized this as the "Roth conversion ladder." Two five-year clocks exist: one for the Roth IRA itself (starts January 1 of the year of your first-ever Roth contribution) and one per conversion — open a Roth early, even with $1, to start the first clock.

## Required minimum distributions (SECURE 2.0)

Traditional IRAs and 401(k)s eventually force you to withdraw. Under SECURE 2.0:

- **Born 1951–1959: RMDs begin at age 73.**
- **Born 1960 or later: RMDs begin at age 75.**
- Roth IRAs *and* Roth 401(k)s (since 2024) have no lifetime RMDs.
- Missed-RMD penalty: 25% of the shortfall, reduced to 10% if corrected within two years.
- The first year's RMD on a $1.5 million balance at 73 is about $56,600 (balance ÷ 26.5 from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table). At 75, roughly $61,000 (÷ 24.6). That's taxable income whether you need it or not, and it can push Social Security into taxability and raise Medicare premiums (IRMAA surcharges, based on income from two years earlier).

Conversions before RMD age shrink the future RMD problem. Once you're in RMD years, you must take the RMD *first*; you can then convert additional amounts, but the RMD itself cannot be converted.

## State tax considerations

- Nine states have no wage income tax (AK, FL, NV, NH, SD, TN, TX, WA, WY). Contributing traditional in a high-tax state and withdrawing in one of these is a double win.
- Several states exempt some or all retirement income (Illinois, Mississippi, Pennsylvania, plus partial exclusions in many others), which makes traditional even better if you'll retire there.
- Some states tax conversions but never taxed the original contribution deduction (or vice versa); check both states if you move.

## How to actually do it

1. Find your marginal federal + state rate this year (tax software shows it; or look up the bracket your last dollar falls in).
2. If it's 22% or higher, default to traditional 401(k) and, if income allows, either a traditional IRA (if deductible) or a backdoor Roth IRA (a Roth IRA is still worth having for diversification and no-RMD status).
3. If it's 12% or lower, go Roth everywhere.
4. Open a Roth IRA now, even with a small amount, to start the five-year clock.
5. Each December, estimate the year's taxable income. If there's room below the top of your target bracket (12% or 22%, typically), convert that amount from traditional to Roth. Do it before December 31; conversions can't be undone since 2018.
6. Pay conversion tax from cash outside the IRA, not by withholding from the conversion (withholding is a taxable, possibly penalized, withdrawal if under 59½).
7. Watch IRMAA thresholds if you're 63 or older (Medicare premiums two years later depend on this year's income) and ACA subsidy cliffs if you buy marketplace insurance.
8. Model RMDs at 73/75 with a free calculator; if projected RMDs would push you into a higher bracket than you're in today, convert more aggressively.

## Key takeaways

- The Roth-vs-traditional question is purely "tax rate now vs tax rate at withdrawal"; growth rate doesn't matter.
- Contributions are deducted at your marginal rate; withdrawals fill brackets from zero upward, which favors traditional for high earners.
- Roth wins in the 10–12% bracket, for people with big future income, and as a hedge.
- Conversions in low-income years (retirement gap years, sabbaticals) are the highest-value tax move most people ever make.
- RMDs start at 73 (born 1951–59) or 75 (born 1960+); Roth accounts have none.
- The Roth conversion ladder gives early retirees penalty-free access after five years.
- Consider state taxes now and at withdrawal; relocating can flip the answer.
- Hold both buckets so you can pick the tax treatment of each retirement dollar.
