# Minimizing Fees and Costs

**In one sentence:** Fees are the one input to your returns you control with certainty, and because they compound against you, a "small" 1% a year can consume a quarter of your final wealth — so measure every cost in dollars over decades, not percentages per year.

*Last checked September 2026.*

## Why 1% is not small

An expense ratio is the annual fee a fund charges, expressed as a percentage of assets. It never shows up on a statement as a line item; it's silently deducted from the fund's returns. That invisibility is why people tolerate it.

**Worked example: $100,000 lump sum, 7% gross return, 30 years**

| Expense ratio | Ending value | Lost to fees |
|---|---|---|
| 0.03% (e.g., VTI, ITOT, SCHB) | $754,849 | — |
| 0.50% | $661,437 | $93,412 |
| 1.00% | $574,349 | $180,500 |

**Same, plus $1,000/month added along the way (starting balance $10,000):**

| Expense ratio | Ending value |
|---|---|
| 0.03% | $1,202,914 |
| 1.00% | $1,006,133 |

The 1% fund costs this saver almost **$200,000** — about 16% of the final balance — without ever sending a bill. John Bogle called this "the tyranny of compounding costs." The math is simple: your return compounds at (7% − fee), and each year's fee is charged on a balance that includes all prior growth.

## Advisor fees: AUM vs flat-fee

The "assets under management" (AUM) model charges roughly 1% of your portfolio per year, every year, regardless of how much work is done. On $500,000 that's $5,000 a year; on $2 million it's $20,000 a year for the same rebalancing spreadsheet.

**Worked example:** $500,000 at 7% for 25 years grows to **$2,713,716**. With a 1% AUM fee dragging returns to 6%, it grows to **$2,145,935**. The advisor collected roughly $250,000 in fees, but the *opportunity cost* — fees plus the growth those fees would have earned — is **$567,781**. An advisor must add well over 1% a year of value (through behavior coaching, tax planning, or estate work) just to break even.

Alternatives:
- **Flat-fee or hourly advisors** (typically $200–$400/hour, or $2,000–$8,000 a year for ongoing planning) do the same work for a fixed price. The Garrett Planning Network, NAPFA, and XY Planning Network list fee-only advisors; "fee-only" means no commissions, unlike "fee-based," which can mean both.
- **Robo-advisors** (Betterment, Wealthfront, Schwab Intelligent Portfolios, Vanguard Digital Advisor) charge 0–0.25%.
- **DIY** with a three-fund portfolio costs about 0.05% and takes an hour a year.

If you do pay an AUM advisor, negotiate; fees are far more flexible than advertised, and breakpoints (lower rates above $1M or $2M) are standard.

## 401(k) fees: read the 404(a)(5) disclosure

Your plan must give you an annual fee disclosure (named after the regulation, 404(a)(5)). It lists each fund's expense ratio plus any administrative or recordkeeping fees charged to your account. Things to check:

- **Fund expense ratios.** Good plans offer index funds at 0.02–0.10%. Bad plans offer only active funds at 0.75–1.5%, sometimes with a "wrap" fee on top. If yours is bad, contribute up to the match, then fund IRA/HSA, then come back to the 401(k) — the tax deduction still beats a taxable account for most people, and you'll roll it to a cheap IRA when you leave.
- **Administrative fees** of $50–$150 a year, or 0.25–0.5% of assets, are common at small employers. Per-head flat fees are fine; asset-based ones get expensive as your balance grows.
- **Brokerage window.** Some plans let you buy any ETF through a self-directed brokerage option; worth using if the core menu is poor.
- **Advocacy.** Employers have a fiduciary duty to keep fees reasonable. A polite email to HR citing the disclosure and the availability of cheaper index share classes has improved many plans. Free tools like FINRA's Fund Analyzer show a fund's cost over time.

## Trading costs you don't see

Commissions are mostly gone, but three costs remain:

1. **Bid-ask spread.** The gap between the price buyers will pay and sellers will accept. Broad ETFs like VTI or SPY trade at spreads of a penny or two (0.01%); thinly traded ETFs or small stocks can have spreads of 0.5–1% or more. Use **limit orders** and avoid trading in the first and last 15 minutes of the day, when spreads widen.
2. **Payment for order flow (PFOF).** Most brokers (Schwab, Robinhood, E*Trade, Webull) sell your orders to market makers who pay for the privilege and profit from the spread. The SEC requires "best execution," and the cost to a buy-and-hold investor is usually a fraction of a cent per share — but it's not zero. **Fidelity** does not accept PFOF for stock and ETF orders and generally shows better price improvement in its execution reports. Public.com also declined PFOF. For an investor making a few trades a year, this is a rounding error; for active traders, it adds up.
3. **Mutual fund transaction fees.** Buying another company's mutual fund at a brokerage can cost $50–$75 per trade (e.g., Vanguard funds at Fidelity or Schwab). Use the ETF version or the brokerage's own funds instead.

## Securities lending: small income, small risks

Brokers lend out shares to short sellers and, in "fully paid lending" programs, share the income with you. Fidelity, Schwab, and IBKR split roughly 50/50; Robinhood's split is less favorable. Income is meaningful only for hard-to-borrow stocks (small caps, meme stocks); for broad ETFs it's a few dollars a year. Downsides: dividends on lent shares become "cash in lieu," taxed as ordinary income instead of qualified; SIPC insurance doesn't cover lent shares (you hold collateral instead); and you lose voting rights. Reasonable to enable for large positions in obscure stocks; not worth thinking about for an index portfolio. Note that index *funds* already lend securities internally and pass most of the income back to the fund, which is one reason some index funds slightly beat their benchmark.

## Load funds and annuity traps

- **Loads** are sales commissions: a 5.75% front-end load on a Class A mutual fund means $5,750 of your $100,000 vanishes on day one. Class C shares have no upfront load but charge ~1% extra every year. There is no reason to own a load fund in 2026; equivalent no-load index funds exist for every category.
- **12b-1 fees** (0.25–1%) are marketing fees baked into the expense ratio. Avoid.
- **Variable annuities** stack mortality-and-expense charges (~1.25%), fund fees (~1%), and rider fees (~1%) into a product costing 2.5–3.5% a year, with surrender penalties of 7–10% for the first 7–10 years. They're sold, not bought, because commissions run 5–8%. **Indexed annuities** cap your upside (often 3–8% a year) while the insurer keeps the rest. The narrow case for annuities is a low-cost *single-premium immediate annuity* (SPIA) bought in retirement to convert a lump sum into guaranteed lifetime income — a different product entirely.
- **Whole life / "infinite banking" as an investment.** Commissions consume most of the first year's premium; internal returns rarely exceed 3–4%. Buy term insurance and invest the difference.

## How to actually do it

1. Pull up every fund you own and write down its expense ratio. Target: under 0.10% for index funds, under 0.20% blended.
2. Replace anything above 0.30% with an index equivalent — immediately in tax-advantaged accounts, and with new money (or after checking gains) in taxable.
3. Find your 401(k)'s 404(a)(5) fee disclosure. If the cheapest stock index option is above 0.25%, use the brokerage window if available, and email HR asking for lower-cost share classes.
4. If you pay an advisor on AUM, ask for a written list of services and compare the annual dollar amount with a flat-fee planner's quote. Negotiate or switch.
5. Use limit orders for every ETF trade; never market-order in the first or last 15 minutes.
6. Never buy anything with a load, a surrender charge, or a commission-paid salesperson. If a "financial advisor" works for an insurance company, they are a salesperson.
7. Run your numbers through the FINRA Fund Analyzer or a simple spreadsheet: (return − fee) compounded over your horizon. Seeing the dollars is what makes people act.

## Key takeaways

- Fees compound against you; 1% a year costs roughly 16–25% of your final wealth over 30 years.
- Broad index ETFs cost 0.03%; there's no category where paying 1% is necessary.
- A 1% AUM advisor on $500,000 costs about $570,000 in lost wealth over 25 years; flat-fee planners do the same work for a fixed price.
- Read your 401(k)'s 404(a)(5) disclosure; if the menu is expensive, prioritize IRA/HSA after the match and lobby HR.
- Spreads and PFOF are small for buy-and-hold investors; limit orders and Fidelity's no-PFOF routing minimize them.
- Securities lending income is trivial for index investors and converts qualified dividends to ordinary income.
- Load funds, 12b-1 fees, variable/indexed annuities, and whole life as an investment are commission products to avoid.
