# Backdoor Roth and Mega Backdoor Roth

**In one sentence:** Income limits stop high earners from contributing to a Roth IRA directly, but nothing stops them from contributing to a traditional IRA and converting it (the backdoor), or from stuffing after-tax dollars into a 401(k) and converting those (the mega backdoor) — as long as they avoid the pro-rata trap and file Form 8606.

*2026 figures from IRS Notice 2025-67. Last checked September 2026.*

## Why the backdoor exists

A Roth IRA takes after-tax money and lets it grow and come out tax-free forever, with no required minimum distributions (RMDs) during your life. Congress limits *direct* Roth contributions by income: in 2026 the ability to contribute phases out between **$153,000 and $168,000** of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and **$242,000 to $252,000** for married filing jointly. But since 2010 there has been no income limit on *converting* a traditional IRA to a Roth. So: contribute to a traditional IRA (no income limit on contributing, only on deducting), then convert. The IRS has explicitly blessed this; it's not a gray area.

## Backdoor Roth, step by step

1. **Contribute** up to $7,500 (or $8,600 if 50+) to a traditional IRA as a *nondeductible* contribution. Don't claim a deduction.
2. **Wait** a short time for the cash to settle (a few days is customary; there is no legally required waiting period).
3. **Convert** the entire balance to a Roth IRA. Most brokerages have a "convert to Roth" button.
4. **File Form 8606** with your tax return. Part I records the nondeductible contribution (your "basis"); Part II reports the conversion. Because your basis equals the amount converted, the taxable amount is roughly zero — maybe a few dollars of interest.
5. **Repeat every January.**

**Worked example:** Priya earns $200,000, single. She puts $7,500 into a traditional IRA on January 5, converts it on January 12 when it's worth $7,503. Form 8606 shows basis $7,500, conversion $7,503, taxable income $3. She now has $7,500 in a Roth that will never be taxed again.

## The pro-rata rule: the trap that ruins the backdoor

The IRS treats *all* your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs as one pot. When you convert, the tax-free portion is your basis divided by the total of all those IRA balances on December 31 of the conversion year. You cannot choose to convert "just the after-tax part."

**Worked example of the trap:** Marcus has a $92,500 rollover IRA from an old job (all pre-tax). He adds $7,500 nondeductible and converts $7,500. His basis is $7,500 out of $100,000 total, so only 7.5% of the conversion is tax-free. Of the $7,500 converted, $6,937.50 is taxable at his 35% rate — a surprise $2,428 bill, and the remaining basis stays trapped in the IRA to be tracked for years.

**Fixes:**
- **Roll the pre-tax IRA into your current 401(k)** before December 31 (a "reverse rollover"). 401(k) balances don't count in the pro-rata calculation. Most large plans accept incoming rollovers; ask HR.
- Self-employed? Open a solo 401(k) and roll the IRA into that.
- Convert the whole IRA and pay the tax (sensible only in a low-income year).
- Note: the rule is per person, not per couple. A spouse's IRAs don't affect yours.

## Other backdoor pitfalls

- **The step-transaction worry is dead.** Congress's 2018 conference report acknowledged the backdoor as legitimate. Waiting months between steps is unnecessary.
- **Excess contributions.** Contributing directly to a Roth, then discovering you're over the income limit, triggers a 6% annual penalty until fixed. Fix by "recharacterizing" the contribution to traditional, then converting.
- **Forgetting Form 8606** means the IRS assumes you had no basis and taxes the whole conversion. Missing forms can be filed late (there's a $50 penalty, usually waived).
- **Five-year clock.** Each conversion has its own five-year clock for penalty-free withdrawal of the converted amount before age 59½. Not an issue if you're leaving it there.

## Mega backdoor Roth

The regular backdoor moves $7,500 a year. The mega backdoor can move $30,000–$47,500 a year, because it uses the 401(k)'s *total* contribution limit instead of the employee deferral limit.

**2026 numbers:**
- Employee deferral (pre-tax or Roth): **$24,500**
- Total annual additions from all sources (section 415(c)): **$72,000** (plus catch-ups on top)
- The gap — $72,000 minus your deferrals minus employer contributions — can be filled with **after-tax** contributions *if your plan allows it*.

**Worked example:** Dana, 40, earns $180,000. She defers $24,500 pre-tax; her employer contributes $9,000 (5% match). Room remaining: $72,000 − $24,500 − $9,000 = **$38,500** of after-tax contributions. She converts that to Roth within days, so almost no earnings accrue and almost no tax is due. Over ten years that's $385,000 of Roth principal on top of her regular contributions.

### What the plan must offer

1. **After-tax (non-Roth) contributions.** Not the same as Roth 401(k) contributions. Roughly a third of large plans allow this; small-company plans rarely do, because after-tax contributions are subject to nondiscrimination testing (the ACP test) and if only highly paid people use them, the plan fails and refunds money.
2. **A way out:** either **in-plan Roth conversion** (after-tax → Roth 401(k), now often automatic and daily at large employers) or **in-service distributions** of the after-tax subaccount (roll to a Roth IRA while still employed).

If your plan has both, the process is usually a checkbox in the payroll portal.

### Mega backdoor mechanics and taxes

- Only *earnings* on after-tax money are taxable at conversion, so convert quickly.
- If you roll out to an IRA rather than converting in-plan, IRS Notice 2014-54 lets you send the after-tax principal to a Roth IRA and the pre-tax earnings to a traditional IRA in one transaction — no tax at all.
- After-tax 401(k) money does **not** count toward the pro-rata rule for IRA conversions (it's in a 401(k), not an IRA).
- You'll get a Form 1099-R for the conversion; your tax software handles it.
- Solo 401(k)s can be written to allow the mega backdoor; some off-the-shelf providers (e.g., Fidelity's basic solo plan) don't, but custom plan documents do.

### Ordering: which comes first?

Only after the match, HSA, IRA, and full $24,500 deferral. After-tax contributions have no upfront deduction, so they're worse than pre-tax dollars — but far better than a taxable account, because the growth is tax-free forever.

## How to actually do it

**Backdoor Roth (every year):**
1. Confirm your total pre-tax IRA/SEP/SIMPLE balances are zero (or roll them into a 401(k) before year-end).
2. Contribute $7,500 to a traditional IRA, marked nondeductible.
3. Convert to Roth after settlement.
4. Enter both steps in your tax software so Form 8606 is generated; verify line 18 (taxable amount) is near zero.
5. Invest the Roth money.

**Mega backdoor Roth:**
1. Read your plan's Summary Plan Description or call the recordkeeper. Ask: "Does the plan permit after-tax contributions? Does it permit in-plan Roth conversions or in-service withdrawals of after-tax money?"
2. Calculate room: $72,000 − expected deferrals − expected employer contributions (− catch-up if applicable).
3. Set the after-tax percentage in payroll. Watch that it doesn't cause your employer match to be capped if your deferrals hit $24,500 early in the year (ask about "true-up" provisions).
4. Turn on automatic in-plan conversion, or set a monthly reminder to convert manually.
5. Reconcile with the 1099-R in February.

## Key takeaways

- There's no income limit on Roth *conversions*, which makes the backdoor legal and routine.
- The pro-rata rule counts every traditional/SEP/SIMPLE IRA you own on December 31; clear them into a 401(k) first.
- File Form 8606 every year or the IRS will tax you twice.
- 2026: IRA $7,500 ($8,600 at 50+); Roth phase-out $153k–$168k single, $242k–$252k joint.
- Mega backdoor room = $72,000 − deferrals − employer contributions, only if the plan allows after-tax contributions and a conversion path.
- Convert after-tax money quickly to keep taxable earnings near zero.
- Do the mega backdoor only after the regular waterfall steps are complete.
