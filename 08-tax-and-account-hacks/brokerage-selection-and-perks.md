# Brokerage Selection and Perks

**In one sentence:** Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard are all fine homes for a long-term index portfolio, but they differ sharply on what your idle cash earns, what margin costs, and what they'll pay you to move — and those differences are worth real money if you know where to look.

*Rates and promotions below were gathered from public sources dated February–July 2026 and change constantly; verify before acting. Last checked September 2026.*

## What actually matters (and what doesn't)

Commissions on stocks and ETFs are $0 everywhere now, so stop comparing them. The things that differ:

1. **Cash sweep yield** — what uninvested cash earns by default. The biggest hidden difference.
2. **Fractional shares** — can you invest $73.12, or must you buy whole shares?
3. **Margin rates** — irrelevant for most people; huge if you ever borrow.
4. **Fund access and fees** — is their in-house index lineup cheap? Do they charge to buy competitors' mutual funds?
5. **Transfer bonuses** — brokers pay 1–4% of assets to lure accounts.
6. **Service and tooling** — 401(k) rollovers, backdoor Roth flow, HSA, cost-basis tools, customer support that picks up the phone.
7. **Payment for order flow** — see [minimizing-fees-and-costs.md](minimizing-fees-and-costs.md); minor for buy-and-hold.

## The big three for long-term investors

| Non-house mutual fund fee | $49.95 (e.g., buying Vanguard funds) | $49.95–$74.95 | $0–$20 |

| HSA | Yes, $0 fee, best in class | No | No |
| Outgoing full transfer fee | $0 | $50 (waived often) | Vanguard added a $100 closure/transfer fee in 2024 for households under $5M; some sources now report $0 — confirm |
| Payment for order flow (equities) | No | Yes | No |
| Auto-invest into ETFs | Yes (recurring, fractional) | Limited to Schwab ETFs / mutual funds | Yes for Vanguard ETFs |

**Bottom line:** Fidelity is the community default in 2026 — best cash yield behavior, full fractional ETFs, no PFOF, the best HSA, and strong service. Schwab has excellent branches and service but you *must* manually move cash into a money market fund or you'll earn almost nothing. Vanguard has the best default sweep yield and the cheapest funds but the weakest app and support, and it has been adding nuisance fees.

**Worked example of the cash-sweep gap:** Someone keeps an average $30,000 uninvested (dividends, cash cushion, dry powder). At Schwab's 0.4% default that's $120 a year; at Vanguard's 3.6% it's $1,080. That $960 difference per year is more than most people's total expense-ratio bill.

IBKR's margin rates are the lowest in the industry by a wide margin — roughly 5% versus 9–10% at Fidelity or Schwab. If you ever plan to borrow against a portfolio (bridge loan for a house, tax-bill float), keep a mirror account there. Robinhood is best used *tactically*: collect a transfer bonus or IRA match, hold for the required period, move on.

## Transfer (ACAT) bonuses — free money for moving

An **ACAT** is the industry standard for moving assets between brokers in kind (your shares move; nothing is sold; no tax). Brokers pay bonuses to receive them:

- **Robinhood** ran a 2% (Gold) / 1% (non-Gold) bonus on taxable and IRA transfers in Feb–Mar 2026, 3% with a $10k+ margin balance; **five-year holding requirement** or the bonus is clawed back; Gold required for a year. Also reimburses up to $75 of the outgoing broker's fee on transfers over $7,500.
- **Webull** has run 3–4% bonuses with 2–5 year holds.
- **Public** has offered tiered bonuses on transfers in (check current terms; typically 1% with holding periods).
- **Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard** rarely pay percentage bonuses; Schwab occasionally offers $100–$1,000 tiered new-account bonuses; Fidelity sometimes $100 for $50 deposits. Merrill Edge, E*Trade, and Wells Fargo have paid $500–$3,000+ tiered bonuses on large transfers.

**Worked example:** Moving a $200,000 IRA to Robinhood for a 2% bonus yields $4,000, minus Gold at $50/year for the required year. Held five years in the same VTI shares you already owned, the cost is essentially nothing but inconvenience. Bonuses into an IRA are *not* contributions and don't count against your limit; bonuses into a taxable account are reported as miscellaneous income on a 1099. The Bogleheads forum maintains a long-running thread tracking every live offer.

Before transferring: make sure the receiving broker supports every holding (proprietary mutual funds like FZROX can't move; sell them in an IRA first), turn off dividend reinvestment during the move, and download your cost-basis records in case they arrive garbled.

## 401(k) rollover mechanics

When you leave a job you can (a) leave the 401(k), (b) roll to the new employer's plan, (c) roll to an IRA, or (d) cash out (don't — see [common-costly-mistakes.md](common-costly-mistakes.md)).

- **Direct rollover** ("trustee-to-trustee"): the old plan sends a check payable to "Fidelity FBO [your name]" or wires directly. No tax withheld, no 60-day clock. Always do this.
- **Indirect rollover**: the check is made out to you. The plan must withhold 20%, you have 60 days to deposit the full amount (replacing the withheld 20% from your own pocket) into an IRA, and you get the withholding back at tax time. Only one indirect IRA-to-IRA rollover per 12 months. Avoid.
- **Roth 401(k) → Roth IRA; pre-tax 401(k) → traditional IRA.** Mixing them up creates a taxable conversion.
- **Consider *not* rolling to an IRA** if you (1) plan to do backdoor Roths (a pre-tax IRA triggers the pro-rata rule — roll into the new 401(k) instead), (2) separated from service at 55–59½ (401(k) withdrawals are penalty-free at 55 under the "Rule of 55"; IRAs make you wait to 59½), or (3) value the stronger federal creditor protection of a 401(k) (IRAs get state-law protection, which varies).
- **Employer stock** in a 401(k) may qualify for Net Unrealized Appreciation treatment — distributing the shares to a taxable account and paying ordinary tax only on the original cost; ask a CPA before rolling it into an IRA, which forfeits the option.
- Most receiving brokers have a rollover concierge; Fidelity and Schwab will call the old plan with you on the line.

## How to actually do it

1. Default choice for a new investor: open a Fidelity brokerage, Roth IRA, and (if eligible) HSA. Buy ITOT/VTI or FZROX in the IRA.
2. Already at Schwab? Log in and buy SWVXX (or SNSXX for state-tax-free Treasuries) with idle cash; set a reminder to sweep monthly.
3. Already at Vanguard? Stay if you're happy; turn on e-delivery to waive the annual fee; note the possible outgoing transfer fee.
4. Set up automatic weekly or monthly ETF purchases with fractional shares so no cash sits idle.
5. Once a year, check the Bogleheads transfer-bonus thread. If a bonus exceeds ~1% and you're comfortable with the hold period, ACAT a taxable or IRA account to collect it.
6. On a job change, initiate a direct rollover from the receiving broker's website; choose the new 401(k) if you're doing backdoor Roths, otherwise an IRA at your main broker.
7. Keep one account at IBKR if you'll ever want cheap margin or international stocks.

## Key takeaways

- Commissions are zero everywhere; compare cash yields, fractional shares, fund fees, and bonuses instead.
- Schwab's default sweep pays ~0.4% (July 2026) — manually buy a money market fund or you're leaving ~3% on the table.
- Fidelity: no PFOF, fractional ETFs, $0 HSA, ~3.3% default sweep; the sensible default.
- IBKR margin ~5% vs ~10% at Fidelity/Schwab (March 2026).
- Robinhood/Webull transfer bonuses of 1–4% are real money but carry 2–5 year holds and Gold subscriptions; read the clawback terms.
- Always roll a 401(k) by direct rollover; consider rolling into the *new* 401(k) if you do backdoor Roths.
- Don't move proprietary zero-fee mutual funds in kind; they can't transfer.
- Rates and promos in this file are dated; re-verify before acting.
